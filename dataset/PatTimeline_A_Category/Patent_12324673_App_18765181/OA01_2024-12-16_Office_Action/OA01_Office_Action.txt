Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 112
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 112(b):
(b)  CONCLUSION.—The specification shall conclude with one or more claims particularly pointing out and distinctly claiming the subject matter which the inventor or a joint inventor regards as the invention.
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 112 (pre-AIA ), second paragraph:
The specification shall conclude with one or more claims particularly pointing out and distinctly claiming the subject matter which the applicant regards as his invention.
Claim 1-10 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 112(b) or 35 U.S.C. 112 (pre-AIA ), second paragraph, as being indefinite for failing to particularly point out and distinctly claim the subject matter which the inventor or a joint inventor (or for applications subject to pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 112, the applicant), regards as the invention.
Regarding claim 1, the preamble states “a multifunctional electric wave generator for simulation of brain wave,”, but it is unclear if applicant intends to use “simulation” or the “stimulation”. The specification (paragraphs [0004], [0069] and [0070]) and claims read as though the word is intended to be “stimulation”, or as defined to function as a physiological stimulus. The word “simulation” is defined as the imitative representation of the functioning of one system or process by means of the functioning of another. While “simulation” is plausible the claimed device does not seem to be intended to simulate brain wave due to the complexity of doing so. Examiner is interpreting the claims in view of the interpretation of “a multifunctional electric wave generator for stimulation of brain wave,” for the purpose of examination. Examiner is requesting clarification as to the intended word.
Claim 1 recites the limitation “an electric wave generator body placed on the support plate, consisting of” but the use of “consisting of” excludes any element not specified in the claim, per MPEP 2111.03. The device claimed is lacking elements to allow the generator to function, such as, but not limited to, power input. The claim does have a power amplifier, but does not disclose any power to the processor, which is required. It also does not include the AI monitoring system disclosed in paragraph [0079].  As the use “consisting of” would require the listing of all elements to make the device function and therefore renders the claim unclear. Examiner is interpreting “consisting of” as “comprising” for examination purposes, unless further clarified by applicant.

Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 103
In the event the determination of the status of the application as subject to AIA  35 U.S.C. 102 and 103 (or as subject to pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 102 and 103) is incorrect, any correction of the statutory basis (i.e., changing from AIA  to pre-AIA ) for the rejection will not be considered a new ground of rejection if the prior art relied upon, and the rationale supporting the rejection, would be the same under either status.
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 103 which forms the basis for all obviousness rejections set forth in this Office action:
A patent for a claimed invention may not be obtained, notwithstanding that the claimed invention is not identically disclosed as set forth in section 102, if the differences between the claimed invention and the prior art are such that the claimed invention as a whole would have been obvious before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which the claimed invention pertains. Patentability shall not be negated by the manner in which the invention was made.
The factual inquiries for establishing a background for determining obviousness under 35 U.S.C. 103 are summarized as follows:
1. Determining the scope and contents of the prior art.
2. Ascertaining the differences between the prior art and the claims at issue.
3. Resolving the level of ordinary skill in the pertinent art.
4. Considering objective evidence present in the application indicating obviousness or nonobviousness.
Claim 1 is rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Ives (US4806881A) in view of Sanus (“CFR2115, CFR2127, CFR2136, CFR2144 Instruction Manual”, 2014) and Al-Samman (“Comparative Study of Indoor Propagation Model Below and Above 6 GHz for 5G Wireless Networks”, 2019).
Regarding claim 1, under broadest reasonable interpretation, examiner is interpreting claim 1 as a common signal generator stored in a common electronics rack. Ives discloses a multifunctional electric wave generator for simulation of brain wave (Abstract, “for simulation of brain wave” is intended use, so any multifunctional electric wave generator would suffice.) comprising an electric wave generator body (Figure 1), consisting of a signal receiver (Col 2, lines 9-14), a frequency controller (Col 3, lines 64-65), a power amplifier (Col 5, lines 15-16), a waveform converter (Col 2, lines 16-18), an output device (Figure 1, “Output channel”), and a digital signal processor (Figure 2, “70”); the signal receiver, frequency controller, power amplifier, waveform converter, output device, and the digital signal processor are interconnected electrically (Figures 1 and 2).
Ives does not disclose the common electronics rack. Sanus discloses a base (See annotated figure below); a protective box fixedly mounted on the top of the base (See annotated figure below); a support plate located inside the protective box (See annotated figure below); and a storage mechanism disposed on the protective box for storing the electric wave generator body (See annotated figure below. The door is considered the storage mechanism. “for storing the electric wave generator body” is considered intended use and as the rack can store an wave generator it meets the limitation).

Neither Ives nor Sanus teach the electric wave generator body consisting of a housing, wherein the signal receiver, frequency controller, power amplifier, waveform converter, output device, and the
digital signal processor are all assembled inside the housing and the housing is placed on the support plate. Al-Samman teaches in figure 1 that the function generator has a housing with the function generator electronics in the housing and the housing is placed on the support plate of an electronics storage racks as part of a standard measurement equipment setup. It would have been obvious before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to one having ordinary skill in the art to modify the multifunctional electric wave generator as taught by Ives, with putting the multifunctional electric wave generator in a housing, with the housing stored in the common electronics rack on a support shelf disclosed by Sanus as taught by Al-Samman, since such a modification would allow the electronics to be stored in a damage safe space in manner that allows the electronics to be easier moved (wheels).
Claim 10 is rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Ives (US4806881A), Sanus (“CFR2115, CFR2127, CFR2136, CFR2144 Instruction Manual”, 2014), and Al-Samman (“Comparative Study of Indoor Propagation Model Below and Above 6 GHz for 5G Wireless Networks”, 2019), as applied to claim 1, in view of Leger (“How to Use a Function Generator”, 2013).
Regarding claim 10, the multifunctional electric wave generator of claim 1 was previously disclosed by Ives, Sanus and Al-Samman. Ives, Sanus and Al-Samman do not explicitly disclose a control method. Leger discloses a control method (“How to Use a Function Generator” Title) of a multifunctional electric wave generator (“Function Generator) for simulation of brain wave (“for simulation of brain wave” is intended use, so any multifunctional electric wave generator would suffice) according to claim 1, comprising the following steps: Step one: Ensuring the power supply is stable (“Power on the generator” in order to power it on, a user would have to make sure it’s plugged in, thereby ensuring the power supply is stable), connecting the power and ground lines correctly (“This entails connecting the ground and signal leads to your oscilloscope to check the controls.”), activating the electric wave generator and allowing it to warm up to improve the frequency stability (“You use a function generator by powering it on”); Step two: Selecting or setting a modulation mode as needed (“Then you adjust the function generator until you get the appropriate signal”), which includes amplitude modulation (“amplitude control”), frequency modulation (“frequency control”), choosing appropriate filters to filter out clutter or noise from the output signal, then setting or adjusting various parameters, such as waveform type (“pick the shape of the output”, frequency, amplitude (“amplitude control”), through a microprocessor to ensure stable DC power supply to the internal circuit of the electric wave generator body (“Its direct current (DC) offset control changes the signal’s average voltage with regard to the ground”); Step three: Selecting the desired waveform type through a waveform converter (“pick the shape of the output”, which includes sine wave, square wave, and triangle wave (Paragraph 3 and 4), setting the desired range of signal output frequency range by a frequency controller (Paragraph 6), adjusting a fine-tuning knob or a keyboard to input the precise frequency (Paragraph 6), adjusting the output power of the power amplifier as needed to ensure sufficient signal strength (Paragraph 5), and monitoring the waveform, frequency, amplitude, and other parameters of the output signal in real-time through a display device (“Then you adjust the function generator until you get the appropriate signal and attach the function generator’s ground and signal leads to the device under test’s input and ground terminals.” A user would have to monitor the parameters in order to adjust the generator), utilizing a digital signal processor to further process and optimize the output signal (“Then you adjust the function generator until you get the appropriate signal and attach the function generator’s ground and signal leads to the device under test’s input and ground terminals.” As disclosed by Ives, the function generator contains a digital signal processor, so adjusting the parameters would be utilizing the processor and a user would not adjust a signal to be less optimal.) As Leger is describing commonly known steps for using a wave generator it would have been obvious before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to one having ordinary skill in the art to modify the multifunctional electric wave generator as taught by Ives, Sanus and Al-Samman, with steps of using a wave generator as taught by Leger, since such a modification would be using the wave generator in manner that is commonly known in the art and within the normal functions of the wave generator.
