Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 112(b)
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 112(b):
(b)  CONCLUSION.—The specification shall conclude with one or more claims particularly pointing out and distinctly claiming the subject matter which the inventor or a joint inventor regards as the invention.
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 112 (pre-AIA ), second paragraph:
The specification shall conclude with one or more claims particularly pointing out and distinctly claiming the subject matter which the applicant regards as his invention.
Claims 1-8 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 112(b) or 35 U.S.C. 112 (pre-AIA ), second paragraph, as being indefinite for failing to particularly point out and distinctly claim the subject matter which the inventor or a joint inventor (or for applications subject to pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 112, the applicant), regards as the invention.
In particular, Claim 1 recites “(MEG,MCG)”; however, such an expression is indefinite because it is unclear whether the biomagnetic imaging inclusive of both MEG and MCG or whether MEG and MCG are alternatives to one another. For the purposes of compact prosecution, the limitation will be interpreted as alternatives.
Claim 6 recite similar limitations and are rejected under the same rationale as claim 1. Dependent claims are rejected by virtue of their dependency to abovementioned claims.
With regards to Claim 6, the claim recites “simple matching”; however, such a limitation does not meet threshold requirement of clarity and precision as laid out in MPEP § 2173.02. The content of the instant application alone or in combination with the state of the art does not apprise one of ordinary skill in the art of the scope of “simple matching” because the language is so ambiguous, vague, and indefinite that it cannot provide clear warning to others as to what constitutes infringement of the patent. More specifically, it is unclear what differentiates simple matching from merely matching or even a complex matching. Furthermore, the instant specification fails to further define simple matching. Therefore, Claim 6 does not satisfy the notice function under 35 U.S.C. 112(b), thus, rendering the claim indefinite.
Allowable Subject Matter
Claims 1 & 6 would be allowable if rewritten or amended to overcome the rejection(s) under 35 U.S.C. 112(b) or 35 U.S.C. 112 (pre-AIA ), 2nd paragraph, set forth in this Office action.
The following is a statement of reasons for the indication of allowable subject matter:
Oshima et al. (US PGPUB 20210267678) teaches of biomagnetic imaging of with a plurality of magnetic sensors 11 in an array 10 arranged on the chest (i.e. MCG, see Oshima FIG. 1 & ¶ [0042]). Even though Oshima et al. does not teach of an external maker, Oshima et al. teaches of an internal maker 24 having a single coil to track a catheter 20 (see Oshima et al. ¶ [0089]) but does not teach of utilizing a plurality of coils.  Furhtermore Oshima et al. is silent to MR imaging and a corresponding MR compatible marker.
Misaka et al. (US PGPUB 20200100695) teaches of an imaging scanner 100 with combined MR imaging 7 and MEG unit 3. Misaka et al. also teaches of using marker coils M1-M3 detected by the magnetic sensors 1 of the MEG unit 3 in order to determine positional information for generating a transformation matrix between the MRI and MEG coordinate systems (see Misaka et al. ¶ [0045]). While Misaka et al. discloses that markers M1, M2, M3 have a coil for generating a magnetic field, Misaka et al. does not teach of said coils being 3-axis coils. Furthermore, while Misaka et al. teaches of seals S1-S3 for camera registration (see Misaka et al. ¶ [0049]), Misaka is silent to contrast markers imaged by MR imaging.
Kudo et al. (US PGPUB 20200305747) teaches of optical co-registration using marker coils, but not 3-axis marker coils.
Messaritaki et al. (“Assessment and elimination of the effects of head movement on MEG resting-state measures of oscillatory brain activity,” (1 October 2017), Neuroimage, 159:302–324) teaches of mitigation of head movement in MEG imaging (see Messaritaki et al. Abstract). Messaritaki et al. also teaches of MEG-MRI co-registration in which 3 fiducial markers with coils were relied upon in which the fiducial are imageable in both MRI & MEG (see Mesaritaki et al. § 2.2). However, the Messaritaki et al. fiducial coils are not characterized as 3-axis coils and there is no suggestion in Messaritaki et al. that the coils are 3-axis coils.
Similar, to Messaritaki et al., Pfeiffer et al. (see IDS of 13 June 2023) teaches of using co-registration of MRI and MEG data based on MR compatible fiducials and head position indicator (HPI) coils (see Pfeiffer et al. § 2.2 & 2.3.4). While the Pfeiffer et al. coils were arranged in sets of triplets, Pfeiffer et al. does not teach or suggest that the triplets are arranged at different coordinate axes. Pfeiffer et al. only teaches that the coils are energized at different times for accurate localization (see Pfeiffer et al. § 2.2). Accordingly, Pfeiffer et al. does not teach of 3-axis coils.
Hill et al. (“Multi-channel whole-head OPM-MEG: Helmet design and a comparison with a conventional system,” (1 October 2020), NeuroImage Volume 219) teaches OPM-MEG co-registration with MRI imaging using HPI coils which are not described as 3-axis coils (see Hill et al. § 2.4.2).
McGowin (“HEAD MOTION EVALUATION AND CORRECTION IN MAGNETOENCEPHALOGRAPHY,” (May 2015), PhD. Dissertation Physics, Wake Forest University,) teaches of MEG localization using fiducial localization coil (see McGowin FIG. 5), but McGowin does not teach of said coils being 3-axis coils.
In sum, the instant specification defines a 3-axis coil as “a coil adapted to emit magnetic fluxes along three mutually perpendicular axes.” The evaluated prior art, cited or otherwise, does not teach of a 3-axis coil, merely a single HPI coil.
