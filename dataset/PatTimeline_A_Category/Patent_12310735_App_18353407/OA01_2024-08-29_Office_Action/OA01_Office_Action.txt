Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 112
The following is a quotation of the first paragraph of 35 U.S.C. 112(a):
(a) IN GENERAL.—The specification shall contain a written description of the invention, and of the manner and process of making and using it, in such full, clear, concise, and exact terms as to enable any person skilled in the art to which it pertains, or with which it is most nearly connected, to make and use the same, and shall set forth the best mode contemplated by the inventor or joint inventor of carrying out the invention.
The following is a quotation of the first paragraph of pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 112:
The specification shall contain a written description of the invention, and of the manner and process of making and using it, in such full, clear, concise, and exact terms as to enable any person skilled in the art to which it pertains, or with which it is most nearly connected, to make and use the same, and shall set forth the best mode contemplated by the inventor of carrying out his invention.

Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 102
The following is a quotation of the appropriate paragraphs of 35 U.S.C. 102 that form the basis for the rejections under this section made in this Office action:
A person shall be entitled to a patent unless –
(a)(1) the claimed invention was patented, described in a printed publication, or in public use, on sale, or otherwise available to the public before the effective filing date of the claimed invention.
Claims 1, 3, 11, and 13 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 102(a)(1) as being anticipated by Baker et al. US Publication 2008/0139953 (hereinafter Baker).
Regarding claim 1, Baker discloses an electrocardiography monitor, comprising: a housing comprising rounded edges along top and bottom surfaces of the housing (elements 201 and 203 combined, see Figure 2, for the rounded top/bottom, see Figure 1B at the cross section where the top and bottom curve slightly); a processor secured by the housing (processor 512 as per Figure 5, within 202, which is housed in 201/203, see Figures 1A-2), an electrographic front end circuit to sense electrocardiographic signals (circuits 506-508, which work together as a traditional front end circuit, see Figure 5); a microcontroller interfaced to the electrocardiographic front end circuit to sample the electrocardiographic signals (516, as per [0076]); and electrical contacts (302) that protrude from the bottom surface of the housing (bottom surface 203 as per Figure 3) to connect to a battery to power the processor (some of the contacts 302 connect to the contacts that feed to the batteries as per Figure 4).
Regarding claims 3 and 13, Baker discloses further electrical contacts to establish an electrical connection with one or more electrodes for sensing the electrocardiographic signals (electrodes contacts 405 which can connect to the contacts shown in Figure 4A at 408-409, and ultimately to electrodes at 103).
Regarding claims 4 and 14, Baker details a housing (as per Figures 1A-2) and includes the contacts and battery as mentioned above. The shaping of the housing is one that is taller than the battery (as per Figure 1C, where over is being read as “at a higher level than”) or it can be read with orientation in mind (Figure 14 where the processor and housing in 102 is “over” the battery).
Regarding claim 11, see contents of rejected claim 1 above. Baker additionally discloses a backing configured to receive the sealed housing (backing 104; in the alternate, the backing can also be considered 105 depending on how the skilled artisan interprets “receive”).
Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 103
In the event the determination of the status of the application as subject to AIA  35 U.S.C. 102 and 103 (or as subject to pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 102 and 103) is incorrect, any correction of the statutory basis (i.e., changing from AIA  to pre-AIA ) for the rejection will not be considered a new ground of rejection if the prior art relied upon, and the rationale supporting the rejection, would be the same under either status.
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 103 which forms the basis for all obviousness rejections set forth in this Office action:
A patent for a claimed invention may not be obtained, notwithstanding that the claimed invention is not identically disclosed as set forth in section 102, if the differences between the claimed invention and the prior art are such that the claimed invention as a whole would have been obvious before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which the claimed invention pertains. Patentability shall not be negated by the manner in which the invention was made.
The factual inquiries for establishing a background for determining obviousness under 35 U.S.C. 103 are summarized as follows:
1. Determining the scope and contents of the prior art.
2. Ascertaining the differences between the prior art and the claims at issue.
3. Resolving the level of ordinary skill in the pertinent art.
4. Considering objective evidence present in the application indicating obviousness or non-obviousness.
This application currently names joint inventors. In considering patentability of the claims the examiner presumes that the subject matter of the various claims was commonly owned as of the effective filing date of the claimed invention(s) absent any evidence to the contrary.  Applicant is advised of the obligation under 37 CFR 1.56 to point out the inventor and effective filing dates of each claim that was not commonly owned as of the effective filing date of the later invention in order for the examiner to consider the applicability of 35 U.S.C. 102(b)(2)(C) for any potential 35 U.S.C. 102(a)(2) prior art against the later invention.
In the alternate, claims 1, 3-4, 8, 11, 13-14, and 18 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Baker in view of Wang et al. US Publication 2011/0160601 (hereinafter Wang).
Regarding claim 1, Baker discloses an electrocardiography monitor, comprising: a housing comprising rounded edges along top and bottom surfaces of the housing (elements 201 and 203 combined, see Figure 2, for the rounded top/bottom, see Figure 1B at the cross section where the top and bottom curve slightly); an electrographic front end circuit to sense electrocardiographic signals (circuits 506-508, which work together as a traditional front end circuit, see Figure 5); a microcontroller interfaced to the electrocardiographic front end circuit to sample the electrocardiographic signals (512 and/or 516, as per [0076]); and electrical contacts (302) that protrude from the bottom surface of the housing (bottom surface 203 as per Figure 3) to connect to a battery to power the processor (some of the contacts 302 connect to the contacts that feed to the batteries as per Figure 4).
Depending on how the processor and microcontroller are interpreted, see 112 rejection above, Baker can be silent on the processor. Wang teaches an ECG monitoring device that includes a processor and microcontroller (both are labeled as 18) where both are secured by a housing. Microcontroller is already detailed by Baker as being synonymous with processor ([0071]). Therefore, it would have been obvious to the skilled artisan before the effective filing date to utilize the processor as taught by Wang in combination to the microcontroller of Baker to split up the processing reducing the workload on a single processing unit).
Regarding claims 3 and 13, Baker discloses further electrical contacts to establish an electrical connection with one or more electrodes for sensing the electrocardiographic signals (electrodes contacts 405 which can connect to the contacts shown in Figure 4A at 408-409, and ultimately to electrodes at 103).
Regarding claims 4 and 14, Baker details a housing (as per Figures 1A-2) and includes the contacts and battery as mentioned above. The shaping of the housing is one that is taller than the battery (as per Figure 1C, where over is being read as “at a higher level than”) or it can be read with orientation in mind (Figure 14 where the processor and housing in 102 is “over” the battery).
Regarding claims 4 and 14, in the alternate, Baker details a housing (as per Figures 1A-2) and includes the contacts and battery as mentioned above, but is silent on the housing being shaped to go over the battery as well. Wang teaches a housing that is shaped to be placed over the battery (Figure 2B battery 11, housing, 19, and processor 18). It would have been obvious to the skilled artisan before the effective filing date to utilize the housing as taught by Wang with the device of Baker as predictable results would have ensued (powering the deice with an internal battery versus an external one.
Regarding claims 8 and 18, Baker is silent on the use of a flash memory. Wang teaches a flash memory configured to store the electrocardiographic signals ([0074][0075]). It would have been obvious to the skilled artisan before the effective filing date to utilize the flash memory as taught by Wang with the device of Baker in order to store the obtained ECG signals while also allowing transfer of the signals by removal of the flash memory.
Regarding claim 11, see contents of rejected claim 1 above. Baker additionally discloses a backing configured to receive the sealed housing (backing 104; in the alternate, the backing can also be considered 105 depending on how the skilled artisan interprets “receive”).
Claims 2 and 12 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Baker in view of Wang, and in further view of Cross et al. US Publication 2008/0288026 (hereinafter Cross).
Regarding claim 2 and 12, Baker is silent on the seal. Cross teaches an ECG monitoring patch that includes a seal coupling surrounding the electrical contacts (0051] at element 90, see also Figure 5). It would have been obvious to the skilled artisan before the effective filing date to utilize the seal as taught by Cross with the device of Baker in order to protect the contacts from moisture.
Claims 5-6 and 15-16 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Baker in view of Wang, and in further view of Hugh et al. US Publication 2011/0021937 (hereinafter Hugh).
Regarding claims 5-6 and 15-16, Baker is silent on the actigraphy sensor and associated circuits. Hugh teaches an ECG monitoring device that includes circuitry for an actigraphy sensor (Figure 12B, circuitry “M”, [0052][0064]), wherein the actigraphy sensor generates interrupt signals to the microcontroller based on a position of the housing ([0052][0064]). It would have been obvious to the skilled artisan before the effective filing date to utilize the actigraphy sensor and circuits as taught by Hugh with the device of Baker in order to aid in determining any anomalies in the ECG signals.
Claims 7 and 17 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Baker in view of Wang, and in further view of McGusty et al. US Publication 2011/0237924 (hereinafter McGusty).
Regarding claims 7 and 17, Baker is silent on the material choice. McGusty teaches an ECG monitoring patch that includes a housing (110), wherein the housing comprises polycarbonate, ABS, or an alloy of polycarbonate and ABS ([0100]). It would have been obvious to the skilled artisan before the effective filing date to utilize the material choice as taught by McGusty with the device of Baker as ABS is both light weight and durable enough to protect the circuits ([0100], McGusty).
Claims 9-10 and 19-20 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Baker in view of Wang, and in further view of Kovacs US Publication 2007/0208232 (hereinafter Kovacs).
Regarding claims 9-10 and 19-20, Baker is silent on the expansion port. Kovacs teaches an ECG monitoring device that includes an expansion port via which an external device interfaces to the microcontroller (Figure 8 at 46, and [0040] which details off chip sensors can be added to the system and connected to the controller), wherein the external device comprises a physiological sensor ([0040]-[0046] details various sensors including accelerometers, load sensors, pulse oximetry and temperature sensors). It would have been obvious to the skilled artisan before the effective filing date to utilize the additional port/sensors as taught by Kovacs with the device of Baker in order to allow for a more robust monitoring system.
