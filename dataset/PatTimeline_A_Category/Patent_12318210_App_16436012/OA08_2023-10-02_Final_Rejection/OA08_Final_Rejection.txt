EXAMINER’S ANSWER
This is in response to the appeal brief filed 07/27/2023.
(1) Grounds of Rejection to be Reviewed on Appeal
Every ground of rejection set forth in the Office action dated 03/30/2023 from which the appeal is taken is being maintained by the examiner except for the grounds of rejection (if any) listed under the subheading “WITHDRAWN REJECTIONS.”  New grounds of rejection (if any) are provided under the subheading “NEW GROUNDS OF REJECTION.”
The following ground(s) of rejection are applicable to the appealed claims.
Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 101
Claims 1-12, 23-27, and 29-40 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 101 because the claimed invention is directed to an abstract idea without significantly more. Claims 1, 23, 32 and 37 recite a system and insertable cardiac monitor for identifying premature ventricular contraction (PVC) based on slope intervals and correlation values between a current ventricular depolarization, a preceding adjacent ventricular depolarization, and a subsequent adjacent ventricular depolarization meeting predefined slope or correlation criteria; determining a PVC detection quantification; and communication circuitry that transmits PVC detection quantification data to a user computing device or server. To determine whether a claim satisfies the criteria for subject matter eligibility, the claim is evaluated according to a stepwise process as described in MPEP 2106(III) and 2106.03-2106.05. The instant claims are evaluated according to such analysis.
Step 1: Is the claim to a process, machine, manufacture or composition of matter?
Claims 1, 23, 32, and 37 are directed towards a system and insertable cardiac monitor, and thus meet the requirements for step 1.
Step 2A (Prong 1): Does the claim recite an abstract idea, law of nature, or natural phenomenon?
Claims 1, 23, 32, and 37 recite a system for identifying premature ventricular contraction (PVC) based on slope intervals and correlation values between a current ventricular depolarization, a preceding adjacent ventricular depolarization, and a subsequent adjacent ventricular depolarization meeting predefined slope or correlation criteria, and determining a PVC detection quantification based on the determination that the ventricular depolarizations are PVC, and communication circuitry that transmits PVC detection quantification data to a user computing device. If a claim limitation, under its broadest reasonable interpretation, covers performance of the limitation in the mind but for the recitation of generic computer components, then it falls within the “Mental Processes” grouping of abstract ideas. Therefore, claims 1, 23, 32, and 37 recite an abstract idea of a mental process.
Claims 1, 23, 32, and 37 recite the abstract idea of a mental process. The limitation of a system and insertable cardiac monitor for identifying premature ventricular contraction, as drafted in claims 1, 23, 32, and 37, under its broadest reasonable interpretation, covers performance of the steps the system performs in the mind, but for the recitation of generic sensors (i.e. electrodes), processing and communication circuitry, and a server and network. For example, identifying PVC can include identifying ventricular depolarizations, a maximum and minimum slope point, an interval from the maximum slope point to the minimum slope point (e.g. Fig. 6 of the instant specification depicts a graph containing a cardiac electrogram including PVC depolarizations. Based on Fig. 6, it would be feasible for a user to visually determine and locate maximum and minimum slope points and an interval from the maximum and minimum slope point), and identifying PVC if the slope intervals satisfy predetermined slope criteria. Determining a PVC detection quantification can include a healthcare professional quantifying the amount of PVC, and communicating it to the patient or another user. Other than reciting electrodes processing and communication circuitry, a server and network, and the device being an insertable cardiac monitor with a housing, nothing in the elements of the claims precludes the step from practically being performed in the mind.
Step 2A (Prong 2): Does the claim recite additional elements that integrate the judicial exception into a practical application?
Claims 1, 23, 32, and 37 recite the additional elements of a “plurality of electrodes”, “processing circuitry”, and “communication circuitry”, claims 32 and 37 recite an “insertable cardiac monitor comprising: a housing configured for subcutaneous implantation within a patient, the housing having a length, a width, and a depth, wherein the length is greater than each of the width and the depth”, and a “network”, and claim 37 recites a “server”. However, these elements are recited at a high level of generality (i.e. as generic electrodes performing the generic function of data gathering (e.g. sensing cardiac electrogram), and as generic processing and communication circuitry and a server and network to perform the function of identifying slope and correlation, determining if the slope and correlation meet certain criteria, and transmitting the data to a user) such that they amount to no more than mere instructions to apply the exception using generic computer components.
Further, the limitation of an insertable cardiac medical device with a housing having a length greater than the width and depth with electrodes positioned at the outer surface is known, as disclosed by Hou et al., US Patent Application Publication 2014/0107723 (e.g. Fig. 3A: implantable device that has a length greater than the width and depth with electrodes 318 on the outside); Bornzin et al., US Patent Application Publication 2015/0335894 (e.g. Par. [0035]: electrodes may be located on the housing of the implantable device; Fig. 10: implantable device that has a length greater than the width and depth); Bornzin et al., US Patent 9,427,594 (e.g. Fig. 5: implantable device that has a length greater than the width and depth with electrode 520 attached to the housing; Col. 14, lines 37-45); Sarkar, US Patent Application Publication 2016/0310031 (e.g. Par. [0024]: “insertable cardiac monitor 300 is defined by a length L, a width W and thickness or depth D and is in the form of an elongated rectangular prism wherein the length L is much larger than the width W, which in turn is larger than the depth D”; Fig. 2: implantable device that has a length greater than the width and depth with electrodes 304 and 306 attached to the housing).
Furthermore, the system provides no means of improving diagnosis or drug dosage or changing any stimulation or therapy parameters (as described in Applicant’s Specification in par. [0050]). See MPEP 2106.05(b) and (f).
Accordingly, the additional elements do not integrate the abstract idea into a practical application.
Step 2B: Does the claim recite additional elements that amount to significantly more than the judicial exception?
The additional elements when considered individually and in combination are not enough to qualify as significantly more than the abstract idea. As discussed above with respect to integration of the abstract idea into a practical application, the additional elements of using electrodes and processing and communication circuitry to perform the steps of identifying cardiac electrogram and determining PVC amounts to no more than mere instructions to apply the exception using generic computer components. Mere instructions to apply an exception using a generic computer component cannot provide an inventive concept. Therefore, the claims are not patent eligible. 9. While there are no prior art rejections for claims 1-12, 23-27, and 29-40, they are not indicated as allowable due to the rejection under 35 U.S.C. 101.

(2) Response to Argument
First, starting at page 17, Appellant argues that the Board should reverse the rejection of all claims based on 35 U.S.C. 101. Appellant further argues that the claims are eligible under both Alice’s step one and step two. For step one, Appellant argues that the claims are like those in CardioNet, which were determined to be “directed to an improved cardiac monitoring device and not to an abstract idea.” CardioNet, 955 F.3d at 1368. For step two, Appellant argues that the record does not suggest that the claimed technique was well-understood, routine, or conventional, as confirmed by the declarations of Dr. Berger and Dr. Sarkar. Starting on page 18, Appellant goes through an analysis of patent eligibility following the MPEP’s multi-part test.
In the paragraph spanning pages 18-19, Appellant agrees that the claims recite statutory subject matter as explained in MPEP Step 1.
Appellant then argues, see pages 19-21, that the claims are not directed to an abstract idea (MPEP Step 2A) under Alice’s step one, and are instead directed to a specific improvement in PVC-detection technology. Appellant further argues, see last paragraph on page 21, that even under the MPEP framework, the claims are eligible under either Step 2A, prong 1 or 2 because they recite a specific, practical application that provides a technological improvement in PVC detection technology.
Appellant then continues with the analysis of the MPEP framework, and argues that the claims do not recite an abstract idea under MPEP Step 2A, Prong 1. Appellant first points to CardioNet, and argues that the instant claims are not directed to an abstract idea because they are akin to those involved in CardioNet, which were found to be patent eligible (see pages 22-26). Appellant argues that the claims at issue in CardioNet were directed to an improved medical device, similar to the instant claims, and as such, the instant claims should be determined to be patent eligible just as CardioNet’s were. However, this is not found to be persuasive, due to distinct fact patterns. While the CardioNet claims may recite a device for cardiac monitoring that involves the detection of ventricular beats, they are different from the slope-based morphology method of the instant claims. Therefore, the instant claims are analyzed as described in the Non-Final Rejection of 03/30/2023 and in the current response.
Appellant further argues, see pages 26-33, that the claims do not recite a “mental process” under MPEP 2106.04(a)(2)(III)(A), because the claims include one or more limitations that cannot practically be performed by the human mind. Appellant argues that the specific methodology for analyzing the morphology of an EGM wave using slope determinations cannot be performed by the human mind, and that it is not feasible for a human to accurately identify, by mere visual inspection, the precise maximum and minimum slope points in a cardiac electrogram signal. Appellant points to SRI Intl and SiRf as examples of patent eligible claims that include limitations that cannot be performed by the human mind.  This is not found to be persuasive. As explained on page 7 of the Non-Final Rejection, it would be feasible for a human to visually determine and locate maximum and minimum slope points and an interval from the maximum and minimum slope points. It would further be possible for a human, using pen and paper, to determine precise maximum and minimum slope points in a cardiac electrogram signal. Therefore, this is not found to be persuasive. Further, the references to SRI Intl and SiRF are not applicable here due to distinct fact patterns.
Appellant further argues that the claims are patent eligible because they are a practical application (MPEP Step 2A, Prong 2). Appellant argues that even if the claims recite an abstract idea, the claims integrate any abstract idea into a practical application. Further, Appellant argues, see pages 33-39, that the instant invention provides an improvement to technology under MPEP 2106.04(d)(1). Appellant argues that the claims are directed towards improved detection of premature ventricular contractions, as explained by the declarations of Dr. Berger and Dr. Sarkar. Appellant further argues, see pages 39-43, that the claims are directed towards a specific PVC detection method, and are not merely applying a preexisting technique using conventional computer equipment. Appellant further argues that the instant claims are comparable to those held eligible in prior cases, and points to Ex Parte Bruce as an example.
However, these arguments are not found to be persuasive. The declarations of Dr. Berger and Dr. Sarkar have been considered, but the improved PVC detection method, as claimed, is still directed to an abstract idea. The abstract idea, as claimed, includes the following steps:
Sensing a cardiac electrogram,
Identifying a plurality of ventricular depolarizations within the cardiac electrogram,
Identifying a maximum and minimum slope point and an interval from the maximum slope point to the minimum slope point for each of the plurality of ventricular depolarizations,
Determine that the intervals from the maximum to the minimum slope for the current, preceding, and a subsequent adjacent ventricular depolarization satisfy one or more slope criteria,
Determine that the current ventricular depolarization is a premature ventricular depolarization (PVC) based on the intervals from the maximum to the minimum slope point satisfying slope criteria,
Determining a PVC detection quantification based on the determinization that the plurality of ventricular depolarizations are PVCs, and
Transmitting the PVC quantification data to a user device.
These are steps that use a processor (i.e. processing circuitry) to analyze data to generate another form of data, and are therefore steps that are directed to the manipulation of information, which is an abstract idea (Recogni Corp, LLC vs. Nintendo Co., 855 F.3d 1322, 1327 (Fed. Cir. 2017) (“A process that start[s] with data, add[s] an algorithm, and end[s] with a new form of data is directed to an abstract idea.”); SAP Am., Inc. v. Invest Pic, LLC, 898 F.3d 1161, 1167 (Fed. Cir. 2018) (“[S]electing certain information, analyzing it using mathematical techniques, and reporting or displaying the results of the analysis. . . is all abstract.”); Elec. Power Grp., LLC y. Alstom S.A., 830 F.3d 1350, 1353 (Fed. Cir. 2016)). Step a is considered to be a data collection step, a mere insignificant extra-solution activity of data gathering by no more than routine means, steps b-f are considered to be data analysis steps that may be performed in the human mind or using pen and paper and by a generic computer, and step g is considered to be a data output step, an insignificant extra-solution activity of outputting data, as explained in MPEP 2106.05(g). The reference to Ex Parte Bruce are not applicable here due to distinct fact patterns.
Appellant further argues, see pages 43-49, that the claims recite an inventive concept, and further recite additional elements that amount to significantly more than the alleged abstract idea. Appellant argues that the slope-based morphology method claimed is an inventive concept that solves problems specific to PVC detection, and is a technique that adds significantly more than a generic mental process. Appellant further argues that in this regard, the instant claims are analogous to those held eligible in Weisner, and are specific as to how the PVC detection is achieved, and is thus sufficient under Alice’s step two. Further, Appellant argues that even if the individual limitations of the claims are “generic” or “known”, the combination of the claim limitations provides an inventive concept, as confirmed by the declaration of Dr. Berger.
However, this is not found to be persuasive. As recited, claims 1, 23, 32, and 37 recite the additional elements of a “plurality of electrodes”, “processing circuitry”, and “communication circuitry”, claims 32 and 37 recite an “insertable cardiac monitor comprising: a housing configured for subcutaneous implantation within a patient, the housing having a length, a width, and a depth, wherein the length is greater than each of the width and the depth”, and a “network”, and claim 37 recites a “server”. These elements are recited at a high level of generality (i.e. as generic electrodes performing the generic function of data gathering (e.g. sensing cardiac electrogram), and as generic processing and communication circuitry and a server and network to perform the function of identifying slope and correlation, determining if the slope and correlation meet certain criteria, and transmitting the data to a user) such that they amount to no more than mere instructions to apply the slope-based morphology methodology using generic computer components.
Further, the limitation of an insertable cardiac medical device with a housing having a length greater than the width and depth with electrodes positioned at the outer surface is conventional, routine, and well-known, as explained by the prior art of record at page 8 of the Non-Final Rejection of 03/30/2023: Hou et al., US Patent Application Publication 2014/0107723 (e.g. Fig. 3A: implantable device that has a length greater than the width and depth with electrodes 318 on the outside); Bornzin et al., US Patent Application Publication 2015/0335894 (e.g. Par. [0035]: electrodes may be located on the housing of the implantable device; Fig. 10: implantable device that has a length greater than the width and depth); Bornzin et al., US Patent 9,427,594 (e.g. Fig. 5: implantable device that has a length greater than the width and depth with electrode 520 attached to the housing; Col. 14, lines 37-45); Sarkar, US Patent Application Publication 2016/0310031 (e.g. Par. [0024]: “insertable cardiac monitor 300 is defined by a length L, a width W and thickness or depth D and is in the form of an elongated rectangular prism wherein the length L is much larger than the width W, which in turn is larger than the depth D”; Fig. 2: implantable device that has a length greater than the width and depth with electrodes 304 and 306 attached to the housing).
Further, par. [0033] of the instant specification describes the processing circuitry as including any of “a microprocessor, a controller, a digital signal processor (DSP), an application specific integrated circuit (ASIC), a field-programmable gate array (FPGA), or equivalent discrete or analog logic circuitry”, and can be embodied as “software, firmware, hardware or any combination thereof.”, which are well known processing components.  Further, the references to Weisner are not applicable here due to distinct fact patterns.
On pages 50-54, Appellant further requests a separate consideration of claims 32-40. Appellant argues that the additional limitations of an “insertable cardiac monitor”, “housing configured for subcutaneous implantation within a patient, the housing having a length, a width, and a depth, wherein the length is greater than each of the width and depth”, and the “plurality of electrodes” that are “configured to continuously sense a cardiac electrogram of the patient while the insertable cardiac monitor is subcutaneously implanted within the patient over a period of time greater than several days” provide invention-specific hardware that further limit the claims by incorporating these technical components in the claims. Appellant argues that the combined sum of these additionally recited features and the features in common with the other claims amounts to significantly more than a mental process because they contain an inventive concept that was not well-understood, routine, or conventional. Further, Appellant argues that the prior art cited in the analysis of Step 2A, prong 2 in the Non-Final Rejection of 03/30/2023 does not answer the question of whether the inventive concept is well-understood, routine, or conventional, and merely conflates the patent-eligibility analysis with an anticipation or obviousness analysis.
This is not found to be persuasive. While the claims include the additional elements of an “insertable cardiac monitor”, “housing configured for subcutaneous implantation within a patient, the housing having a length, a width, and a depth, wherein the length is greater than each of the width and depth”, and the “plurality of electrodes” that are “configured to continuously sense a cardiac electrogram of the patient while the insertable cardiac monitor is subcutaneously implanted within the patient over a period of time greater than several days”, these elements are well-understood, routine, and conventional, as explained by the prior art reference cited in the Non-Final Rejection on page 8. While these additional elements provide specific hardware, these elements are well-known in the art, and are not considered to be the inventive concept. As claimed, the inventive concept is considered to be the slope-based morphology analysis, which is directed to an abstract idea, as explained above and in the Non-Final Rejection of 03/30/2023.
For the above reasons, it is believed that the rejections should be sustained.
