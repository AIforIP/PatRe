Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 103
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 103 which forms the basis for all obviousness rejections set forth in this Office action:
A patent for a claimed invention may not be obtained, notwithstanding that the claimed invention is not identically disclosed as set forth in section 102, if the differences between the claimed invention and the prior art are such that the claimed invention as a whole would have been obvious before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which the claimed invention pertains. Patentability shall not be negated by the manner in which the invention was made.
The factual inquiries for establishing a background for determining obviousness under 35 U.S.C. 103 are summarized as follows:
1. Determining the scope and contents of the prior art.
2. Ascertaining the differences between the prior art and the claims at issue.
3. Resolving the level of ordinary skill in the pertinent art.
4. Considering objective evidence present in the application indicating obviousness or nonobviousness.
Claims 27-29 and 31-34 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Ive et al. (hereinafter “Ive,” US 2024/0012601) in view of Saur et al. (hereinafter “Saur,” US 2018/0308451).

Allowable Subject Matter
Claims 21-26 are allowed.
The following is a statement of reasons for the indication of allowable subject matter:
Claims 21-26, as amended, are allowable because they are directed to at least a video display having a variable transparency, having an adjustable brightness to illuminate the eyes of a wearer, and adjusting the transparency or brightness so that an observer can see the eyes of the wearer through the video display. These features are not disclosed by the primary reference Ive.
Response to Arguments
The Applicant’s arguments filed on July 12, 2024 have been fully considered by the Examiner.
The Applicant respectfully argues that for both independent claims 21 and 27, “the video display is see-through (e.g., so that an observer can directly see the user's eyes, or so that the user can see the surrounding world past the display). This differs substantially from Ive, in which the user can see only an inward-facing screen (which can display the user's surroundings, but which is not see-through to allow the user to see their surroundings directly). Similarly, although an observer of the Ive device can, in some cases, see a representation of the user's eyes on an outward facing screen, the observer cannot see the user's actual eyes, because the display is not transparent. Indeed, if the Ive device were to lose power or malfunction, the wearer would need to remove the device in order to see their surroundings or to allow observers to see their eyes.”
While this appears to be true for independent claim 21, the Examiner respectfully disagrees that claim 27 is limited by these features. Independent claim 27 does not recite a video display having a variable transparency, having an adjustable brightness to illuminate the eyes of a wearer, and adjusting the transparency or brightness so that an observer can see the eyes of the wearer through the video display, as claim 21 does. Notably, claim 27 instead recites that the outward facing video display “is see-through from the side of the user.” This implies that the display is not necessarily see-through from the side of the viewer. Claim 27 has no mention of transparency, illumination, or seeing the eyes of the wearer, and therefore remains rejected under 35 USC 103 in view of Ive and Saur supra.
