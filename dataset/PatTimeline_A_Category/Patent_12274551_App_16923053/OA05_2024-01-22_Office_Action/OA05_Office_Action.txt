Response to Arguments
Applicant’s arguments in combination with amendments, see claims and remarks, filed 10/09/2023, with respect to the rejection(s) of claim(s) 1-5, 7, 21-23, 26-27 under 35 USC 103 have been fully considered but are not persuasive.
Beginning on page 8, the applicant argues that the claim estimates a measure of effectiveness as recited in paragraph 0130. Initially, it is noted that the claim does not require all the limitations provided in the paragraph. Additionally, as written, the claim does not require the data to be processed in real-time. As such, one could argue that a person monitoring the user, and the user’s reaction could choose the next “content” to be played based on any “targeted story arc”.
The applicant also argues that “the human mind is not equipped to perform the claim limitations”. This argument is fully considered but is not convincing. As written, there is nothing in the claim that would prevent a human to view the reaction of the user and to choose the next content to be played when given sufficient time.
On page 9, the applicant argues that the claim includes additional elements that are sufficient to amount to significantly more”. The applicant points to the limitation of “automatically generating scripts for target users”. These arguments are fully considered but are not convincing. As written, the claim does not require the method to be performed “automatically”, therefore, the applicant’s arguments regarding the methods automatic operation are considered irrelevant. Additionally, as written the claim does not provide any details on the “script” nor the “story arc”. Therefore, under its broadest reasonable interpretation, when given sufficient time, a person could provide the user with the next “content” having reviewed the user’s current response to the current content.
For at least the reasons cited above, the claims, as amended, do not overcome the current 101 rejection. See details below.

Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 101
35 U.S.C. 101 reads as follows:
Whoever invents or discovers any new and useful process, machine, manufacture, or composition of matter, or any new and useful improvement thereof, may obtain a patent therefor, subject to the conditions and requirements of this title.
Claims 1-5, 7, 21-23, 26-27 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 101 because of the following analysis:
1 – statutory category: Claim 1 recites a series of steps and therefore, falls under the statutory category of being a process. See MPEP 2106.03. Claim 7 recites an apparatus, and therefore, falls under the statutory category of being a thing or products. See MPEP 2106.03.
2A – Prong 1: The independent claims 1 and 7 recite a judicial exception by reciting the limitations of “randomizing parameters of a model”, “generating a plurality of scripts”, “varying parameters between different ones of the script”, “targeted story arc and neuro-physiological response predicted by the model”, “correlating known or estimated neurological and cognitive characteristics”, “selecting a segment”, “provide segment to the user”. These limitations, as drafted, is a process that, under its broadest reasonable interpretation, covers performance of the limitation in mind or by a person using a pen and paper.
2A – Prong 2: The claim does not include additional elements that are sufficient to amount to significantly more than the judicial exception because the additional elements when considered both individually and as an ordered combination do not amount to significantly more than the abstract idea. The independent claims 1 and 7 recite the additional limitations of “one or more processor”. The “processor” is recited at a high level of generality and is recited as performing generic computer functions. i.e., data processing. The elements amount to mere instructions to implement an abstract idea on a computer, or merely uses a computer as a tool to perform an abstract idea (see MPEP 2106.04(d) and 2106.05(f)). Accordingly, each of the additional elements do not integrate the abstract idea into a practical application because they do not impose any meaningful limitations on practicing the abstract idea.
2B: The emphasized elements cited above do not amount to significantly more than the judicial exception because these limitations are simply appending well-understood, routine and conventional activities previously known in the industry, specified at a high level of generality, to the judicial exception, e.g., a claim to an abstract idea requiring no more than a generic computer to perform generic computer functions that are well-understood, routine and conventional activities previously known in the industry (see Electric Power Group, 830 F.3d 1350 (Fed. Cir. 2016); Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank Int’I, 110 USPQ2d 1976 (2014)).
In view of the above, the additional elements individually do not amount to significantly more than the above-judicial exception (the abstract idea). Looking at the limitations as an ordered combination (that is, as a whole) adds nothing that is not already present when looking at the elements taken individually. There is no indication that the combination of elements improves the functioning of a computer, for example, or improves any other technology. There is no indication that the combination of elements permits automation of specific tasks that previously could not be automated. There is no indication that the combination of elements includes a particular solution to a computer-based problem or a particular way to achieve a desired computer-based outcome. Rather, the collective functions of the claimed invention merely provide conventional computer implementation, i.e., the computer is simply a tool to perform the process. Simply appending well-understood, routine, conventional activities previously known to the industry, specified at a high level of generality, to the judicial exception, e.g., a claim to an abstract idea requiring no more than a generic computer to perform generic computer functions that are well-understood, routine and conventional activities previously known to the industry, as discussed in Alice Corp., 573 U.S. at 225, 110 USPQ2d at 1984 (see MPEP § 2106.05(d)).
Claims 2-5, 26-27 depend from independent claim 1 while claims 21-23, 25 depend from independent claim 7. The mentioned claims 2-6, and 21-27 recite the same abstract idea as the independent claims. Furthermore, these claims only contain recitations that further limit the abstract idea (that is, the claims only recite limitations that further limit the mental process). For example, the dependent claim recites the limitations “calculating content engagement power (CEP)”, “determining arousal values”, “comparing average arousal with an expectation average”, “CEP including valence values”, “determining a neurological error”, “story arc comprising a set of neurological values”, “three-dimensional model used for complex emotions”, and “neuro-physiological responses including … data”, which are recited at a high level of generality and is recited as performing generic computer functions. i.e., data processing and display. The elements amount to mere instructions to implement an abstract idea on a computer, or merely uses a computer as a tool to perform an abstract idea (see MPEP 2106.04(d) and 2106.05(f)).
