Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 103
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 103 which forms the basis for all obviousness rejections set forth in this Office action:
A patent for a claimed invention may not be obtained, notwithstanding that the claimed invention is not identically disclosed as set forth in section 102, if the differences between the claimed invention and the prior art are such that the claimed invention as a whole would have been obvious before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which the claimed invention pertains. Patentability shall not be negated by the manner in which the invention was made.
Claims 1-5, 7, and 18-19 is/are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Yee (US 2010/0324392) in view of Zdeblick (US 2015/0150480).
Regarding claim 1, Yee discloses a device comprising:
a biosensor comprising:
a first electrode having a distal end for insertion into a subcutaneous layer beneath a patient's skin (paragraph [0076]),
a second electrode coupled to the first electrode, a portion of the second electrode for insertion into the subcutaneous layer (paragraph [0076]), and
a first battery to apply a voltage across the first and second electrodes, the first battery electrically decoupled from the at least one of the electrodes (element 82; paragraph [0131]);
and a wake-up circuit configured to receive a signal upon insertion into the subcutaneous layer and, in response, to electrically couple the battery to the first and second electrodes (paragraph [0131]).
Yee does not disclose an additional battery formed from an anode material physically coupled to a portion of the distal end of the first electrode and the distal portion of the second electrode, where the additional battery is configured to activate upon immersion in an electrolytic fluid, and the wake-up circuit being configured to receive a signal from the additional battery to trigger the electrical coupling; rather, Yee teaches detecting insertion of the distal portions of the electrodes into an electrolytic fluid by using an alternate configuration of an electric circuit that also indicates insertion via a change in electrical characteristics (paragraph [0131], [0136], [0137]) and then triggers the electrical coupling to the main battery upon receipt of the signal indicating that insertion (paragraph [0131]).
Zdeblick teaches a sensing system comprising a battery including an anode, where exposure of the anode to an electrolytic fluid in a desired location results in a measurable change in electrical characteristics activates that battery such that it sends a signal signaling to the remainder of the system to activate (paragraphs [0118]-[0120]). It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art at the time the invention was filed to have made the device of Yee with the components for activating upon insertion to send a signal including a battery with anode that transmits a signal upon exposure to electrolyte, as taught by Zdeblick, because it is merely the substitution of one process of detecting insertion using components that detect an electrical change generated by insertion into a body for an equivalent. As modifying the device of Yee, exposure to electrolytic fluid would necessarily require the location of the anode to be in an inserted area, such as on the distally inserted portions of the electrodes.
Regarding claim 2, Yee further discloses a glucose oxidase material disposed on a portion of the distal end of the first electrode (paragraph [0032]).
Regarding claim 3, Yee further discloses a radio frequency (RF) transmitter (paragraph [0081]), and wherein: the battery is at least partially electrically decoupled from the RF transmitter and is further configured to be coupled to the first battery which applies power to the RF transmitter after being activated (paragraph [0099], [0131] - the RF transmitter is an electronics component of the electronics assembly; as the assembly is a whole is coupled to the battery powered after activation, the RF transmitter would be among these devices).
Regarding claim 4, Yee further discloses that one electrode may comprise a platinum material (paragraph [0090]) and the other may comprise a silver/silver-chloride material coating a portion of the first electrode (paragraph [0090] incorporates by reference US 6103033, which recites such materials in column 11, lines 49-61); Zdeblick’s  anode material comprises zinc, iron, aluminum, or nickel (Table 1).
Regarding claim 5, Zdeblick does not specify a particular amount of anodic material, such as approximately 10 to 100 nanograms (ng) of zinc, approximately 10 to 100 ng of nickel, approximately 10 to 100 ng of iron, or approximately 3 to 30 ng of aluminum, but where the general conditions of a claim are disclosed in the prior art, it is not inventive to discover the optimum or workable ranges by routine experimentation. In re Aller, 220 F.2d 454, 456, 105 USPQ 233, 235 (CCPA 1955) (MPEP 2144.05), such that it would be obvious to try using these amounts of anodic material to achieve efficient activation of the device.
Regarding claim 7, Yee further discloses that the device further comprises a processor, and wherein the wake-up circuit is further to electrically couple the second battery to the processor (paragraph [0099], [0131] - the processor is an electronics component of the electronics assembly; as the assembly is a whole is powered after activation, the processor would be among these devices).
Regarding claim 18, Yee discloses a device comprising:
a biosensor comprising:
a first electrode having a distal end for insertion into a subcutaneous layer beneath a patient's skin (paragraph [0076]), and
a second electrode coupled to the first electrode, a portion of the second electrode for insertion into the subcutaneous layer (paragraph [0076]).
Yee does not disclose a battery formed from an anode material physically coupled to a portion of the distal end of the first electrode and the distal portion of the second electrode, where the battery is configured to activate upon immersion in an electrolytic fluid; rather, Yee teaches detecting insertion of the distal portions of the electrodes into an electrolytic fluid by using an alternate configuration of an electric circuit that also indicates insertion (i.e., “activates”) via a change in electrical characteristics (paragraph [0131], [0136], [0137]).
Zdeblick teaches a sensing system comprising a battery including an anode, where exposure of the anode to an electrolytic fluid in a desired location “activates” the battery which subsequently results in a measurable change in electrical characteristics signaling to the remainder of the system to activate (paragraphs [0118]-[0120]). It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art at the time the invention was filed to have made the device of Yee with the components for detecting insertion including a battery with anode that “activates” by transmitting a signal upon exposure to electrolyte, as taught by Zdeblick, because it is merely the substitution of one process of detecting insertion using components that detect an electrical change generated by insertion into a body for an equivalent. As modifying the device of Yee, exposure to electrolytic fluid would necessarily require the location of the anode to be in an inserted area, such as on the distally inserted portions of the electrodes.
Regarding claim 19, Yee further discloses a glucose oxidase material disposed on a portion of the distal end of the first electrode (paragraph [0032]).
Claims 6, 8 is/are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Yee and Zdeblick, as applied above, and further in view of Yin (US 2004/0263264).
Yee, as modified, does not disclose that the wake-up circuit comprises a ring oscillator electrically coupled to a clock circuit of the processor (paragraphs [0078]-[0079]) to activate the clock circuit after receiving the activation signal. Yin teaches a circuit which comprises a ring oscillator electrically coupled to a clock circuit of a processor to activate the clock circuit after receiving an activation signal (paragraph [0029]). It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art at the time the invention was filed to have made the device of Yee, as modified, with a ring oscillator electrically coupled to a clock circuit of the processor to activate the clock circuit after receiving the activation signal, as taught by Yin, in order to control timing of additional functions in the circuit based on the timing of the activation.
Response to Arguments
Applicant's arguments filed 8 May 2024 have been fully considered but they are not persuasive.
Applicant initially argues that modifying Yee with Zdeblick would not result in the anode material being “coupled to” a portion of the distal end of the first electrode; as Yee teaches the components used to detect insertion being “coupled to” the electrode, modifying Yee to include the anode material of Zdeblick as the component used to detect insertion would inherently result in the anode material being “coupled to” the distal end of the first electrode.
Applicant then argues that Zdeblick does not teach a wake-up circuit coupled to the second battery coupled to the second battery and configured to electrically couple the first battery to the electrodes in response to a signal from the first battery and instead teaches a mechanical switch used to activate the device, supposedly cited on p. 7 of the previous Office Action, and asserts that this has not been “squarely” addressed. It is unclear how Applicant has reached this conclusion as the previous Office Action cited to only the same portions of Zdeblick, which address a battery formed of an anode, cathode, and electrolyte, which is activated when the anode and cathode are exposed to the electrolyte and sends a signal indicative of the battery being activated by its insertion. Yee is clearly cited as teaching a “wake-up circuit” which is configured to electrically couple the “first battery” to the electrodes upon receipt of a signal, in this case a signal indicative of insertion; as modified by Zdeblick the received signal would be the signal received from the battery formed by the anode being inserted and thus completed.
Applicant concludes by asserting that Zdeblick “has no wake-up circuit” which couples the battery upon receiving a signal; again, this has been disclosed by Yee, as is set forth in the rejections of the previous Office Action and again above. This misinterpretation of the references is not persuasive to overcome the rejections as applied. The Examiner also notes that Applicant’s arguments do not address any of the disclosures of Yee or Yin, only Zdeblick.
