Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 102
In the event the determination of the status of the application as subject to AIA  35 U.S.C. 102 and 103 (or as subject to pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 102 and 103) is incorrect, any correction of the statutory basis (i.e., changing from AIA  to pre-AIA ) for the rejection will not be considered a new ground of rejection if the prior art relied upon, and the rationale supporting the rejection, would be the same under either status.
The following is a quotation of the appropriate paragraphs of 35 U.S.C. 102 that form the basis for the rejections under this section made in this Office action:
A person shall be entitled to a patent unless –
(a)(1) the claimed invention was patented, described in a printed publication, or in public use, on sale, or otherwise available to the public before the effective filing date of the claimed invention.
(a)(2) the claimed invention was described in a patent issued under section 151, or in an application for patent published or deemed published under section 122(b), in which the patent or application, as the case may be, names another inventor and was effectively filed before the effective filing date of the claimed invention.
Claims 1 - 20 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 102(a)(1) and/or 102(a)(2) as being anticipated by Ye (US Pub. No. 2021/0370186 A1).
As per claim 1, Ye et al. discloses an apparatus, comprising: at least one computer storage that is not a transitory signal and that comprises instructions executable by at least one processor (see [0073]) to: generate a first screen render from a first viewpoint, the first viewpoint being of a first character, the first character being a player’s character (see Fig. 3B, [0073], [0076]); input the first screen render to at least one machine learning (ML) model (Fig. 3B: 400, 410 and [0073]); receive from the ML model at least one transform of the first screen render (See Fig.3B:430b), the transform comprising at least a second screen render from a second viewpoint, the second viewpoint being of a non-player character (NPC) (Fig. 3B and [0072] - [0076]); and input the second screen render to a ML-implemented agent controlling the NPC, or present on at least one display the second screen render, or both input the second screen render to a ML-implemented agent controlling the NPC and present on at least one display the second screen render (See Fig. 3B, [0072] – [0074]).
As per claim 2, Ye et al. discloses the first and second screen renders comprise respective first and second game videos (first screen renders element OCV and second screen render elements ASCV, see [0092]).
As per claim 3, Ye et al. discloses the ML model is trained using game images captured during a human play session (model is further trained using additional game play data generated with ongoing game inputs provided by the players during the game session, see [0050]).
As per claim 4, Ye et al. discloses the instructions are executable to avoid rendering multiple viewpoints simultaneously at least in part by: rendering a single viewpoint (see Fig. 3B and [0062]); using the ML model, transforming the single viewpoint to output video at viewpoints of one or more other NPCs in the area in a single inference pass (Fig. 3B, [0065], [0072]).
As per claim 5, Ye et al. discloses the first screen render is sent from a cloud server to at least one display (metadata from the executing video game may be transmitted over the network to the game cloud server, see [0049]) for presentation of the first screen render at a first time and actions of NPCs are delayed for at least one frame while the ML model generates the second screen render (see [0016] and [0054]).
As per claim 6, Ye et al. discloses the at least one processor programmed with the instructions (see [0073]).
As per claim 7, Ye et al. discloses the instructions are executable to: input the second screen render to a ML-implemented agent controlling the NPC ([0058], [0072]); and execute the ML-implemented agent to control the NPC (active spectator camera view generated from inputs of active spectator captures a different perspective of the game scene capture and includes a view of a second  game object in addition to game object captured, see [0058]).
As per claim 8, Ye et al. discloses a method, comprising: inputting, to at least one machine learning (ML) model, at least a player character (PC) video stream of at least one computer simulation (see Fig. 3B and [0073]); receiving, from the ML model, at least one non-player character (NPC) video stream for the computer simulation (see Fig. 3B. [0072] – [0076]);
presenting the PC video stream on at least one video display (see [0067]); and inputting the NPC video stream to a ML-implemented agent controlling the NPC (rendering the camera views output from recommendation engine element with the machine learning model and streaming them to a spectator element, see Fig. 3B, [0070] and [0115]).
As per claim 9, Ye et al. discloses presenting the PC video stream on a player display and presenting the NPC video stream on a video display other than the player display (see Fig.2A).
As per claim 10, Ye et al. discloses inputting information related to one or more NPCs to the ML model (see [0086]).
As per claim 11, Ye et al. discloses the information related to one or more NPCs comprises geometric information indicating a virtual spatial relationship between at least one NPC and at least one player character (see [0057]).
As per claim 12, Ye et al. discloses training the ML model using game images captured during a human play session (model is further trained using additional game play data generated with ongoing game inputs provided by the players during the game session, see [0050]).
As per claim 13, Ye et al. discloses avoiding rendering multiple viewpoints simultaneously at least in part by: rendering a single viewpoint; (see Fig. 3B and [0062]); using the ML model, transforming the single viewpoint to output video at viewpoints of one or more other NPCs in the area in a single inference pass (Fig. 3B, [0065], [0072]).
As per claim 14, Ye et al. discloses sending the PC video stream from a cloud server to at least one display for presentation of the PC video stream at a first time (metadata from the executing video game may be transmitted over the network to the game cloud server, see [0049]); and delaying actions of NPCs for at least one frame while the ML model generates the NPC video stream (see [0016] and [0054]).
As per claims 15 – 20, the instant claims are an apparatus in which corresponds to the method of claims 9 – 14. Therefore, it is rejected for the reasons set forth above.
