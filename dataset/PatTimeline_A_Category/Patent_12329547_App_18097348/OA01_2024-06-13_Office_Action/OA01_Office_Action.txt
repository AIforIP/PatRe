Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 112
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 112(b):
(b)  CONCLUSION.—The specification shall conclude with one or more claims particularly pointing out and distinctly claiming the subject matter which the inventor or a joint inventor regards as the invention.
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 112 (pre-AIA ), second paragraph:
The specification shall conclude with one or more claims particularly pointing out and distinctly claiming the subject matter which the applicant regards as his invention.
Claims 1-20 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 112(b) or 35 U.S.C. 112 (pre-AIA ), second paragraph, as being indefinite for failing to particularly point out and distinctly claim the subject matter which the inventor or a joint inventor (or for applications subject to pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 112, the applicant), regards as the invention.
Claims 1 and 13 recites the limitation the first pulmonary signal metric and “the second and the third pulmonary signal metrics” in the claims.  There is insufficient antecedent basis for these limitations in the claims. It is not clear whether the signal metrics (or the physiological information) should be pulmonary signal metrics and/or whether the physiological information should be pulmonary information.
Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 101
35 U.S.C. 101 reads as follows:
Whoever invents or discovers any new and useful process, machine, manufacture, or composition of matter, or any new and useful improvement thereof, may obtain a patent therefor, subject to the conditions and requirements of this title.
Claims 1, 5-6, 8-13, and 16-20 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 101 because the claimed invention is directed to an abstract idea without significantly more. The claim(s) recite(s) the process of using a mathematical formula to output a pulmonary risk score. This judicial exception is not integrated into a practical application because the calculation is merely output such as the case in Electric Power Group v. Alstom, S.A., 830 F.3d 1350, 1353-54, 119 USPQ2d 1739, 1741-42 (Fed. Cir. 2016). The claim(s) does/do not include additional elements that are sufficient to amount to significantly more than the judicial exception because as discussed above, the inputs to this formula are well-known and conventional metrics, further analogous to Electric Power. Evidence that such additional elements are well-known and convention is shown by the publications cited herein. The use of a neural network as claimed in claim 18 is insufficient to transform the abstract idea into eligible subject matter given that this neural network is still another calculation using well-known and conventional techniques to arrive at a mathematical answer that is a pulmonary risk score.  Moreover, simply providing therapy based on a calculated metric is no more than routine and conventional techniques that fail to integrate the abstract idea into a practical application given that there does not appear to be any language in the claim that particularly links the abstract idea to the further limitations.

As to claim 1, Patangay discloses a medical-device system, comprising:
a risk stratification circuit configured to:

generate distinct first, second, and third signal metrics using the received physiological information (see Fig 3, elements 430A, 440A, and 440B, respectively);
calculate a primary pulmonary risk score using the first pulmonary signal metric (see Fig 4, element 433 – note that other scores may be used when calculating the first pulmonary risk score given that the open-ended “comprising” is used in the claim), and calculate a secondary pulmonary risk score using the second and the third pulmonary signal metrics (see Fig 4, element 443);
calculate a composite pulmonary risk score using the primary and the secondary pulmonary risk scores (see Fig 4, element 450) ; and
an output circuit configured to provide the calculated composite pulmonary risk to a user or a process (see Fig 3, element 335 and [0046])
As to claims 5, Patangay further discloses wherein the first, the second, and the third signal metrics are each selected from the group consisting of: a respiration rate; a ratio of respiration rate to tidal volume; a third heart sound (S3) intensity; a ratio of the S3 intensity to a reference heart sound intensity; a heart rate; a thoracic impedance; and a physical activity level (see [0040]-[0042]).
As to claims 12 and 20, Patangay further discloses a therapy circuit configured to initiate or adjust a therapy to treat a cardiorespiratory disease in accordance with the calculated composite pulmonary risk score (see [0045]).
Claim 13 is rejected for the reasons provided in the treatment of claim 1.

Claim(s) 2, 4, 14 is/are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Patangay in view of US 2008/0183085 A1 to van Oort et al. (“van Oort”).
As to claims 2, Patangay fails to disclose wherein the system further comprises a physiological event detector circuit configured to automatically detect a cardiorespiratory event of the patient based at least in part on the calculated composite pulmonary risk score.
However, van Oort discloses a similar system and further teaches a physiological event detector circuit configured to automatically detect a cardiorespiratory event of the patient based at least in part on a calculated health risk in several health categories, many of which include pulmonary risk (see, e.g., [0028], [0032], [0037], [0044], and [0049]). It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art to combine the pulmonary risk score of Patangay with the automatic detection of cardiorespiratory events of a patient as taught by van Oort in order to achieve the predictable result of allowing the device to store higher resolution data that may be particularly relevant for a health care clinician and/or delivering particular therapies to the user based on the more detailed monitoring caused by increased risk determination.
As to claims 4 and 14 (see treatment of claim 2 for other limitations), van Oort further discloses wherein the cardiorespiratory event includes at least one of worsening heart failure (WHF), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, or pulmonary edema (see [0013]).
Claim(s) 8-9 and 17-18 is/are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Patangay and further in view of US 2013/0116578 A1 to An et al. (“An”).
As to claims 8 and 17, it does not appear that either Patangay discloses wherein the risk stratification circuit is configured to calculate the secondary pulmonary risk score using the second signal metric weighted by the third signal metric.
However, in a similar invention, An teaches that a first signal metric may be used to influence (i.e., weight) of another signal metric when combining the two to signal an alert (i.e. a score that reaches a particular threshold for sounding an alarm) (see, e.g., [0194] – “In an illustrative example, the first physiological parameter may be the amplitude of the S3 heart sound and the second physiological parameter may be fluid status of the subject that is derived from intrathoracic impedance. Decreasing intrathoracic impedance may indicate fluid retention. Based on the measured S3 amplitude, the risk analysis module 3005 may assign a higher risk to the subject. The risk analysis module 3005 may then lower the value of an intrathoracic impedance threshold used by the WHF detection module 3010 to increase the sensitivity of prediction of a WHF event. If the risk analysis module 3005 does not identify an S3 heart sound in the heart sound signal, the risk analysis module 3005 may raise the value of an intrathoracic impedance threshold used by the WHF detection module 3010 to decrease the sensitivity of prediction of an HF event.”)(emphasis added).
It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filling date of the claimed invention to modify the calculation of the second pulmonary risk score of Patangay as taught by An in order to achieve the predictable result of lowering false positives and/or reducing false negatives by adjusting the weights to better reflect a user’s health condition as taught by An.
As to claim 9, Patangay fails to disclose wherein the risk stratification circuit is configured to calculate the composite pulmonary risk score using a linear or a nonlinear combination of the primary and secondary pulmonary risk scores.
However, An discloses such a combination for calculating a related composite health score (see [0061]-[0065]). It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filling date of the claimed invention to combine the linear combination as taught by An given that An teaches that such a composite score allows its user to weight symptoms and signs in such a way as to emphasize those factors that are most important to patient health.
As to claims 10 and 18, Patangay fails to disclose wherein the risk stratification circuit is configured to calculate the composite pulmonary risk score using a neural network taking the primary and the secondary pulmonary risk scores as input.
However, An discloses such a risk stratification circuit that uses a neural network in a similar fashion (see [0174]). It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filling date of the claimed invention to combine the teachings of Patangay with the neural network of An given that neural networks were well-known for the advantage of automatically learning and extracting relevant features from raw sensor data, reducing the need for manual feature engineering.
Claim(s) 3 and 15 is/are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Patangay in view of van Oort and further in view An.
As to claims 3 and 15, neither Patangay nor van Oort discloses wherein to automatically detect the cardiorespiratory event, the physiological event detector circuit is configured to:
calculate a cardiorespiratory event score using one or more physiological signals sensed from the patient;
determine or adjust an alert threshold based at in part on the calculated composite pulmonary risk score; and
generate an alert of the cardiorespiratory event when the cardiorespiratory event score exceeds the alert threshold.
However, in a related invention, An discloses wherein the physiological event detector circuit is configured to:
calculate a cardiorespiratory event score using one or more physiological signals sensed from the patient (see [0193] – “The risk analysis module 3005 may adjust the detection threshold criterion to make the detection more sensitive or less sensitive to detection according to the determined HF risk score.”);
determine or adjust an alert threshold based at in part on the calculated composite pulmonary risk score (see [0193]); and
generate an alert of the cardiorespiratory event when the cardiorespiratory event score exceeds the alert threshold (see [0193]).
It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filling date of the claimed invention to combine the pulmonary risk score and event detection of Patangay/van Oort with the threshold modification based on a calculated composite pulmonary risk score in order to achieve the predictable result of increasing sensitivity of the event detection.
Claim(s) 6-7 and 16 is/are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Patangay in view of van Oort and further in view of US 2014/0243690 A1 to Dumont et al. (“Dumont”).
As to claims 6 and 16, it does not appear that either Patangay or van Oort discloses wherein the risk stratification circuit is configured to calculate the secondary pulmonary risk score using measurements of the second signal metric obtained at times when the third signal metric satisfies a specified condition.
However, in a related disclosure, Dumont teaches that decision trees may be constructed that operate in a Boolean decision tree that calculates risk scores (see Fig 1 – alert/no alert is a binary score, but still a score) using measurements of one metric obtained at time when another metric satisfies a specified condition (see Fig 1 and [0058]-[0063]). It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to use the teachings of Dumont in the systems of Patangay/van Oort to calculate the secondary pulmonary risk score using measurements of the second signal metric obtained at times when the third signal metric satisfies a specified condition in order to achieve the predictable result of reducing false positives and increasing true negatives by finding the proper combinations of thresholds and signal metrics that are most powerful.
As to claim 7, Dumont further discloses wherein the second signal metric include a respiratory rate and the third signal metric incudes a physical activity intensity or duration, wherein the measurements of the second signal metric include respiratory rates measured during a time period when the physical activity intensity or duration exceeds a threshold (see Fig 1 and [0058]-[0063]).
