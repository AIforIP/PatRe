Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 101
35 U.S.C. 101 reads as follows:
Whoever invents or discovers any new and useful process, machine, manufacture, or composition of matter, or any new and useful improvement thereof, may obtain a patent therefor, subject to the conditions and requirements of this title.
Claims 1, 5-6, 8-13, and 16-20 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 101 because the claimed invention is directed to an abstract idea without significantly more. The claim(s) recite(s) the process of using a mathematical formula to output a pulmonary risk score. This judicial exception is not integrated into a practical application because the calculation is merely output such as the case in Electric Power Group v. Alstom, S.A., 830 F.3d 1350, 1353-54, 119 USPQ2d 1739, 1741-42 (Fed. Cir. 2016). The claim(s) does/do not include additional elements that are sufficient to amount to significantly more than the judicial exception because as discussed above, the inputs to this formula are well-known and conventional metrics, further analogous to Electric Power. Evidence that such additional elements are well-known and convention is shown by the publications cited herein. The use of a neural network as claimed in claim 18 is insufficient to transform the abstract idea into eligible subject matter given that this neural network is still another calculation using well-known and conventional techniques to arrive at a mathematical answer that is a pulmonary risk score.  Moreover, simply providing therapy based on a calculated metric is no more than routine and conventional techniques that fail to integrate the abstract idea into a practical application given that there does not appear to be any language in the claim that particularly links the abstract idea to the further limitations.
In keeping with the requirement that all claims be considered as a whole, Examiner notes that several claims have not been rejected under Section 101. As an example, claim 3 is not rejected because it includes the limitation wherein the physiological detection circuit is configured to determine or adjust an alert threshold based at least in part on the calculated composite pulmonary risk score. This has an effect of technological improvement as one of ordinary skill in the art would appreciate that the composite pulmonary risk score is being used to adjust the operation of the machine itself by determining or adjusting alert thresholds. This has the effect of making the device more responsive to the needs of the user by at least reducing false positive alerts. Moreover, such a configuration must take in as an input the current state of the user, much like the rubber molding press in Diamond v. Diehr, 450 U.S. 175 (1981) (holding patentable a process that iteratively computed a cure time using a mathematical formula to control the physical process of transforming rubber). These additional elements in, for example, claim 3 amount to significantly more than the judicial exception such that it is integrated into a practical application.

Response to Arguments
Applicant's arguments filed August 12, 2024 have been fully considered but they are not persuasive.
As to the arguments in favor of eligibility under Section 101, Applicants argue that the inventions of claims 1, 5-6, 8-13, and 16-20 amount to an improvement in technology and are therefore eligible under the Alice/Mayo test. Applicants argue that the improvement is in the field of computerized, device-based patient monitoring and pulmonary risk management. Applicants describe the features of the invention as allowing the device to be turned off when the patient as low pulmonary risk as well as back on when the patient has high pulmonary risk. A more accurate composite pulmonary risk assessment would lead to improved accuracy of risk-based pulmonary disease detection. Further, because pulmonary disease detection can be turned on when high pulmonary risk is indicated, using the pulmonary risk assessment can help save battery power and conserve computing resources, thereby improving the functionality of the medical-device system.
These arguments are respectfully found not persuasive for at least two reasons.
First, a more accurate composite risk assessment is an insufficient reason to convey subject matter eligibility. Examiner has concluded that the judicial exception in the inventions of claims 1, 5-6, 8-13, and 16-20 is the computation of a pulmonary risk score. That such a score results in an improvement in the assessment of pulmonary risk that is sufficient to confer patentability is at odds at least in the conclusion reached in Parker v. Flook, 437 U.S. 584, 598, n. 18 (“…[the patentee’s] process patent rests solely on the claim that his mathematical algorithm, when related to a computer program, will improve the existing process for updating alarm units. Very simply, our holding today is that a claim for an improved method of calculation, even when tied to a specific end use, is unpatentable subject matter under § 101”) (emphasis added). Here, Applicants have failed to identify a technological problem that the claimed invention addresses that is of the type that has been found eligible by the courts.
In reviewing the pertinent precedent out of the Federal Circuit, the “technological improvement” argument has been successful in very different scenarios than at issue here. In DDR Holdings, LLC v. Hotels.com, L.P., 773 F.3d 1245 (Fed. Cir. 2014), the problem was unique to e-commerce in that it concerned the tendency for users to “click-away” from a merchant’s site. The eligible patent solved this problem by modifying the website interface to retain consumers. In BASCOM Global Internet Services, Inc. v. AT&T Mobility LLC, 827 F.3d 1341 (Fed. Cir. 2016), the problem was unique to content filtering over the internet and allowed for customizable filtering at a specific network location, such as a particular ISP, that would be less susceptible to circumvention by a user. By making the filter more dynamic and efficient, the software-based invention improved the performance of the computer itself. In Data Engine Technologies LLC v. Google LLC, 906 F.3d 999 (Fed. Cir. 2018), the problem was unique to navigation through prior art three-dimensional or multipage electronic spreadsheets. The eligible patent solved the problem in a particular way by providing an intuitive, user-friendly interface with familiar notebook tabs for navigating the three-dimensional worksheet environment.
As summarized above, Applicants argue that the technology improvement is in the field of computerized, device-based patient monitoring and pulmonary risk management. A more accurate composite pulmonary risk assessment, Applicants further argue, would lead to improved accuracy of risk-based pulmonary disease detection. The problem with this framing of the issue is that the benefits of the composite pulmonary indicator are independent of any technological use case. In other words, whether or not the pulmonary risk score was used in a patient monitor, its salient benefit would still be the same. A doctor could independently calculate this score based on medical records alone and the result would still be the same—an assessment of medical risk for the patient. For at least this reason, the claims cannot be said to be a technological improvement of the type that would confer patent eligibility.
Second, while there are several examples offered by Applicants as to why the claimed invention is a technological improvement, none of these benefits are commensurate with the scope of claims 1, 5-6, 8-13, and 16-20. For example, Applicants argue that the device may be turned off or on depending on the patient risk. Setting aside the question of how the patient risk can be calculated by the monitor when it is turned off, these features are nowhere to be found in the text of claims 1 and 13. Claim 1 is illustrative of this point. It comprises two circuits. One to calculate the risk and another to output it. There is little in the text of the claims that would realize the advantages proposed by Applicants. Applicants further argue that the score uses a unique combination of primary and secondary risk scores each computed using distinct signal metrics. But close scrutiny of the claims finds little “distinct” or “unique” about the metrics or risks scores claimed here. Given that they are only tethered to “physiological information,” the metrics embrace almost any information related to patient health. Similarly, it is difficult to understand how these scores may be unique without any distinguishing text. In short, the claims are insufficiently aligned with the features pointed out in Applicants’ remarks. For at least this reason, the arguments are found not persuasive to finding patent eligibility. Accordingly, the rejection of claims 1, 5-6, 8-13, and 16-20 under Section 101 are maintained.
As to the art rejections, Applicants’ arguments are moot in view of the newly applied rejections.
