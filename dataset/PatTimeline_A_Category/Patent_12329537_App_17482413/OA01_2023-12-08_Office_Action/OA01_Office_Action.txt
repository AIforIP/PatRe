Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 112
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 112(b):
(b)  CONCLUSION.—The specification shall conclude with one or more claims particularly pointing out and distinctly claiming the subject matter which the inventor or a joint inventor regards as the invention.
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 112 (pre-AIA ), second paragraph:
The specification shall conclude with one or more claims particularly pointing out and distinctly claiming the subject matter which the applicant regards as his invention.
Claim 1-10 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 112(b) or 35 U.S.C. 112 (pre-AIA ), second paragraph, as being indefinite for failing to particularly point out and distinctly claim the subject matter which the inventor or a joint inventor (or for applications subject to pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 112, the applicant), regards as the invention.
Regarding claim 1, at lines 7-8, the limitations “an epoch signal containing 1-second and 10% overlapping epochs” render the claim indefinite because it is unclear whether the duration “1-second” refers to the value or duration of the epoch and if so, it is further unclear how such value or duration would overlap with another value or duration; for examination purposes, the Office has construed the language to mean --an epoch containing samples recorded for 1 second, wherein the samples of adjacent epochs overlap by 10%--; at line 23, the limitations “accumulated squared-differences among different epochs” render the claim indefinite because it is unclear whether each epoch of the plurality of epochs include squared-differences of the 1-second of samples contained in each epoch; at line 25, the limitations “the epochs accumulated” lack sufficient antecedent basis.
Regarding claim 2, at line 9, the limitation “the power-line interference” lacks sufficient antecedent basis.
Regarding claim 3, at line 12, the limitations “a second filter”  because there is no prior recitation of a first filter in claim 1 from which the claim depends; at lines 2-4, the limitations “a filtering frequency comparable to an average frequency of a wide electroencephalography frequency region in a range of 0-250 Hz of the incoming signal from the animal subject” render the claim indefinite because it is unclear whether the claimed “filtering frequency” is “in a range of 0-250 Hz of the incoming signal from the animal subject”.
Regarding claim 4, at line 18, the limitations “the second filter” lack sufficient antecedent basis.
Regarding claim 5, at line 21-23, the sentence “It is optimally designed to consume lower resources and the smallest possible size of memory unit (RAM).” renders the claim indefinite because the scope cannot be determined. The specification fails to provide a standard by which to judge what is construed as “less resources” and “smallest possible size of memory unit”. The claim is also indefinite because the limitation “It” is unclear as to what “it” is referring to. The claim is also indefinite because it is unclear whether the claim requires just a memory or a random-access memory (RAM). The claim is also indefinite because the claim should only read as one sentence and each element should be separated by a line indentation (see MPEP 608.01(i) part (i)).
Regarding claim 5, line 22, the term “optimally” is a relative term which renders the claim indefinite. The term “optimally” is not defined by the claim, the specification does not provide a standard for ascertaining the requisite degree, and one of ordinary skill in the art would not be reasonably apprised of the scope of the invention. It has been rendered indefinite by the use of the term optimally.
Regarding claim 6, at line 26, the limitations “a multiplier to square the differences” render the claim indefinite; it is unclear as to which differences the claim refers. The term “difference” in mathematics is defined as “the degree or amount by which things differ in quantity or measure.” The claim saliently fails to disclose the “things” whose difference is referenced in the claim.
Regarding claim 7, at lines 31-32, the limitation “the squared deviation” renders the claim indefinite because it is unclear as to which squared deviation the claim refers. The claim saliently fails to disclose the “things” whose deviation is referenced in the claim.
Regarding claim 8, at line 6, the limitation “for being fed to the subsequent predictor” renders the claim indefinite because it is unclear as to what “thing” is being fed to the predictor in the claim.
Regarding claim 9, at lines 8-9, recites the limitation the level of anesthesia.  There is insufficient antecedent basis for this limitation in the claim.

Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 101
35 U.S.C. 101 reads as follows:
Whoever invents or discovers any new and useful process, machine, manufacture, or composition of matter, or any new and useful improvement thereof, may obtain a patent therefor, subject to the conditions and requirements of this title.
Claims 1-10 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 101 because the claimed invention is directed to an abstract idea without significantly more.
Step 1 of the subject matter eligibility test (see MPEP 2106.03):
Claims 1-9 are directed to a “machine”, which describes one of the four statutory categories of patentable subject matter.
Claim 10 is directed to a “method”, which describes one of the four statutory categories of patentable subject matter.
Step 2A of the subject matter eligibility test (see MPEP 2106.04):
Prong 1 – Claim 1 recites the abstract ideas of mathematical process and mental processes as follows:
“Derivative calculator” and “Feature extractor” which comprises steps of “calculating a mean of accumulated squared-differences among different epochs”. The determining step can be practically performed by a mathematical process. There is nothing to suggest an undue level of complexity in a mean of accumulated squared-differences.
“Variance calculator” and “Feature extractor” which comprises steps of “determining a mean of the accumulated epochs”,  “calculating a squared deviation” and “determining square root of an average squared deviation”. The determining step can be practically performed in the human mind by a mathematical process, with the aid of a pen and paper. There is nothing to suggest an undue level of complexity in a squared deviation.
“Classifier” which comprises steps of “feature mapping to set a classification boundary”. The determining step can be practically performed in the human mind by a mathematical process, with the aid of a pen and paper.
“Predictor” which comprises steps of “determining level of anesthesia of the animal subject in terms of the constrained decision value based on the animal subject's real-time electroencephalogram”; The determining step can be practically performed by a mathematical process or with the aid of a generic computer to perform the steps. For example, if a user were to obtain the electroencephalography (EEG) signal of the animal subject, said user would be able to filter the signal (generic pre-solution activity using a generic computer), divide the signal into epochs (generic pre-solution activity using a generic computer), extract features from the epochs (generic mathematical processes using a generic computer), determine a classification boundary from the features (human mind/generic computer), and classify epochs between 0 and 1 to determine level of anesthesia of an animal subject (human mind). There is nothing to suggest an undue level of complexity in the level of anesthesia of the animal subject.
Prong 1 – Claim 10 recites the abstract ideas of mathematical processes and mental processes as follows:
“determine a classification boundary”; The determining step can be practically performed in the human mind, with the aid of a pen and paper, by a mathematical process or with the aid of a generic computer to perform the steps. There is nothing to suggest an undue level of complexity in a classification boundary.
“determine a sigmoid function”; The determining step can be practically by a mathematical process or with the aid of a generic computer to perform the steps. There is nothing to suggest an undue level of complexity in a sigmoid function.
“predict a likelihood of a successive awake or anesthetized epoch”; The determining step can be practically performed in the human mind, with the aid of a pen and paper, by a mathematical process or with the aid of a generic computer to perform the steps. There is nothing to suggest an undue level of complexity in a likelihood of a successive awake or anesthetized epoch.
Prong 2 – Claims 1 and 10 do not include additional elements that integrate the mental process or the mathematical process into a practical application. Therefore, the claims are “directed to” the mental process or mathematical process.
The additional elements comprise a signal pre-processor, epoch generator, multiplexer, and steps for outputting EEG data. These additional elements merely add insignificant extra-solution activity or pre-solution activity of obtaining data from an object.
As a whole, the additional elements serve to gather and feed information to the abstract idea. There is no practical application because the abstract idea is not applied, relied on, or used in a meaningful way. No improvement to the technology is evident, and the determined level of anesthesia of the animal subject is not displayed in any way such that a diagnostic benefit is realized. Therefore, the additional elements, alone or in combination, do not integrate the abstract idea into a practical application.
Step 2B of the subject matter eligibility test (see MPEP 2106.05):
Claims 1 and 10 do not include additional elements, alone or in combination, that are sufficient to amount to significantly more than the judicial exception for the same reasons as listed above. The additional limitations generically recite sending or receiving EEG data, see for example, Rothman (US20140289172A1) (P.0003-P.0005) for sending or receiving EEG data, Delorme (EEGLAB) (2.1.1) for signal pre-processor, epoch generator, and Matlab (Mux) for multiplexer.
Dependent claims 2-9, merely further define the  additional elements and are thus directed to an abstract idea for similar reasons as above:

Claim 9: constrain an output value as a decision score between 0 and 1

Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 103
In the event the determination of the status of the application as subject to AIA  35 U.S.C. 102 and 103 (or as subject to pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 102 and 103) is incorrect, any correction of the statutory basis (i.e., changing from AIA  to pre-AIA ) for the rejection will not be considered a new ground of rejection if the prior art relied upon, and the rationale supporting the rejection, would be the same under either status.
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 103 which forms the basis for all obviousness rejections set forth in this Office action:
A patent for a claimed invention may not be obtained, notwithstanding that the claimed invention is not identically disclosed as set forth in section 102, if the differences between the claimed invention and the prior art are such that the claimed invention as a whole would have been obvious before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which the claimed invention pertains. Patentability shall not be negated by the manner in which the invention was made.
Claim 10 is rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Nagaraj (US20200253544A1) in view of Nierenberg (US8666484B2) and in further view of Abele (WO-2004005895-A1).
Regarding claim 10, Nagaraj discloses a method for determining depth of anesthesia of an animal subject from a transient behavior of an electroencephalography using machine learning techniques thereof (see P.0004), the method comprising:
recording electroencephalographic (EEG) signal of the animal subject (The method can include receiving an electroencephalography (EEG) signal from a sensor electrode attached to the patient, see P.0007) and filtering thereof within a relatively lower frequency band to remove unwanted signals and noise (the band pass filter may have a frequency range of 0.5-25 Hz., see P.0051, 0108 & 0130);
down-sampling the filtered EEG signal to at least ten times for reducing data size and accelerating subsequent processing without losing essential features of the EEG for subsequent classification (the signal is sampled at 5 kHz (P.0041), and it is mentioned that “the collection and processing of EEG data can be performed by the controller 1400”(P.0041). That same controller 1400 may down sample the EEG signals to 250 Hz (P.0051), resulting in a down-sampling of twenty times);
generating epochs from the down-sampled EEG signals comprising segmenting each EEG channel with a shortened signal time (see P.0052);
extracting two features from each epoch selected from derivative and variance thereof (interpreted as the plain meaning as in something that is derived from something else: “several features derived from the frontal EEG, commonly reported for EEG and sedation level analysis, can be used as input to a logistic regression for automatically predicting a patient’s sedation level” see P.0035, P.0054, and Table 1);
followed by using an output of the mapping to determine a sigmoid function as a decision boundary between awake and anesthetized states (see P.0057):
accumulating the output value of consecutive awake and anesthetized epochs (“A continuous sedation level or patient state index can be obtained by developing a binary classifier, which is trained only on awake and sedated epochs, to assign a probability score to all EEG epochs”, in having a continuous sedation level, it is implied that the epochs are consecutive see P.0080-0083, respectively, and constraining each of the output value between 0 and 1 in order to predict a likelihood of a successive awake or anesthetized epoch (see P.0058).
Nagaraj fails to teach
overlapping one EEG signal with a preceding EEG signal thereof.
mapping the derivative and variance features to determine a classification boundary
if the output value is lower than 0.5, the epoch is classified as awake; otherwise, the epoch is classified as anesthetized
However, It would have been prima facie obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to modify the probability score of Nagaraj to indicate that if the output value is lower than 0.5, the epoch is classified as awake, because a score of 0.6 equates to a 60% chance of sedation as described in P.0081, a score of less than 0.5 would equate to a less than 50% chance of sedation, meaning a more than 50% chance of being awake. “The approach is beneficial because it can reduce the score “annotation-noise” due to interobserver variability during sedation level assessment” (P.0080 Nagaraj).
Nierenberg is pertinent prior art as it is a method for processing EEG data into overlapping signals. Nierenberg teaches overlapping one EEG signal with a preceding EEG signal thereof (see Col. 7, lines 54-67 and Col. 8, lines 1-5)
It would have been prima facie obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to modify the EEG signals of Nagaraj to include overlapping as taught by Nierenberg to produce a “continuous processed EEG report” that is “not missing timeframes from stitching or creating discontinuities” (see Nierenberg Col. 7, lines 41-47).
However, the combination of Nagaraj and Nierenberg fails to teach:
mapping the derivative and variance features to determine a classification boundary
Abele is pertinent prior art as it is a system that classifies patient data and outputs diagnostic results. Abele teaches mapping the derivative and variance features to determine a classification boundary (statistical classification techniques including a method based on maximal variance, and non-statistically-derived information P.0026 – P.0028).
It would have been prima facie obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to modify the classification of Nagaraj and Nierenberg to include a method based on variance and derived information as described in Abele to achieve “increased diagnostic sensitivity and specificity by combining a plurality of statistical classification techniques” (Abele P.0018).
