All Claims: “And/Or”
The applicant notes that the office action contended that each use of “and/or” in the claims renders all claims indefinite because it is allegedly “unclear whether 1) just one […] or 2) both/all listed options are required. In particular, the office maintains that, with the use of “and/or” it is unclear: 1) for claims 1 and 24, “just one of the mean curvatures need to be calculated” or “both mean curvatures need to be calculated; 2) for claims 3, 8, 16, 25, 31 and 32, the claim “only requires characterizing whether the patient has multiple sclerosis”, “only requires characterizing a type of multiple sclerosis” or requires “both characterizing whether the patient has multiple sclerosis and the type of multiple sclerosis; 3) for claims 3, 4, 8, 25, 26, 29, 31, and 32 “just one mean curvature/rate of change is required to characterize the patient” or “both mean curvatures/rates of chance are required to characterize the patient”; 4) for claim 6 and 27, “just one of the recited ranges is required” or “all of the recited ranges are required”; 5) for claims 7, 9 22 and 28, “just one of the recited rates of change is required” or both of the recited rates of change are required.
But as the Board has repeatedly held, “and/or” has a clear meaning: see Ex parte Gross, Appeal No. 2011-004811, slip op. at page 4 (PTAB Jan. 3, 2014); Ex parte Gamooll, Appeal No. 2011-006816, (PTAB Aug. 7, 2013). The use of “and/or” in these claims is thus, clear and means that the claims are broad enough to cover using one or multiple ones of the recited options linked by “and/or” i.e. breath is not indefiniteness (see In re Gardner, 427 F.2d 786, 788 (CCPA 1970).
Independent claims 1 and 24: Use of Mean Curvature:
The Office action also implies that claims 1 and 24 are unclear because the recite “determining” or processor(s) configured to determine “a mean curvature of at least a region of a surface of the dorsal portion and/or a mean curvature of at least a region of a surface of the ventral portion”, but do not specify how the mean curvature calculations are used such that it is allegedly “unclear what the mean curvature calculations are used for”. The office this “recommend amending the independent claims such that is it clear what the mean curvature is used for within the context of the method and system to analyze the one or more sections of a central nervous system of a patient”.
However, the applicant argues that the office makes the breath of claims 1 and 24 –which, without specifying a particular use of the mean curvatures, broadly permit many different uses of thereof – with indefiniteness. “Breadth is not indefiniteness” and the office has not established that a POSITA would have been unable to ascertain the scope of the actually-recited limitations with reasonable certainty (see Gardner, 427 F.2d at page 788. Action at pages 3-4, e.g., Ex parte Desabhatla, Appeal No. 2017-005957, slip op. at pages 2-3 (PTAB Feb. 8, 2018). The claims are thus clear.
Claims 3-5, 8-9, 11, 13-14, 16-17, 23, 25-26, 29, and 31-32: “At least in part”:
The Office contends that the recitation of basing a characterization or calculation “at least in part” recited parameters in claims 3, 8, 9, 11, 13, 16, 23, 25, 29, 31 and 32 “makes it unclear whether” the recited parameters “are actually required in order to” do the characterization or calculation and “what other factors can be included or excluded” (see Office action pages 4-5 and 7-8. But, as the Board has held, making a determination “based at least in part” on a recited parameter “in not indefinite” –broadly, if a parameter is accounted for in a determination, the determination can be based at least in part on the parameter, including if other unrecited parameters are also accounted for. (See e.g., Desabhatla, Appeal No. 2017-005957, slip op. at pages 2-3). Such breadth is not indefiniteness.
Claims 3-6, 8, 11, 13-14, 16-17, 25-27, 29, 31-32: Conditional Determinations:
The Office contends that the claims reciting characterizing or processor(s) configured to characterize “whether the patient has multiple sclerosis and/or a type of multiple sclerosis” is unclear because “it is unclear what happens within the method when the patient does not have multiple sclerosis (i.e. or a type of multiple sclerosis) (see Office action at page 5). The Office also contends that claims 6 and 27 are unclear because “it is unclear what the method does when the mean curvature of the region of the surface of the ventral portion of the first section is not within any of the recited ranges” (see Office action at page 6.
The applicant notes that the examiner impermissibly conflates breadth with indefiniteness (Gardner, 427 F.2d at 788). For example, if the characterization of “whether the patient has multiple sclerosis, then that is the characterization in the recited step—if subsequent steps are not recited, they broadly can (but need not) be performed. Likewise, for claims 6 and 27, if the determination that the mean curvature is not within one of the recited ranges, then that is the determination in the recited step—if subsequent steps are not recited, they broadly can (but need not) be performed. This does not render the claims indefinite (see e.g., Desabhatla, Appeal No, 2017-005957, slip op. at pages 2-3.
Claims 5, 11, 14, 17, 21 and 25: Patient Ethnicity:
The Office contends that claims 5, 14, 17 and 21 are unclear for reciting the ethnicity of the patient—that “the patient is White” or “the patient is Black”—because “it is unclear what the race/ethnicity of the patient is used for”, “what impact the race/ethnicity of the patient has on the characterizing” and thus, “why an ethnicity/race of the patient is mentioned within the method claim” (see Action at page 6). And the Office similarly contends that claims 11 and 25 are indefinite for reciting that the characterization is based at least in part on “an ethnicity of the patient because “it is unclear what impact the race/ethnicity of the patient has on the characterizing” (See Action at page 7).
The applicant argues that, claims 5, 14, 17 and 21 merely specify the ethnicity of the patient analyzed in the claimed methods, and a POSITS would have had no issue determining what it means for a patient to be “White” or “Black” –this is enough for definiteness, which merely requires a POSITA to be able to determine “the scope of the invention with reasonable certainty” (see Nautilus, Inc. v. Biosig Instruments, Inc., 572 U.S. 898,899 (2014)). And, for all claims, section 112(b) does not require a specification of “what the race/ethnicity of the patient is used for” or the impact thereof “on the characterizing”---since, as noted above, that concerns breadth, not indefiniteness—let alone an explanation for “why” a claim recites particular features (see Gardner, 427 F.2d at page 788; Desabhatla, Appeal No. 2017-005957, slip op. at pages 2-3). These claims are thus also clear.
The examiner respectfully agrees that the rejections of the claims in each of sections A-E noted above are simply broad in scope and are not indefinite. Therefore, the rejection of claim 1-32 under 35 U.S.C. 112(b) in the non-final rejection of 07/15/2024 has been withdrawn.

Step 2A: The claims are not directed to a judicial exception:
The applicant argues that the claims are not directed to a judicial exception and thus are patent-eligible. The Office contends that under Step 2A, Prong One of the subject-matter-eligibility analysis (MPEP 2106.04.II.A.1), independent claims 1 and 24 each “fall […] within the ‘mental processes’ grouping of abstract ideas” (see Action at page 9). This is because, according to the Office, the recited “segmenting each of the 3D representation(s) into ventral and dorsal portions’ can be achieved by a user viewing a 3D image and selecting/deciding which section represent the ventral and dorsal portion via a user interface” and “determining the mean curvature can be performed in the human mind by performing a calculation of an average […] by: 1) mental math; 2) pen/paper. The Office further contends that under Step 2A, Prong Two of the subject matter eligibility analysis (MPEP 2106.04.II.A.2), “the claims do not recite any additional elements to integrate the judicial exception into a practical application because the claim as written does not include elements to 1) improve the functioning of a computer […] 2) effect a particular treatment or prophylaxis […] 3) use a particular machine […] 4) use the judicial exceptions in a meaningful way beyond generally linking the use to a particular technological environment” (See Office action pages 9-10. That is incorrect.
1. Prong one: The claims do not recite a mental process:
First, the applicant argues that the Office has not established that the recited “segmentation of each of the 3D representation(s) of the data “into ventral and dorsal portions” and the “determination of a mean curvature of at least a region of a surface of the dorsal portion and/or a mean curvature of at least a region of a surface of the ventral portion” could “as a practical matter, be performed entirely in a human’s mind” CyberSource Corp. v. Retail Decisions, Inc., 654 F.3d 1366, 1376 (Fed. Cir. 2011). Notably, not even the Office’s explanation sets forth a way in which the recited steps could “be performed entirely in a human’s mind”: the Office’s explanation invokes reliance on a “user interface (i.e. touchscreen, cursor, buttons, and the like)” to segment “the ventral and dorsal portions” of the 3D representation of a section of a patient’s central nervous system, and as the Board has held a “user’s interaction with a GUI […] is not a mental process” (see Action at page 9; CyberSource, 654 F.3d at 1376; Ex parte Jones, Appeal No. 2019-004559, slip op at pages 10-11 (PTAB Jan. 1, 2021)). The Office thus, has not carried its burden to show that the claimed steps could practically be performed entirely in the human mind.
Nor could the Office make the requisite showing. In addressing the recited segmentation and mean-curvature-calculation separately, the Office “does not appreciate how the limitations […] operate as a whole” (see Jones, Appeal No. 2019-004559, slip op. at pages 11-12. The recited segmentation of a 3D representation “into ventral and dorsal portions” is a precursor to the mean-curvature calculation, as the calculation is of the “mean curvature of at least a region of a surface of the dorsal portion” and/or “of the ventral portion” from the segmentation. Thus, input data---a 3D representation of a section of a central nervous system---is segmented and the calculation is performed for one of both of the segmented portions. This is a patent-eligible “specific manner” of performing an analysis rather than a “conventional” or “generic” image analysis, akin to other cases that involved “the manipulation of […] data structures” and “the output of a modified computer structure”, which “could not, as a practical matter, be performed entirely in a human’s mind” (see CyberSource, 654 F.3d at page 1376; Jones, Appeal No. 2019-0045589, slip op. at page 11-12 (quoting Core Wireless Licensing S.A.R.L. v. LG Elecs., Inc., 880 F.3d 1356, 1363 (Fed. Cir. 2018))). Being “mindful not to exaggerate the reasonable capacity of the human mind to perform concepts as a practical matter”, segmentation of data as complex as a 3D representation of a section of a central nervous system---which “can comprise data that represents the geometry of the section” like “a polyhedron whose surface is defined by a plurality of polygons”---to perform segment-specific calculations thereon exceeds what “the human mind is […] equipped to detect” (see Ex parte Loose, Appeal No. 2022-001026, slip op. at 15-17 (PTAB Jun. 6, 2022) (citing SRI Int’l, Inc. v. Cisco Systems, Inc., 930 F.3d 1295, 1304 (Fed. Cir. 2019)); Specification at paragraphs [0047]-[0048]).
Beyond that, the Office “exaggerate(s) the reasonable capacity of the human mind” in contending that the “mean curvature of at least a region of a surface” of a segment of the 3D representation “can be performed in the human mind” via “mental math” or “pen/paper”, especially for a 3D representation of a structure as complex as a section of a central nervous system (see Action at page 9; Loose, Appeal No. 2022-001026, slip op. at page 15). The human mind is not practically equipped to determine curvature at one location—let alone multiple locations, to take a mean thereof---of such a surface. As an illustration of the types of calculations that may be involved, when the 3D representation comprises a polyhedron, “curvature of each of the region’s polygonal faces” can be determined by determining “a curvature […] for each vertex […] thereof using, for example, paraboloid fitting (e.g., with least squared residuals) or quadric fitting of a curved surface”, and the curvatures of the polygonal faces can be averaged (see specification at [0052]). As another illustration “curvature calculations can also be based on Gaussian curvature and the Gauss-Bonnet theorem of angle deficit”. These are not the type of calculations performable entirely within the human mind. Underscoring this, the specification does not suggest that surface curvature can be determined mentally and instead highlights the “Materialize Mimics Curvature Analysis tool” as an “illustrative program for determining a region’s mean curvature.
The examiner respectfully agrees and acknowledges that the steps recited in the claims could not be performed entirely in a human’s mind. The examiner recognizes that the rejection of claim 1 exaggerates the reasonable capacity of the human mind by contending that the mean curvature of at least a region of a surface of a segment of the 3D representation “can be performed in the human mind” via “mental math” or “pen/paper”, especially for a 3D representation of a structure as complex as a section of a central nervous system.
2. Prong Two: The Claimed Approach is an Improvement to Technology:
Second, the claims as a whole recite an approach that “improves another technology or technical field”, demonstrating that—to the extend they recite a judicial exception (which they no not)—they “integrate […] the exception into a practical application” and are thus not “directed to” a judicial exception (see MPEP 2106.04(d), 2106.04(d)(1)). As the specification explains, segmenting “each of the 3D representation(s) […] into ventral and dorsal portions” and performing a “textural analysis” that includes “determining a mean curvature of at least a region of the surface of the section” can “yield information that facilitates diagnosis of neurological disorders and the assessment of the progression thereof for a wide range of patient” (see specification at [0005]-[0006], [0065]. The recited segmentation of a 3D representation into ventral and dorsal portions before performing the mean-curvature calculation for at least one of the portions is thus an improvement in that it can “provide reliable metrics” that “permit more personalized assessments than prior metrics” that did not rely on such segmentations. This is supported by, for example, the studies set forth in Examples 1 and 2 of the specification.
As such, the claims are not directed to a judicial exception.
The examiner respectfully agrees and acknowledges that the claims as a whole recite an approach that “improves another technology or technical field”, demonstrating that—to the extend they recite a judicial exception (which they do not)—they “integrate […] the exception into a practical application” and are thus not “directed to” a judicial exception. The recited segmentation of a 3D representations into ventral and dorsal portions before performing the mean curvature calculation for at least one of the portions is thus an improvement in that is can “provide reliable metrics” that “permit more personalized assessments than prior metrics” that did not rely on such segmentation.
Step 2B: The claims amount to significantly more:
Furthermore, the claims “amount to significantly more than [a] judicial exception itself” because, as noted in Section V.A.2, the claimed segmentation of a 3D representation of a section of a central nervous system into ventral and dorsal portions to perform a calculation on at least one of the segmented portions is an improvement over a generic image analysis, as it “provide(s) reliable metrics” that “permit more personalized assessments than prior metrics” (see specification paragraphs [0005]-[0006], [0065], and MPEP 2106.05, 2106.05(a).
The examiner respectfully agrees and acknowledges that the claims amount to significantly more than a judicial exception itself because, as noted in Section V.A.2, the claimed segmentation of a 3D representation of a section of a central nervous system into ventral and dorsal portions to perform a calculation on at least one of the segmented portions is an improvement over a generic image analysis because it provides reliable metrics that permit more personalized assessments than prior metrics.
Therefore, the rejection of claims 1-32 under 35 U.S.C. 101 in the non-final rejection of 07/15/2024 has been withdrawn.
Reasons for Allowance
The following is the examiner’s statement of reasons for allowance:
The primary reason for allowance of the claims are the limitations in claims 1, 3, 24 and 25:
Specifically, the claims recite “A method of analyzing one or more sections of a central nervous system of a patient, the method comprising: […] segmenting each of the 3D representation(s) into ventral and dorsal portions; and for at least one of the 3D representation(s), determining a mean curvature of at least a region of a surface of the dorsal portion and/or a mean curvature of at least a region of a surface of the ventral portion“ (Claim 1); “[…] comprising characterizing whether the patient has multiple sclerosis and/or a type of multiple sclerosis in the patient based at least in part on the mean curvature of the region of the surface of the dorsal portion of the first section and/or the mean curvature of the region of the surface of the ventral portion of the first section” (Claim 3); “A system for analyzing one or more sections of a central nervous system of a patient, the system comprising one or more processors configured to: […] segment each of the 3D representation(s) into ventral and dorsal portions; and for at least one of the 3D representation(s), determine a mean curvature of at least a region of a surface of the dorsal portion and/or a mean curvature of at least a region of a surface of the ventral portion […]” (Claim 24); “[…] the processor(s) are configured to characterize whether the patient has multiple sclerosis and/or a type of multiple sclerosis in the patient based at least in part on an ethnicity of the patient and: the mean curvature of the region of the surface of the dorsal portion of the first section; and/or the mean curvature of the region of the surface of the ventral portion of the first section” (Claim 25).
During the examiner’s search the following prior art references were found which are pertinent to the claimed invention: Butson et al. US 2013/0150922 A1 “Butson” and Hubbard US 2012/0277572 A1 “Hubbard”.
Butson is pertinent to the claimed invention because it discloses “One embodiment provided a computer-implemented method that includes storing a volume of tissue activation (VTA) data structure that is derived from analysis of a plurality of patients. Patient data is received for a given patient, the patient data representing an assessment of a patient condition. The VTA data structure is evaluated relative to the patient data to determine a target VTA for achieving a desired therapeutic effect for the given patient” [Abstract]; “The invention relates generally to systems and methods for determining a target volume of tissue activation (VTA) for stimulation of a patient’s tissue, such as neural tissue” [0038]; “FIG. 1 depicts an example of a system 10 that can be employed to determine a target VTA. The system 10 is shown as including a computer 12 that employs data and program methods to determine a target VTA 14 for a given patient according to an aspect of the invention” [0042]; “The computer 12 includes a processor 16 that executes instructions programmed for performing the methods described herein” [0043]; “As used herein, a VTA represents an anatomical region of tissue, such as a three-dimensional volume of tissue that includes cells of the nervous system. For example, the tissue can be neural tissue in the brain or other part of the central nervous system, such as the spinal cord” [0044]. Therefore, Butson describes a system and method for analyzing one or more sections of a central nervous system of a patient (i.e. brain and other parts of the central nervous system such as the spinal cord), the system comprising one or more processors (i.e. processor 16);
“for each of the section(s), from data that includes one or more 3-dimensional (3D) representations of the section:” (Claims 1 and 24) (See [0044] above. Therefore, the target volume of tissue activation (TVA) represents data that includes one or more 3-dimensional (3D) representations of the section.);
“segment(ing) each of the 3D representation(s) into ventral and dorsal portions” (Claims 1 and 24) (“As an example, a plurality (e.g., two or more, such as five) of the top scoring electrode designs 204 for the target VTA 206 can be subjected to further analysis including scoring. For example, the electrode location and trajectory can be incrementally adjusted (e.g. relative to the geometric center of the target VTA) in the dorsal/ventral, anterior/posterior, and medial/lateral direction) and the resulting design VTAs 216 can be scored according to the sub-optimal electrode placements” [0116]. Therefore, the method involves segmenting each of the 3D representation(s) into ventral and dorsal portions for performing analysis/scoring.).
However, Butson does not teach “for at least one of the 3D representation(s), determine(ing) a mean curvature of at least: a region of a surface of the dorsal portion and/or a mean curvature of at least a region of a surface of the ventral portion, wherein each of the section(s) includes at least a portion of a brainstem of the patient” (Claims 1 and 24); “[…] comprising characterizing whether the patient has multiple sclerosis and/or a type of multiple sclerosis in the patient based at least in part on the mean curvature of the region of the surface of the dorsal portion of the first section and/or the mean curvature of the region of the surface of the ventral portion of the first section” (Claim 3) or “[…] the processor(s) are configured to characterize whether the patient has multiple sclerosis and/or a type of multiple sclerosis in the patient based at least in part on an ethnicity of the patient and: the mean curvature of the region of the surface of the dorsal portion of the first section; and/or the mean curvature of the region of the surface of the ventral portion of the first section” (Claim 25).
Hubbard is pertinent to the claimed invention because it discloses “wherein each of the section(s) includes at least a portion of a brainstem of the patient” (Claims 1 and 24) (“Finally, in step 400 the HDR graph is analyzed to determine if the HDR response is abnormal and vascular drainage insufficiency in the central nervous system can be identified based on the abnormal HDR response. […] Components of the central nervous system that can be diagnoses include the cerebral cortex, sub-cortical white matter, thalamus, basal ganglia, brainstem, and the spinal cord” [0073].).
However, Hubbard does not teach “for at least one of the 3D representation(s), determine(ing) a mean curvature of at least: a region of a surface of the dorsal portion and/or a mean curvature of at least a region of a surface of the ventral portion, wherein each of the section(s) includes at least a portion of a brainstem of the patient” (Claims 1 and 24), “[…] comprising characterizing whether the patient has multiple sclerosis and/or a type of multiple sclerosis in the patient based at least in part on the mean curvature of the region of the surface of the dorsal portion of the first section and/or the mean curvature of the region of the surface of the ventral portion of the first section” (Claim 3) or “[…] the processor(s) are configured to characterize whether the patient has multiple sclerosis and/or a type of multiple sclerosis in the patient based at least in part on an ethnicity of the patient and: the mean curvature of the region of the surface of the dorsal portion of the first section; and/or the mean curvature of the region of the surface of the ventral portion of the first section” (Claim 25).
Therefore, no prior art references have been found to teach the claimed limitations both alone or in combination with the other limitations of the claim(s).
Furthermore, the applicant’s arguments with respect to the rejection of claims under 35 U.S.C. 112(b) and 35 U.S.C. 101 are persuasive and have therefore been withdrawn.
Thus, claims 1-32 are allowable.
