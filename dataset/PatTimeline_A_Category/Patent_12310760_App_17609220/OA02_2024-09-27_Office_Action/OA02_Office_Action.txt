Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 112
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 112(b):
(b)  CONCLUSION.—The specification shall conclude with one or more claims particularly pointing out and distinctly claiming the subject matter which the inventor or a joint inventor regards as the invention.
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 112 (pre-AIA ), second paragraph:
The specification shall conclude with one or more claims particularly pointing out and distinctly claiming the subject matter which the applicant regards as his invention.
Claims 65-67 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 112(b) or 35 U.S.C. 112 (pre-AIA ), second paragraph, as being indefinite for failing to particularly point out and distinctly claim the subject matter which the inventor or a joint inventor (or for applications subject to pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 112, the applicant), regards as the invention.
Dependent claim 65 includes the recitations “compute a trend line for at least one extracted feature…” and “risk stratify the computed trend line based on a result of comparing  …” that infer a computer is part of the “feature analysis system”. As stated in MPEP 2181 II B, the specification must disclose an algorithm for performing the claimed specific computer function, or else the claim is indefinite under 35 USC 112(b), “to claim a means for performing a specific computer-implemented function and then only disclosing a general purpose computer as the structure designed to perform that function amounts to pure functional claiming”, a rejection under 35 USC 112(b) is appropriate when the specification fails to disclose an algorithm corresponding to the processor or system. In this case, the specification mentions that the comparison and/or risk predictive algorithms may be trained from a cohort population data set, recorded from similar subjects in similar environments and after similar medical procedures and that the algorithms may then be implemented on new test cases to stratify risk during the monitoring period so as to predict the likelihood that an individual (being monitored) may develop a cardiac disorder or disfunction, such as arrhythmia, heart block, or other conduction-related disorders or complications that can arise following a cardiac procedure (paragraph [0129] of the originally-filed specification (OFS)). That is, the disclosed algorithm is specific to a cardiac procedure and cardiac disorder or disfunction. The feature analysis system of claim 65 may be configured to analyze the extracted features and predict a risk of the individual developing a post-procedural dysfunction of the anatomical feature as a result of the medical procedure that is not limited to a cardiac procedure or to a risk of developing a cardiac disorder or disfunction.
Claims 66-67 are rejected because they depend from an indefinite claim and because the OFS does not disclose that an algorithm achieves the functions recited in those claims.
Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 101
35 U.S.C. 101 reads as follows:
Whoever invents or discovers any new and useful process, machine, manufacture, or composition of matter, or any new and useful improvement thereof, may obtain a patent therefor, subject to the conditions and requirements of this title.
Claims 60-62 and 65-67 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 101 because the claimed invention is directed to an abstract idea that can be performed in the human mind (including observation, evaluation/analyzing, judgement, opinion) without significantly more. The claims recite a processing system that analyzes physiological signals and extracts features from the physiological signals and a feature analysis system for analyzing the extracted features and predicting a risk of the individual developing a dysfunction, which can be done by a human observing the physiological signals, extracting features and evaluating/analyzing the features and predicting a risk (judging and making an informed opinion). This judicial exception is not integrated into a practical application because merely using a computer as a tool to perform an abstract idea and using sensors to monitor physiological signals (adding an insignificant extra-solution activity) does not rise to a practical application. The claims do not include additional elements that are sufficient to amount to significantly more than the judicial exception because use of a generic computer has been held as insufficient to  integrate a judicial exception into a practical application and mere data gathering to obtain input for a clinical test has been held to be an insignificant extra-solution activity (In re Grams, 888 F.2 835, 839-840 (Fed Cir. 1989) .

Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 103
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 103 which forms the basis for all obviousness rejections set forth in this Office action:
A patent for a claimed invention may not be obtained, notwithstanding that the claimed invention is not identically disclosed as set forth in section 102, if the differences between the claimed invention and the prior art are such that the claimed invention as a whole would have been obvious before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which the claimed invention pertains. Patentability shall not be negated by the manner in which the invention was made.
Claim 61 is rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Bashour as applied to claim 60 above, and further in view of US Patent Application Publication No. 2011/0224565 to Ong et al. (hereinafter referred to as “Ong”).
Bashour discloses the system of claim 60, but does not expressly disclose that the sensors comprise at least one of (i) sensor devices that are worn by the individual, and (ii) sensor patches that are coupled to the individual's skin. However, Ong, in a related art: method of predicting acute cardiopulmonary events and survivability of a patient, teaches that a patient-worn device may be used to continuously monitor and detect heart rhythm by means of sensing electrodes, or, traditional disposable ECG sensing electrodes may be attached to the patient’s skin (e.g., Fig. 34 and paragraphs [0052]-[0053] of Ong). Accordingly, one of ordinary skill in the art would have recognized the benefits of using either sensor devices worn by the patient (Fig. 34) or sensor patches coupled to the patient’s skin (traditional disposable ECG electrodes) in view of the teachings of Ong that both are capable of continuously monitoring a patient’s heart rhythm. Consequently, one of ordinary skill in the art would have modified the system of Bashour so that the ECG data relied upon is achieved using  patient-worn sensor device as taught by Ong to be a well-known expedient for monitoring a patient’s heart, and because the combination would have yielded predictable results.
Claims 65 and 67 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Bashour as applied to claim 60 above, and further in view of the article entitled “P-wave duration is a predictor for long-term mortality in post CABG patients” by Kimura-Medorima et al. (cited by Applicant and hereinafter referred to as “Kimura-Medorima”).
With respect to claim 65, Bashour discloses the system of claim 60, wherein the feature analysis system is configured to: render and display at least one of the time series data and the computed trend line for the at least one extracted feature on a display system (e.g., paragraphs [0062] of Bashour: system allows a user to display analysis results including P-waves; and [0068] of Bashour: AF prediction system can be incorporated into patient’s monitoring apparatus and the calculated AF index can be displayed on a screen), but does not expressly disclose that the feature analysis system is configured to: compute a trend line for at least one extracted feature of the plurality of extracted features based on time series data collected for the at least one extracted feature; compare the computed trend line with known trend lines for the at least one extracted feature as determined from a cohort patient population; risk stratify the computed trend line based on a result of comparing of the computed trend line with the known trend lines. However, Kimura-Medorima teaches, in a related art: P-wave duration is a predictor for long-term morality in post CABG patients, using a spline curve model to observe a linear association/trend line between P-wave duration/extracted feature and the incidence rate of long-term all-cause death as shown in Fig. 4) (e.g., page 6, first full paragraph of Kimura-Medorima); comparing the computed trend line of a patient with known trend lines for the at least extracted feature as determined from a cohort patient population to determine risk of death, atrial fibrillation, or heart failure hospitalization (e.g., page 2, paragraphs 1-3: “both cardiovascular and all-cause mortality were shown to be predictable by P-wave duration in a robust clinical cohort; data was collected from 520 patients who underwent CABG and page 4, paragraphs 2-3: Table 1 summarizes the cohort’s baseline characteristics; No difference was found when comparing survival curves to P-wave amplitude or P-wave dispersion (Fig. 3); and page 6, Discussion section: P-wave duration is a predictor of death, atrial fibrillation, or heart failure hospitalization in a large spectrum of patients including those post CABG); and risk stratifying the computed trend line based on the comparing step with known trend lines (e.g., page 6, Discussion section). One of ordinary skill in the art would have recognized the benefits of computing a trend line for at least one extracted feature of the physiological signal, comparing the computed trend line with known trend lines determined from a cohort population, and risk stratifying the computed trend line based on the results of comparing step in view of the teachings of Kimura-Medorima. Consequently, one of ordinary skill in the art would have modified the feature analysis system/classifier 30 of Bashour to compute a trend line for an extracted feature (e.g., P-wave), compare the computed trend line with known trend lines determined from a cohort patient population, and risk stratify the computed trend line based on the comparing step in view of the teachings of Kimura-Medorima that such was a known analysis of extracted features in the cardiac art, and because the combination would have yielded a predictable result. It would have been further obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art to render and display the computed trend line for at least one extracted feature on a display system, such as that shown in Fig. 4 of Kimura-Medorima in addition to the AF index as shown in Fig. 7 of Bashour.
As to claim 67, Bashour in view of Kimura-Medorima teaches the system of claim 65, wherein the feature analysis system is configured to: process time series data of at least one extracted feature of the plurality of extracted features using a neural network (e.g., paragraphs [0022]-[0023] of Bashour: feature extractor 20 computes features from detected/extracted R-waves, premature atrial contractions, P-waves, which include PAC activity and HRV parameters in time; [0027]: classification system 30 calculates an AF index representing the likelihood that a patient will experience postoperative atrial fibrillation using artificial neural networks; [0029]-[0036]: Receiver Operating Characteristics curves can be constructed for interpreting the AF index, classification system 30 can comprise an artificial neural network trained to distinguish between expected post-surgical outcomes according to the extracted features and processes the same, and training the neural network involves sets of ECG features (parameters related to PAC activity, HRV, and P-wave morphology) that are selected for AF prediction; and claim 19 of Bashour); and classify a risk profile of the at least one extracted feature based on processing results of the time series data applied to the neural network (e.g., paragraphs [0027] and [0068] of Bashour: classification system 30 calculates an AF index according an algorithm according to one of more extracted features (PAC, HRV, P-wave) and AF index represents an increased risk of atrial fibrillation).
Claim 66 is rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Bashour in view of Kimura-Medorima as applied to claim 65 above, and further in view of US Patent Application Publication No. 2016/0038093 to Sharma et al. (hereinafter referred to as “Sharma”).
Bashour in view of Kimura-Medorima teaches the system of claim 65, wherein the feature analysis system is configured to: risk stratify the computed trend line into one of a plurality of zones, and a high-risk zone; and generate an alert notification when an extracted feature is determined to enter into a high-risk zone (e.g., paragraph [0068] of Bashour: “When the AF index 352 exceeds a threshold value, an alert 354 can be displayed on the monitor to alert the medical staff of the increased risk of AF). The combination differs from the claimed invention in that an AF index, instead of a computed trend line, has a high-risk zone (above the threshold) and a low-risk zone (below the threshold). It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art to modify the combination of Bashour in view of Kimura-Medorima to divide the trend line to show low-risk and high-risk areas as such was a well-known engineering expedient in the medical art as taught by Bashour.
Sharma teaches, in a related art, determining prospective risk of heart failure hospitalization (HFH), that it would be desirable to have a method or system for predicting HFH risk that was able to present increased gradations of HFH risk than broad risk categories such as high risk and low risk (e.g., paragraph [0007]) and that an alert is generated when an extracted feature is determined to enter a high-risk zone (e.g., Figs. 6-7, critical indicator 140 and highlighted risk level 144 and paragraphs [0112]-[0116] of Sharma). Accordingly, one of ordinary skill in the art would have further modified Bashour in view of Kimura-Medorima so that it has at least one additional increasing risk zone in view of the teachings of Sharma.
