Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 103
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 103 which forms the basis for all obviousness rejections set forth in this Office action:
A patent for a claimed invention may not be obtained, notwithstanding that the claimed invention is not identically disclosed as set forth in section 102, if the differences between the claimed invention and the prior art are such that the claimed invention as a whole would have been obvious before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which the claimed invention pertains. Patentability shall not be negated by the manner in which the invention was made.
This application currently names joint inventors. In considering patentability of the claims the examiner presumes that the subject matter of the various claims was commonly owned as of the effective filing date of the claimed invention(s) absent any evidence to the contrary.  Applicant is advised of the obligation under 37 CFR 1.56 to point out the inventor and effective filing dates of each claim that was not commonly owned as of the effective filing date of the later invention in order for the examiner to consider the applicability of 35 U.S.C. 102(b)(2)(C) for any potential 35 U.S.C. 102(a)(2) prior art against the later invention.
Claims 11, 13-23, and 25-31 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Jain et al. (US 2016/0287128 A1) in view of Chahine et al. (US 2017/0056644 A1).
Regarding claims 11 and 29, Jain describes a smart garment for detecting physiological data of a user ([0062]), the smart garment comprising
a garment configured to be worn on an upper body of the user ([0069])
a plurality of textile-based ECG electrodes ([0062]), wherein each of the plurality of textile-based ECG electrodes comprises interlaced conductive fibers for conducting a plurality of ECG signals with respect to the upper body of the user;
wherein the plurality of textile-based ECG electrodes includes at least one ECG generator electrode for transmitting a generated ECG signal through the patient's upper body and at least one ECG receiver electrode spaced apart from the ECG generator electrode for sensing the generated ECG signal ([0087] - [0088])
a controller device electronically coupled to the plurality of textile-based ECG electrodes ([0087]) and configured to
generate the ECG signal ([0087])
sense the ECG signal for use as the physiological data ([0087] - [0088])
process the physiological data to determine a defined health event of the user ([0006])
based on the ECG signal, determine at least one of the plurality of textile-based ECG electrodes is at least partially out of contact with skin of the user and/or causes the ECG signal to be of unacceptable signal quality ([0087], [0039]),
deselect the at least one determined textile-based ECG electrode, and select at least another one of the plurality of textile-based ECG electrodes ([0025], [0039])
repeat said generate and said sense of the ECG signal using the at least another one of the plurality of textile-based ECG electrodes ([0039])
Regarding claims 11 and 29, Jain does not explicitly disclose wherein each of the plurality of textile-based electrodes comprises interlaced conductive fibers for conducting a plurality of signals with respect to the upper body of the user.  However, Chahine also describes a smart garment for detecting physiological data of a user ([0026]), including the use of textile-based electrodes comprising interlaced conductive fibers for conducting a plurality of signals with respect to the upper body of the user ([0109], [0206], [0182]).  As Chahine is also directed towards smart garments for detecting physiological data and is in a similar field of endeavor, it would have been obvious to a person having ordinary skill in the art at the time the invention was filed to incorporate interlaced conductive fibers in a manner similar to that described by Chahine when using the garment described by Jain, as doing so advantageously allows the resulting garment to better sense and transmit the physiological data throughout the garment.
Regarding claim 13, Jain describes wherein the controller detects whether one or more of the plurality of textile-based ECG electrodes are in acceptable contact with the skin of the user ([0039]).
Regarding claim 14, Jain describes comprising monitoring a signal quality of the ECG signal and repeating the process of deselecting the determined textile-based ECG electrode and selecting a different textile-based ECG electrode as a result of said monitoring ([0039]).
Regarding claim 15, Chahine describes wherein the interlaced conductive fibers comprise conductive yarns ([0104]).
Regarding claim 16, Chahine describes wherein the conductive yarns are knitted into the electrode ([0148]), and Jain describes wherein the electrode is an ECG electrode ([0062]).
Regarding claim 17, Jain describes wherein the controller determines a change in a desired location of one or more of the plurality of the textile-based ECG electrodes ([0025]).
Regarding claim 18, Jain describes a plurality of the ECG generator electrodes and plurality of the ECG receiver electrodes (figures 2, 13a, and 13b, for example).
Regarding claim 19, Jain describes wherein the ECG signal is generated using a given generator electrode of the plurality of textile-based ECG generator electrodes and the ECG signal is sensed using a given receiver electrode of the plurality of textile-based ECG receiver electrodes ([0087] - [0088]).
Regarding claim 20, Jain describes wherein the controller determines whether the ECG signal generated using the given generator electrode and sensed using the given receiver electrode does not satisfy a quality threshold, and selects an alternative generator electrode and/or an alternative receiver electrode ([0039]).
Regarding claim 21, Jain describes wherein the controller continues selecting alternative generator electrodes and/or alternative receiver electrodes to choose a generator electrode and receiver electrode pairing that satisfies a signal quality threshold ([0029], [0039]).
Regarding claim 22, Chahine describes wherein the garment includes a belt portion having at least one of the plurality of textile-based electrodes (figures 32-34, “band” configuration).
Regarding claim 23, Chahine describes wherein the interlaced conductive fibers are formed by weaving electrically conductive threads ([0097]).
Regarding claim 25, Chahine describes wherein the plurality of textile-based ECG electrodes are exposed on a first side and insulated on a second side ([0101]).
Regarding claim 26, Chahine describes wherein the interlaced conductive fibers are interlaced with non-conductive fibers of a body of the smart garment ([0108], [0110]).
Regarding claim 27, Chahine describes a conductive trace connected to the each of the plurality of textile-based electrodes, each of the conductive traces for connecting the plurality textile-based electrodes to the controller for sending and receiving one or more electronic signals using the plurality of textile-based electrodes ([0050]).
Regarding claim 28, Jain describes wherein the controller alternates different pairings chosen from the plurality of textile-based ECG electrodes as one or more selected pairs in order to determine a decided optimum signal from the one or more electronic signals received from the different pairings ([0021], [0023]).
Regarding claim 30, Jain describes wherein the controller processes the physiological data to determine a defined health event of the user ([0006], [0064]).
Claim 12 is rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Jain in view of Chahine, further in view of Biel et al. (US 2018/0168508 A1).
Regarding claim 12, Jain in view of Chahine suggests the smart garment of claim 11, but neither Jain nor Chahine explicitly disclose wherein the controller determines a percentage of contact surface area of one or more of the plurality of textile-based ECG electrodes with the skin of the user.  However, Biel also describes a garment configured to be worn on the upper body of a user and comprising a plurality of ECG electrodes ([0057], [0064]), including wherein a controller determines a percentage of contact surface area of electrodes with the skin of the user ([0124]).  As Biel is also directed towards a smart garment comprising sensors and is in a similar field of endeavor, it would have been obvious to a person having ordinary skill in the art at the time the invention was filed to quantify the surface area of electrode-skin contact, in a manner similar to that described by Biel, when using the smart garment suggested by Jain and Chahine, as doing so advantageously allows the resulting system to better monitor the electrode-skin contact and take corrective action when necessary.
Claim 24 is rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Jain in view of Chahine, further in view of Rapin et al. (US 2019/0223739 A1).
Regarding claim 24, Jain in view of Chahine suggests the smart garment of claim 11, but neither Jain nor Chahine explicitly disclose wherein the controller uses the physiological data to determine a quantity of the user selected from the group consisting of a PR interval, a QT interval, a corrected QT (QTc) interval, a PR axis, and a QRS axis.  However, Rapin also describes analyzing ECG data for monitoring a user’s health ([0002]), including using a controller to determine a PR interval, a QT interval, a corrected QT (QTc) interval, a PR axis, and/or a QRS axis of a user ([0020], [0060]).  As Rapin is also directed towards using ECG data for health analysis and is in a similar field of endeavor, it would have been obvious to a person having ordinary skill in the art at the time the invention was filed to use measures similar to those described by Rapin when analyzing the data generated by the smart garment suggested by Jain and Chahine, as doing so advantageously allows the resulting system to better characterize a patient’s health status and suggest corrective action when necessary.
