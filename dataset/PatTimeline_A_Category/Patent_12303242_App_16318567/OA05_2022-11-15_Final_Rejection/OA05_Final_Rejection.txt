EXAMINER’S ANSWER
This is in response to the appeal brief filed August 22, 2022.
(1) Grounds of Rejection to be Reviewed on Appeal
Every ground of rejection set forth in the Office action dated March 22, 2022 from which the appeal is taken is being maintained by the examiner except for the grounds of rejection (if any) listed under the subheading “WITHDRAWN REJECTIONS.”  New grounds of rejection (if any) are provided under the subheading “NEW GROUNDS OF REJECTION.”
(2) Response to Argument
Response to Appellant’s arguments regarding the rejection of claims 1-2, 5-10, 12, 14-21, and 23-25 under 35 U.S.C. 103 OVER Asada et al. (U.S. 2007/0055163 A1) (hereinafter – Asada) AND Nitzan (U.S. 2018/0325398 A1) (hereinafter -- Nitzan):
Appellant argues that the combination of Asada and Nitzan does not disclose mapping information derived from the AC component of a PPG signal to a blood pressure value  (Brief, pages 6-13 of 20).
As a preliminary matter, Examiner provides the following citation of the Advisory Action received May 18, 2022 to define an “AC component of a PPG signal:”
“… one of ordinary skill in the art would recognize that… a PPG wave is comprised of an AC (pulsatile) component and a DC (baseline) component.  This concept has been clarified previously in the Final Rejection mailed May 12, 2021 (see Response to Arguments), the Advisory Action mailed July 6, 2021 (see continuation of box 12), and the RCE Final Rejection mailed March 22, 2022 (see Response to Arguments).  Furthermore, such elementary knowledge is acknowledged in Nitzan in Paragraph 0037: “The AC component (also referred to herein as pulsatile component)... The DC component (also referred to herein as baseline component or trend).”
Regarding Brief, page 6 of 20:
Appellant initially defines an equivalency/substitution between the terms “amplitude of the AC component” and “information regarding a blood volume pulse from the AC component” (Brief, page 6 of 20).  Although Appellant argues that such a substitution is proper because Appellant’s Brief “… clearly identify the amplitude of the AC component is directly related to the magnitude of the directly related to the magnitude of the blood volume pulse…,” such a substitution is improper because it constitutes a substantial narrowing in scope of the claim language.
Claim 1 recites:
“a processing unit configured to:
extract information regarding a blood volume pulse from the AC component of the output signal of the PPG sensor during application of the force applied to the skin of the user,”
Appellant’s arguments hereafter will be considered with respect to the broadest reasonable interpretation of terms recited in the claims in light of the specification.
Regarding Brief, pages 7-9 of 20, “Asada”:
On Brief, page 7 of 20, Appellant alleges a differentiation of the claimed device from conventional techniques of constricting an artery and detecting; however, such a differentiation is not recognized under broadest reasonable interpretation of the claim language.  The recitation that “subsequent estimates of the person’s blood pressure (MAP) can be determined directly based solely on the measured AC component of the PPG signal” is incommensurate with the scope of the claims.
Examiner would like to further indicate that Appellant argues that the claimed inventive concept is reliant on a particular force being applied to the measurement surface, but does not provide further recitation of the size of the surface/sensor/object applying such force.  Typically, occlusive/tonometric blood pressure monitoring arts identify a pressure (i.e., force over area) to be applied to a measurement surface.  Since no measurement/contact area of the force-applying object is defined in the claims, the scope of the invention may be encompassed by any art which recites adequate ranges of applied pressures and sensor contact areas, as well as optimization of either of such ranges.
Appellant further states:
Both Asada and Nitzan apply a varying pressure and monitor the PPG signal for a particular event (e.g. peak amplitude). The pressure that was being applied when the event occurred is then mapped to an estimated blood pressure (MAP). (Briefs, Page 7 of 20).
Such an interpretation of Asada and Nitzan appears to read significantly on the previously recited limitations of claim 1, repeated below for convenience:
“a processing unit configured to:
extract information regarding a blood volume pulse from the AC component of the output signal of the PPG sensor during application of the force applied to the skin of the user,”
On Brief, page 8 of 20, Appellant’s citation of Asada appears to further indicate that Asada alone provides significant support for the above-cited limitation of claim 1, particularly in the following Appellant-emphasized portions:
“At maximum compliance, the known pressure applied to the cuff is equal to the ABP [Average Blood Pressure].  Thus, with a PPG sensor and a cuff, the ABP can be found by changing the external pressure applied to the extremity and locating the maximum PPG amplitude.”
Such a locating of the maximum PPG amplitude can be considered, under broadest reasonable interpretation, an extraction of “information regarding a blood volume pulse from the AC component of the output signal of the PPG sensor.”  Such a recitation of modifying externally applied pressure is provided in the Final Office Action mailed May 12, 2021, page 5, citing Paragraph 0029 of Asada.  Asada provides further support for such a limitation in at least Fig. 9: step 910, applying a different known PPG sensor unit pressure.  In page 4 of the Non-Final Office Action mailed October 21, 2021, as well as in page 3 of the Final Office Action mailed March 22, 2022, Asada is described as analyzing a pulsatile AC component of a plethysmographic (PPG) waveform, where the later combination of Nitzan was provided solely to explicitly teach both AC and DC components for the sake of expediting prosecution, however implicit or inherent such a concept may be in the art of PPG monitoring.  Thus, later recitations of Nitzan in such Office Actions to teach the step of extracting information regarding a blood volume pulse from the AC component of the PPG sensor are performed merely for the sake of convenience and do not subvert the previous citations of Asada as also teaching such a limitation.  This is supported by Appellant’s earlier-identified statement:
Both Asada and Nitzan apply a varying pressure and monitor the PPG signal for a particular event (e.g. peak amplitude). The pressure that was being applied when the event occurred is then mapped to an estimated blood pressure (MAP). (Briefs, Page 7 of 20).
On Brief, page 9 of 20, Appellant states, regarding Asada:
“In both embodiments, the estimated blood pressure is based on the pressure being applied. This estimate is performed when the PPG signal is at its peak, but the amplitude of the PPG signal at this peak is irrelevant to Asada's determination of the person's blood pressure.”
This appears to contradict earlier Appellant-recited portions of Asada, wherein the peak, i.e., a maximum amplitude, of the PPG signal is explicitly required in the determination of blood pressure.  Although Asada utilizes a formula which does not explicitly utilize the amplitude of the PPG signal for an estimate of mean arterial pressure, such a formula utilizes a variable which is dependent on the maximum height of the PPG amplitude (Asada, Paragraph 0069; Figs. 2, 4A, 4B).  The fact that Asada utilizes further or additional variables does not support Appellant’s conclusion that Asada does not “extract information regarding a blood volume pulse from the AC component of the output signal of the PPG sensor,” as recited in claim 1.  The scope of the term “mapping” appears to be broader than the scope of Appellant’s argument.
Furthermore, Appellant’s argument that Asada does not teach “mapping the value of the AC component of the PPG signal to a blood pressure value,” (Brief, page 7 of 20) is not claimed by the Appellant (see Examiner’s Answer, page 3, regarding substitution of terms; see Appellant’s claim 1).  Thus, Appellant’s arguments towards Asada directed to non-claimed language are considered moot.  Examiner notes that Appellant did not provide amendments to support Appellant’s presently argued position after:
the Non-Final Office Action mailed October 21, 2021,
the Final Office Action mailed March 22, 2022, or
the Advisory Action mailed May 18, 2022.
Regarding Briefs, pages 10-14 of 20, “Nitzan”:
On Briefs, pages 11-12 of 20, Appellant asserts that Nitzan does not disclose mapping the extracted information from the AC component of the output of the PPG sensor to a blood pressure value.  Appellants state:
The applicants agree with the Examiner's characterization of Nitzan, but respectfully note that this assertion fails to identify where Nitzan discloses mapping the extracted information from the AC component of the output of the PPG sensor to a blood pressure value, as specifically claimed by the applicants.
Appellant’s citation of Examiner’s argument appears to provide, under broadest reasonable interpretation, a mapping of measured forces to information extracted from the AC component of a PPG signal.  Particularly:
Paragraph 0057, Figs. 1A, 1B: gradual decreases in cuff pressure and mapping of certain points of cuff pressure to mean, systolic, and diastolic blood pressures.  Examiner notes that gradual increasing/decreasing in pressure and identification of fiducial points during the gradual pressure changes reads upon a predetermined force value, since the values of a ramp function are predetermined within a range; Examiner further notes that limiting an applied pressure or range of pressures based on another detected signal component also reads upon a predetermined pressure since the criteria for limiting the pressure is predetermined) (Office action, page 4, lines 5-22).
Thus, Nitzan teaches that:
the known conventional blood pressure measuring technique of oscillometry (which Asada also references in Paragraphs 0053, 0055, 0066, 0073) reads upon Appellant’s claim limitation of mapping measured forces to information extracted from the AC component of a PPG signal (Paragraph 0005: referencing Fig. 1B), and
at least one embodiment of Nitzan measures “an initial mean blood pressure of the examined subject is measured by the oscillometry method by identifying the air pressure pulse of maximal amplitude measured in the air pressured cuff, which appears when the instantaneous absolute cuff air pressure is equal to the value of mean blood pressure, as described hereinabove with reference to Fig. 1B” (Paragraph 0057); Examiner notes that Fig. 1B indicates mean blood pressure at the point Mx on air pressure pulse amplitudes, which is defined as “maximal peak-to-peak amplitude in a pulsatile component of the measured cuff air pressure” (Paragraph 0005).
On Brief, pages 13-14 of 20, Appellant presents arguments against the correction factor utilized for the calculation of average mean blood pressure described in Paragraph 0057 of Nitzan.
The portions of Nitzan in the citation of Paragraph 0057 above describing ascertaining an initial mean blood pressure is sufficient in and of itself to read upon Applicant’s limitation of “mapping the extracted information from the AC component of the output of the PPG sensor to a blood pressure value.”  Although Appellant states that Nitzan utilizes the conventional blood pressure measurement technique of oscillometry, Appellant’s claims do not possess claim limitations which markedly separate Appellant’s claimed invention from such a known technique, nor has Appellant provided sufficient amendment in opportunities for response to previous Actions.
As a secondary consideration, Appellant’s argument against Nitzan’s usage of a correction factor appears to be (similar prior arguments against Asada) incommensurate with the broadest reasonable interpretation of the term “mapping.”  Examiner emphasizes Appellant’s recited portions of Nitzan:
“In order to assess the average mean blood pressure of the examined subject, a correction factor is computed using characteristic parameter (e.g., signal minimum Is, maximum Ip, or its amplitude Ip-ls) of the sequence of 2N+1 pulses measured (e.g., optically measured by PPG, or pressure pulses measured by a pressure transducer) in the pressure-free organ, including and centralized about the time at which the pressure pulse of maximal air pressure occurred in the pressure-affected organ.”
Thus, Nitzan (similar to Asada) presents an explicit relationship between the maximum air pressure, i.e., information extracted from the AC component of a PPG signal (supported by Nitzan, Paragraph 0005) and the average mean blood pressure and correction factor.  Such a relationship can be considered a “mapping” of the point of maximal air pressure (an identified amplitude at a particular point in time) and the center of 2N+1 pulses (a range of amplitudes over a range of time) to obtain an average for the purposes of computing a correction factor.  Appellant’s provided definition of “mapping” (Brief, Page 13 of 20) though narrow, is still fulfilled by the correspondence of two points in differing sets of pressure pulses taught in Nitzan: a point in time in which a maximum occurs is related to the point in time at which the center of 2N+1 pulses occur.  Thus, the average blood pressure obtained may still be considered “mapped” to information extracted from the AC component of a PPG signal.  Regardless, Examiner reiterates that the above is merely secondary consideration, and that Nitzan’s derivation of an initial mean blood pressure via the well-known technique of oscillometry is sufficient to read upon Appellant’s argued claim limitations.
On Brief, page 14 of 20, Appellant argues that the “PPG signal in Nitzan that is used to determine the correction factor is from the pressure-free PPG sensor on a different body part from the PPG sensor to which force is being applied.”  Examiner does not map Nitzan do describe the claim limitation of applying a force applied via the contact surface of the PPG sensor to the skin of the user. This is cited as taught by Paragraph 0024 of Asada, as well as in the steps disclosed in Asada’s Fig. 9.
With regards to both references, Appellant has provided no argument against Examiner’s combination of Asada and Nitzan and has merely argued against each reference individually.  Appellant also has provided no argument to the particulars of the combination, i.e., whether Nitzan’s oscillometric method of obtaining an initial mean blood pressure would be combinable with Asada (wherein the device of Asada appears to be capable of utilizing such a technique, as described in Asada, Paragraphs 0053, 0055, 0066, 0073).
Regarding Briefs, pages 14-15 of 20, “Claim 12”:
On Brief, page 14 of 20, Appellant asserts that the device of Asada does not apply a pressure to the skin of the user only with the contact surface of the PPG sensor.  Appellant states:
The text cited by the Examiner refers to Asada's FIG. 11. Asada discloses electro-actuated polymer bands 1004a, 1004b that shrink so as to draw the opposing finger base 802 and PPG sensor together. The applicants respectfully maintain that Asada's FIG. 11 cannot reasonably be said to disclose that only the contact surface of the PPG sensor is used to apply the force to the skin of the user, as asserted by the Examiner.
In the above citation, Appellant describes how the device of Asada functions, and then follows with only an assertion that Asada’s device does not disclose Appellant’s claim limitations.  Appellant has not explicitly connected such structural components to the argued claim language, nor does Appellant provide argument as to how the wrist-worn device of Asada differs significantly Appellant’s claimed invention.  Examiner disagrees with Appellant’s assertion for the following reasons:
The antecedent basis for “the skin” recited in claim 12 can be found in claim 1, reciting:
“An optical vital signs sensor configured to measure or determine vital signs of a user, comprising a photoplethysmographic (PPG) sensor comprising a light source configured to generate light that is directed towards a skin of the user…”
It can be reasonably assumed from Appellant’s disclosure (Specification, Page 2, lines 20-26 and Figs. 1, 5, 6, and 8: wrist-mounted devices) that Appellant’s recitation of “a skin of the user” is directed to a portion of the skin on the human body, and not the entirety thereof, where it can be reasonably assumed that such a portion of the skin is the portion of skin measured by the PPG sensor.  The claims do not contain a further delineation of what is considered “the skin.”  Thus, Examiner has interpreted claim 12 to disclose a device where only the contact surface of a PPG sensor contacts a portion of the skin where a PPG measurement takes place, which is disclosed by Asada.  Although Appellant’s Brief does not present points as to how Asada does not teach such a limitation, Examiner presents the following arguments:
The PPG sensor of Asada directs force to two sides of a limb, wherein such a limb is not limited to any particular extremity (Paragraph 0114).  While Asada shows a finger base 802 (Fig. 11) which presses on a side opposite the PPG sensor unit 800, under the above-stated interpretation regarding “the skin,” the PPG sensor unit 800 directs force only to a measured area of the skin directly contacting the PPG sensor unit.
The structure of Appellant’s invention is incapable of applying a force solely to the measured area of skin directly contacting the PPG sensor.  The most claimed detail regarding a wrist-mounting structure for the PPG sensor is found in claim 5, which describes a wristband having a distensible membrane for applying pressure to the PPG sensor (Examiner additionally notes that it is not explicit that such a pressure exerted on the pressure sensor is the same as the force exerted by a force transducer described in claim 1; however, Examiner has presented art which reads closest to Appellant’s intended claim language for the sake of expediting prosecution).  Appellant’s specification on page 7, lines 31-35 describe such a wristband as “a flexible strap.”  Thus, any force applied via the contact surface of the PPG sensor would also cause an equal force to be distributed to any portion of the skin of the user contacting the PPG sensor affixing structure (Appellant’s claimed wristband), similar to the forces applied by finger base 802 of Asada opposite the PPG sensor unit 800 (Fig. 11).
Because Appellant’s arguments are individually unpersuasive with regards to Asada and Nitzan, and because Appellant’s arguments are absent any consideration of the combination of Asada and Nitzan nor its particulars, Examiner believes that the Rejection of claims 1-2, 5-10, 12, 14-21, and 23-25 under 35 U.S.C 103 over Asada and Nitzan should be upheld by the Board.
For the above reasons, it is believed that the rejections should be sustained.
