Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 101
35 U.S.C. 101 reads as follows:
Whoever invents or discovers any new and useful process, machine, manufacture, or composition of matter, or any new and useful improvement thereof, may obtain a patent therefor, subject to the conditions and requirements of this title.
Claims 1-4, 6-8, and 21-23 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 101 because the claimed invention is directed to non-statutory subject matter. The claim(s) as a whole, considering all claim elements both individually and in combination, do not amount to significantly more than an abstract idea. A streamlined analysis of claim 1 follows.
STEP 1
Regarding claim 1, the claim recites a series of structures, including a first tracking device. Thus, the claim is directed to a machine, which is one of the statutory categories of invention.
STEP 2A, PRONG ONE
The claim is then analyzed to determine whether it is directed to any judicial exception. The steps of registering a patient to a pre-operative image to enable the robotic arm to be positionable relative to the anatomical element, processing each of the first image and the second image to identify the first tracking device and second tracking device sets, determining a first relative pose and a second relative pose from the processed first and second images, respectively, comparing the first dataset and the second dataset to determine if a relative pose of the first tracking device and the second tracking device has changed, generating a notification when the relative pose of the first tracking device and the second tracking device has changed, and reregistering the patient to the pre-operative image based on the notification indicating that the relative pose of the first tracking device and the second tracking device has changed set forth a judicial exception. These steps describe a concept performed in the human mind (including an observation, evaluation, judgment, opinion). Thus, the claim is drawn to a Mental Process, which is an Abstract Idea.
STEP 2A, PRONG TWO
Next, the claim as a whole is analyzed to determine whether the claim recites additional elements that integrate the judicial exception into a practical application. The claim fails to recite an additional element or a combination of additional elements to apply, rely on, or use the judicial exception in a manner that imposes a meaningful limitation on the judicial exception. Claim 1 recites automatically position the robotic arm relative to the anatomical element after the relative pose has changed, which is merely adding insignificant extra-solution activity to the judicial exception (MPEP 2106.05(g)). The automatic positioning of the robotic arm does not provide an improvement to the technological field, the method does not affect a particular treatment or effect a particular change based on the automatically positioned robotic arm, nor does the method use a particular machine to perform the Abstract Idea.
STEP 2B
Next, the claim as a whole is analyzed to determine whether any element, or combination of elements, is sufficient to ensure that the claim amounts to significantly more than the exception. Besides the Abstract Idea, the claim recites additional steps of receiving a first dataset and receiving a second dataset. Receiving data (first dataset and second dataset) is well-understood, routine and conventional activity for those in the field of medical diagnostics. Further, the receiving steps are each recited at a high level of generality such that it amounts to insignificant presolution activity, e.g., mere data gathering step necessary to perform the Abstract Idea. When recited at this high level of generality, there is no meaningful limitation, such as a particular or unconventional step that distinguishes it from well-understood, routine, and conventional data gathering and comparing activity engaged in by medical professionals prior to Applicant's invention. Furthermore, it is well established that the mere physical or tangible nature of additional elements such as the obtaining and comparing steps do not automatically confer eligibility on a claim directed to an abstract idea (see, e.g., Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank Int'l, 134 S.Ct. 2347, 2358-59 (2014)).
Consideration of the additional elements as a combination also adds no other meaningful limitations to the exception not already present when the elements are considered separately. Unlike the eligible claim in Diehr in which the elements limiting the exception are individually conventional, but taken together act in concert to improve a technical field, the claim here does not provide an improvement to the technical field. Even when viewed as a combination, the additional elements fail to transform the exception into a patent-eligible application of that exception. Thus, the claim as a whole does not amount to significantly more than the exception itself. The claim is therefore drawn to non-statutory subject matter.
Regarding claim 1, the device recited in the claim is a generic device comprising generic components configured to perform the abstract idea. The recited first and second tracking devices are generic sensors configured to perform pre-solutional data gathering activity, the memory is configured to perform WURC activity, and the processor is configured to perform the Abstract Idea. According to section 2106.05(f) of the MPEP, merely using a computer as a tool to perform an abstract idea does not integrate the Abstract Idea into a practical application.
The same rationale also applies to claim 21.
The dependent claims also fail to add something more to the abstract independent claims as they generally recite generic data analyzing/WURC steps. Claim 2 recites using a user interface to perform WURC, post-solutional activity, claims 3 and 4 recite steps that add to the Abstract Idea, claim 6 further defines what the anatomical element is, claim 7 recites a navigation system that performs pre-solutional activity of data gathering, and claim 8 further defines the second tracking device as a generic plurality of tracking devices. Claims 22 and 23 merely recite the use of a generic sensor to perform the pre-solutional activity of data gathering. The steps recited in the independent claim maintain a high level of generality even when considered in combination with the dependent claims.

Response to Arguments
Applicant argues that the steps of the robotic arm being positionable in any pose and configured to support an object, registering a patient to a pre-operative image to enable the robotic arm to be positionable relative to the anatomical element, reregistering the patient to the pre-operative image based on the notification indicating that the relative pose of the first tracking device and the second tracking device has changed, and automatically positioning the robotic arm relative to the anatomical element after the relative pose has changed cannot be performed mentally or by hand. Examiner respectfully disagrees, as there is no particular machine that performs such steps. Although claim 1 and claim 21 mention that the at least one processor performs these steps, according to section 2106.05(f) of the MPEP, merely using a computer as a tool to perform an abstract idea does not integrate the Abstract Idea into a practical application. Furthermore, each step is recited at high generality and that each step is interpreted broadly. There is no specificity to any of these steps that would direct these steps towards not being a mental process. Regarding the registering and reregistering steps, it is noted that the claims and disclosure do not clearly describe what “registering” is. To best to the Examiner’s understanding, the registering and reregistering steps are interpreted as a medical professional using a pre-operative image as a map to move the robotic arm for a patient. Regarding the positioning steps, these steps could merely be a user manually moving the robotic arm.
Applicant argues that the claims are not directed towards a judicial exception. However, based on the amendments, it is unclear how the automatic positioning step is linked to the reregistering of the patient. Furthermore, this step could be performed mentally or by hand as this could merely be a medical practitioner that moves the robotic arm. It is noted that the claim does not recite what particular structure performs the automatically positioning step.
Regarding the tracking markers mentioned on page 11 of the filed Remarks, it is noted that the tracking markers are not clearly mentioned in the claims. For example, claim 1 merely recites using a first tracking device and second tracking device. Although the 35 U.S.C. 112(f) claim interpretation from the Non-Final Office Action mailed on 04 May 2023 interprets the first and second tracking devices as reference markers, it is noted that the first and second tracking devices are broadly recited and are non-specific. Furthermore, the steps recited in the independent claims merely recite the input of gathered data, comparing the data, outputting the compared data, and using that outputted to move the robotic arm. Based on these arguments, in both the step 2A prong two and step 2b arguments, the Applicant is arguing that the tracking markers are particular structures. If this is so, Examiner suggests to provide more details to the tracking markers in the claim to clearly reflect that these tracking markers are not WURC.
Applicant argues that Nikou et al. ‘335 fails to teach the reregistering step in the independent claims. Examiner respectfully disagrees, as “reregister” is not clearly defined in the claim or in the disclosure. [0053] of Nikou et al. ‘335 teachers that a pre-operative and intraoperative surgical plan is developed. As mentioned in the 35 U.S.C. 103 rejection for claim 1, the intraoperative surgical plan is interpreted as reregistering the patient. As such, Applicant’s arguments are not persuasive.
Applicant argues that Nikou et al. ‘335 does not teach automatically positioning the robotic arm relative to the anatomical element after the relative pose has changed. However, it was found that [0082] of Nikou et al. ‘335 recites “…the robotic arm 105A can be moved by hand (e.g., by the surgeon) or with internal motors into its ideal position and orientation for the task being performed.” [0083] of Nikou et al. ‘335 also recites “…the surgeon can activate the foot pedal to instruct the CASS 100 to place the robotic arm 105A or end effector 105B in an automatic mode that brings the robotic arm or end effector into the proper position with respect to the patient’s anatomy in order to perform the necessary resections.”. As such, Applicant’s arguments are not persuasive.
