Claim Objections
Claims 6-7 are objected to because of the following informalities:
Claim 6 lines 1-4, it appears that “wherein the controller is configured to detect one of an onset of an intrinsic uterine contraction, an increase in uterine contraction forces, and a decrease in uterine contraction forces by processing electrical signals indicative of intrinsic uterine contractions” should read “wherein the controller is configured to detect one of the onset of an intrinsic uterine contraction, the increase in uterine contraction forces, and the decrease in uterine contraction forces by processing electrical signals indicative of intrinsic uterine contractions” to clarify that the detected events are the same events presented in claim 1.
Claim 7 lines 1-2 it appears that “obtain electrical signals indicative of intrinsic uterine contractions” should read “obtain the electrical signals indicative of intrinsic uterine contractions” to clarify that the electrical signals obtained by the sensor are the same signals utilized in claim 6.
Appropriate correction is required.

The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 112(b):
(b)  CONCLUSION.—The specification shall conclude with one or more claims particularly pointing out and distinctly claiming the subject matter which the inventor or a joint inventor regards as the invention.
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 112 (pre-AIA ), second paragraph:
The specification shall conclude with one or more claims particularly pointing out and distinctly claiming the subject matter which the applicant regards as his invention.
Claims 1-9 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 112(b) or 35 U.S.C. 112 (pre-AIA ), second paragraph, as being indefinite for failing to particularly point out and distinctly claim the subject matter which the inventor or a joint inventor (or for applications subject to pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 112, the applicant), regards as the invention.
Claim 1 recites “a controller configured to: detect one of an onset of an intrinsic uterine contraction, an increase in uterine contraction forces, and a decrease in uterine contraction forces” in lines 4-6, but it is generally unclear how this detection is performed. In particular the claimed system does not include any form of sensor or a recitation that sensors outside the system provide information to the processor. Thus it is unclear how the processor is capable of detecting these events without any form of received information as presently claimed.
Claims 2-9 are rejected by virtue of their dependance on claim 1.
Prior Art
The closest prior art of record is considered to be:
US Patent Number US 5405356 A (Hahn) discloses a child birthing assistance device which utilizes an automatically synchronized expandable pneumatic girdle to externally augment the expulsion force of labor. The girdle is fitted around the abdomen of the woman and the girdle is inflated to create downward pressure on the abdomen during contraction. The inflation is synchronized with the contraction through an electronic controller which receives signals from an inter-uterine monitor (Abstract). Hahn discloses a system which detects the onset, increase, and or decrease of uterine contractions. When uterine contractions begin, the balloon within the girdle expands to enhance the expulsion force and when the contractions end the girdle deflates to relieve pressure from the patient (Col. 2 Lines 50-66). The uterine contractions are detected through an inter-uterine pressure monitor (Col. 4 Lines 3-23)
US Patent Number US 7144379 B2 (Belli) discloses an apparatus for assisting with childbirth with sensors for detecting electrical signals of a patient’s uterus. The apparatus includes a pneumatic belt which inflates to provide additional thrust to aid in the expulsion of a fetus (Abstract). Belli teaches that electrical signals may be analyzed to detect a uterine contraction. In response to the detection of a uterine contraction, a pneumatic belt may inflate to generate a greater expulsion force for the fetus. The pneumatic belt may then deflate after reaching a preset pressure (Col. 3 Lines 49-62). The electrical system detects the natural or involuntary uterine contraction and synchronizes the inflation of the belt to actuate accordingly (Col. 3 Line 63 — Col. 4 Line 15).
US Patent Number US 5951497 A (Wallace) discloses a pressure catheter for monitoring changes in pressure within the body. The system deforms during changes in pressure causing a change in the internal air pressure which is registered by a pressure sensor. The pressure change data is converted to an electrical signal recognizable by a fetal monitor (Abstract). Wallace discloses an intrauterine pressure sensor including a balloon (Col 13 Lines 22-47). The balloon is made of a low durometer material which permits deformation with an increase or decrease in pressure. The deformation of the balloon modifies the pressure of the air inside which is registered by a pressure sensor where it is converted into an electrical signal for communication with a fetal monitor (Col. 13 Lines 47-62). The balloon may be positioned between the amniotic fluid space and endometrial lining (Fig. 11; Col. 15 Lines 31-51)
US Patent Number US 7220252 B2 (Shah) discloses a dual balloon catheter for controlling postpartum hemorrhage in uterine or vaginal cavities including a catheter with an inflatable lumen. The balloon inflates to provide a substantially even pressure along the inner surface of the cavity to control postpartum hemorrhage (Abstract). The device utilizes a dual balloon system so that the gaseous inflation media is not introduced to the hemorrhage site but is rather discharged through the deflation media into the atmosphere or through a vacuum (Col. 4 Lines 35-45).
While Wallace and Shah teach balloons inserted into the uterine cavity, neither teach that the inserted balloons augment the expulsive forces of uterine contraction by performing the required functions. Hahn and Belli each teach inflatable balloons that augment the expulsive forces of uterine contraction but neither teach or suggest that the balloons may located inside the uterus as required by claim 1. Both Hahn and Belli are drawn towards girdles which wrap around the patient.
Claims 1-9 are not rejected over the prior art.
Double Patenting
The nonstatutory double patenting rejection is based on a judicially created doctrine grounded in public policy (a policy reflected in the statute) so as to prevent the unjustified or improper timewise extension of the “right to exclude” granted by a patent and to prevent possible harassment by multiple assignees. A nonstatutory double patenting rejection is appropriate where the conflicting claims are not identical, but at least one examined application claim is not patentably distinct from the reference claim(s) because the examined application claim is either anticipated by, or would have been obvious over, the reference claim(s). See, e.g., In re Berg, 140 F.3d 1428, 46 USPQ2d 1226 (Fed. Cir. 1998); In re Goodman, 11 F.3d 1046, 29 USPQ2d 2010 (Fed. Cir. 1993); In re Longi, 759 F.2d 887, 225 USPQ 645 (Fed. Cir. 1985); In re Van Ornum, 686 F.2d 937, 214 USPQ 761 (CCPA 1982); In re Vogel, 422 F.2d 438, 164 USPQ 619 (CCPA 1970); In re Thorington, 418 F.2d 528, 163 USPQ 644 (CCPA 1969).
A timely filed terminal disclaimer in compliance with 37 CFR 1.321(c) or 1.321(d) may be used to overcome an actual or provisional rejection based on nonstatutory double patenting provided the reference application or patent either is shown to be commonly owned with the examined application, or claims an invention made as a result of activities undertaken within the scope of a joint research agreement. See MPEP § 717.02 for applications subject to examination under the first inventor to file provisions of the AIA  as explained in MPEP § 2159. See MPEP § 2146 et seq. for applications not subject to examination under the first inventor to file provisions of the AIA . A terminal disclaimer must be signed in compliance with 37 CFR 1.321(b).
The filing of a terminal disclaimer by itself is not a complete reply to a nonstatutory double patenting (NSDP) rejection. A complete reply requires that the terminal disclaimer be accompanied by a reply requesting reconsideration of the prior Office action. Even where the NSDP rejection is provisional the reply must be complete. See MPEP § 804, subsection I.B.1. For a reply to a non-final Office action, see 37 CFR 1.111(a). For a reply to final Office action, see 37 CFR 1.113(c). A request for reconsideration while not provided for in 37 CFR 1.113(c) may be filed after final for consideration. See MPEP §§ 706.07(e) and 714.13.

The actual filing date of the application in which the form is filed determines what form (e.g., PTO/SB/25, PTO/SB/26, PTO/AIA /25, or PTO/AIA /26) should be used.
