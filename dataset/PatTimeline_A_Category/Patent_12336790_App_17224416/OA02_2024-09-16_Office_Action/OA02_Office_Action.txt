Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 103
In the event the determination of the status of the application as subject to AIA  35 U.S.C. 102 and 103 (or as subject to pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 102 and 103) is incorrect, any correction of the statutory basis (i.e., changing from AIA  to pre-AIA ) for the rejection will not be considered a new ground of rejection if the prior art relied upon, and the rationale supporting the rejection, would be the same under either status.
The following is a quotation of pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 103(a) which forms the basis for all obviousness rejections set forth in this Office action:
(a) A patent may not be obtained though the invention is not identically disclosed or described as set forth in section 102, if the differences between the subject matter sought to be patented and the prior art are such that the subject matter as a whole would have been obvious at the time the invention was made to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which said subject matter pertains. Patentability shall not be negated by the manner in which the invention was made.
The factual inquiries for establishing a background for determining obviousness under pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 103(a) are summarized as follows:
1. Determining the scope and contents of the prior art.
2. Ascertaining the differences between the prior art and the claims at issue.
3. Resolving the level of ordinary skill in the pertinent art.
4. Considering objective evidence present in the application indicating obviousness or nonobviousness.
Claims 2-26 are rejected under pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 103(a) as being unpatentable over Matos (U.S. 2007/0299473, previously cited) in view of Guzik (U.S. 2011/0018998, previously cited), and further in view of Gopinathan et al. (U.S. 2011/0092825, previously cited). Regarding claims 2, 15, 16, 21, and 22, Matos discloses a monitoring and defibrillation system for assisting a caregiver in treating a subject and acquiring images and video of the subject during an emergency (see Abstract), the system comprising: a video camera 112/154 configured to acquire one or more still images and real-time video of the subject at a first framerate during the emergency (see Figures 2B, 6A, and 6B and paragraph [0549]); a data collection unit configured to collect data comprising at least ECG signals, SpO2 levels and blood pressure of the subject (see paragraph [0130]); a communications unit 122 configured to establish a communication channel with a remote location (see Figure 4A and paragraph [0516]); and at least one processor 118 communicatively coupled with the video camera, the data collection unit and the communications unit (see Figure 4A and paragraph [0516]), the at least one processor configured to: establish at least one of cellular or wireless communication channel with the remote location (see paragraph [2097], which describes cellular communication, and paragraphs [0692] and [0693], which describes the communications as “wireless”), transmit the ECG signals, SpO2 levels and blood pressure of the subject to the remote location (see paragraph [0516], “signals from data processing unit 118 to the PU encoder 120 (described in detail below) are encoded and transmitted to the central station 300 by portable unit transmitter 122”), transmit the acquired one or more still images of the subject to the remote location (see paragraph [0517], “Referring again to inputs to the PU, audio input signals including the voice of enabler 100 and video input signals showing the victim and the scene of the emergency are amplified, filtered, digitized and processed by the portable unit audio and video input processing units 124. The audio and video data is then encoded by portable unit encoder 120, and then transmitted by portable unit transmitter 122.”). However, while Matos does disclose controlling transmission based on signal quality (see paragraph [0636]), including indicating that bandwidth plays a role in what types and quality of data can be sent (see paragraphs [0904], [0905], [1396], [1444], [1453], [1519], and [1710]), Matos fails to disclose that the processor is configured to determine, before transmitting the real-time video of the subject to the remote location, an available bandwidth of the communication channel, and in response to the available bandwidth exceeding a predetermined threshold bandwidth, transmit the real-time video of the subject to the remote location at the first framerate, and in response to the available bandwidth not exceeding the predetermined threshold bandwidth, transmit the real-time video of the subject to the remote location at a second framerate less than the first framerate.
Guzik is in the field of the relaying of response information to emergency responders over a bandwidth allocated system (see paragraphs [0171] and [0360]). Guzik teaches wherein a processing unit is configured to: compare available bandwidth of the communication channel to a threshold bandwidth (see Figure 8 and paragraph [0171], “when the parameter... between the mobile client 500 and the recipient device 802 reaches a first threshold 807, the QoS failover application 783 may command the mobile client 500 to switch to the streaming of still shots 808 instead of full-motion video 804; paragraph [0200], “the QoS failover application 783 may assess the bandwidth of a wireless communication connection between the mobile client 500 and the recipient device 802. Bandwidth may be assessed based on a rate of data transfer, which may be measured in bits per second (bps), or throughput, which is the average rate of successful data delivery”); if the available bandwidth exceeds the threshold bandwidth, cause transmission of both the video of the subject and the data associated with the subject (see paragraph [0171], “While the police officer 803 is near the recipient device 802 (e.g., mobile client 500 parameter is a distance), the mobile client 500 may be streaming data to the recipient device 802. In particular, the mobile client 500 may be streaming full-motion video 804, full-quality audio 805, and metadata 806”). Further, Gopinathan teaches that real-time transmission of video data is preferable during communication of data for remote medical consultations, however if the bandwidth is insufficient to support real-time transmission, the frame rate may be reduced (“As described above, the audio/video link preferably enables real-time two-way communication between a patient and remotely located medical personnel. However, depending upon available communication channel bandwidth and traffic, video and/or audio information may be reduced in quality or frame rate.”, paragraph [0047]). It would have been obvious to one having ordinary skill in the art at the time the invention was made to modify the system of Matos to include the bandwidth threshold based data-transmissions as taught by Guzik and Gopinathan in order to ensure that no data deterioration occurs during transmission due to insufficient bandwidth (Guzik, paragraph [0170]).

Regarding claim 26, Matos in view of Guzik and Gopinathan provides the invention substantially as claimed, but fails to provide that the ratio of the first framerate to the second framerate is at least 2:1. It would have been obvious to one having ordinary skill in the art at the time the invention was made to modify the system of Matos in view of Guzik and Gopinathan such that the reduced frame rate is less than half of the real-time frame rate (i.e. the ratio of the first framerate to the second framerate is at least 2:1), as it has been held that discovering an optimum value of a result effective variable (i.e. the decrease in framerate necessitated by the decrease in bandwidth) involves only routine skill in the art.
