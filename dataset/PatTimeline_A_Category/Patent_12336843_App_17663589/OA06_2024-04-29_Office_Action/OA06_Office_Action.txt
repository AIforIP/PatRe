Response to Arguments
Applicant’s arguments, see Pgs 10-13, filed 1/18/2024, with respect to the rejection(s) of claim(s) 1, 3-5, 7-8, 10-11 and 13-16,18, 20-21, 23-24, and 26-32 under USC 103 have been fully considered and are persuasive.  For example, on Pages 10-12, the Applicant argued that the current prior art does not disclose the amended claim element: “to perform the analysis of the trends the processing circuitry is configured to: determine a first variation value of at least one physiological parameter of the plurality of physiological parameters for a first period and a second variation value of the at least one of the physiological parameter for a second period preceding the first period; and determine whether a comparison of the first variation value and the second variation value satisfies a criterion.”, support can be found in Specification para. 152. Therefore, the rejection has been withdrawn.  However, upon further consideration, a new ground(s) of rejection is made in view of Stahmann (US 2005/0115561 A1), in view of McCabe (US 2010/0121399 A1), further in view of Zhang (US 2011/009760 A1), further in view of Siejko (US 2005/0148896 A1), further in view of Gheeraert (US 2016/0045166 A1).
Claim 12 has been cancelled.
Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 103
In the event the determination of the status of the application as subject to AIA  35 U.S.C. 102 and 103 (or as subject to pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 102 and 103) is incorrect, any correction of the statutory basis (i.e., changing from AIA  to pre-AIA ) for the rejection will not be considered a new ground of rejection if the prior art relied upon, and the rationale supporting the rejection, would be the same under either status.
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 103 which forms the basis for all obviousness rejections set forth in this Office action:
A patent for a claimed invention may not be obtained, notwithstanding that the claimed invention is not identically disclosed as set forth in section 102, if the differences between the claimed invention and the prior art are such that the claimed invention as a whole would have been obvious before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which the claimed invention pertains. Patentability shall not be negated by the manner in which the invention was made.
The text of those sections of Title 35, U.S. Code not included in this action can be found in a prior Office action.
Claims 1, 3-5, 7-8, 10-11, 13-16, 18, 20-21, and 23-24, 26-32 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Stahmann (US 2005/0115561 A1) in view of McCabe (US 2010/0121399 A1), further in view of Siejko (US 2005/0148896 A1) further in view of Zhang (US 2011/009760 A1) as cited previously, further in view of Gheeraert (US 2016/0045166 A1).
Regarding claim 1, 18, 27, and 29, Stahmann discloses a system comprising: an insertable cardiac monitor (ICM) (eg. Fig 4A, 400) comprising: a housing configured for subcutaneous implantation within a patient (eg. Fig. 4A, housing 401), the housing having a length, a width, and a depth, wherein the length is greater than the width and the width is greater than the depth, and wherein the length is within a range from 40 millimeters (mm) to 60 mm (eg. Para. 266); a heart sound sensor (eg. Para. 219, 231, 1486), wherein (in multiple embodiments) the ICM is configured to sense heart sounds of the patient via the heart sound sensor (eg. Para. 219, 231, 1486); wherein the ICM is configured to sense an electrocardiogram (ECG) of the patient via the plurality of electrodes (eg. Para. 217, 376); a remote computing device (eg. Para. 24, 177); and processing circuitry of at least one of the insertable cardiac monitor or the remote computing device (eg. Para. 24), the processing circuitry configured to: periodically determine a respective value for each of a plurality of physiological parameters based on one or more of the heart sounds or the ECG (eg. Para. 231, 261); output trends of the periodically determined values of the physiological parameters at the remote computing device (eg. Para. 199 stored in separate device for storage and further processing/trending/etc.); and determine whether to present an indication of a cardiac event based on an analysis of the trends of the periodically determined values of the physiological parameters (eg. Para. 199, 972, 1520).
It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to have combined the embodiments of Stahmann to incorporate heart sound sensors to better improve the diagnoses of diseases by providing additional parameters for analysis (eg. Para. 1486 and 1516-1522).
While Stahmann discloses a housing (eg. Fig. 4A 401 housing), Stahmann does not disclose multiple electrodes on the housing.
McCabe teaches an implantable medical device that has a housing (eg. Fig. 17, 1788) with leadless ECG electrodes on the housing of the device (eg. Fig. 17, 1790).
It would have been obvious to before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to have modified the implant as disclosed by Stahmann (eg. Fig. 4A) to have leadless ECG electrodes as taught by McCabe (eg. Fig. 17, electrodes 1790) for the expected outcome of making the implant more compact for ease of use and implantation.
Stahmann as modified by McCabe teaches the limitations above, but does not disclose determining a heart failure deterioration based on trends and parameters based on heart sounds and ECG.
Zhang teaches a medical monitoring device (eg. Para. 34) that detects deterioration in a patient’s heart failure status by comparing changes in heart sounds (eg. Para. 126) and ecg data (eg. Para. 47 and 127) past a certain threshold (eg. Para. 61).
It would have been obvious before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to combine the invention of Stahmann and McCabe with the deterioration thresholds for physiological parameters as taught by Zhang for the expected outcome of improving detection of heart failure and deterioration associated with it during a period of time.
The combined invention of Stahmann modified by McCabe and Zhang discloses the elements above, but does not disclose the first physiological parameter comprises a frequency of occurrence of an S3 heart sound.
Siejko teaches a cardiac rhythm management system that includes a heart sound detector for the detection of S3 heart sounds where the an S3 index is produced by counting the number of S3 beats to determine to the total number of heart beats and presented as a percentage (eg. Abstract, Para. 5 and 49, Fig. 4, step 440).
It would have been obvious before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to combine the invention of Stahmann, McCabe, and Zhang to use S3 heart sound trends/indices as taught by Siejko as an additional indication of heart failure since S3 is related to ventricular stiffness and degree of strictive filling which indicate abnormal cardiac conditions (eg. Siejko, Para. 5).
The combined invention of Stahmann modified by McCabe, Zhang, and Siejko discloses the above elements, but does not disclose to perform the analysis of the trends the processing circuitry is configured to: determine a first variation value of at least one physiological parameter of the plurality of physiological parameters for a first period and a second variation value of the at least one of the physiological parameter for a second period preceding the first period; and determine whether a comparison of the first variation value and the second variation value satisfies a criterion.
Gheeraert teaches an ECG analysis system that takes ECG data and compares it to a previous sequence of consecutive data for a patient based on different conditions in the form of difference vectors and provides a signal representative of a risk of a myocardial infarction (eg. Abstract, Para. 31-32, 86, 172-176).
It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to have combined the invention of Stahmann, McCabe, Zhang, and Siejko with the difference vector analysis as taught by Gheeraert to improve assessment with low sensitivity to false positives (eg. Gheeraert, Para. 18-30). While Gheeraert does not disclose these difference metrics with regards to heart sounds, the claim is written such that a first physiological parameter of a plurality of parameters is a frequency of S3 heart sounds, but is interpreted to be merely a non-limiting example for the amended limitation stating “at least one physiological parameter of the plurality of physiological parameter”, which can be either the ECG based or heart sound based parameters.
