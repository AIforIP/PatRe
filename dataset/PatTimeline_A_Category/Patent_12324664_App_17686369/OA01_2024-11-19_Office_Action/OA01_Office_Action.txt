Claim Interpretation
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 112(f):
(f) Element in Claim for a Combination. – An element in a claim for a combination may be expressed as a means or step for performing a specified function without the recital of structure, material, or acts in support thereof, and such claim shall be construed to cover the corresponding structure, material, or acts described in the specification and equivalents thereof.
The following is a quotation of pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 112, sixth paragraph:
An element in a claim for a combination may be expressed as a means or step for performing a specified function without the recital of structure, material, or acts in support thereof, and such claim shall be construed to cover the corresponding structure, material, or acts described in the specification and equivalents thereof.
The claims in this application are given their broadest reasonable interpretation using the plain meaning of the claim language in light of the specification as it would be understood by one of ordinary skill in the art.  The broadest reasonable interpretation of a claim element (also commonly referred to as a claim limitation) is limited by the description in the specification when 35 U.S.C. 112(f) or pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 112, sixth paragraph, is invoked.
As explained in MPEP § 2181, subsection I, claim limitations that meet the following three-prong test will be interpreted under 35 U.S.C. 112(f) or pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 112, sixth paragraph:
(A)	the claim limitation uses the term “means” or “step” or a term used as a substitute for “means” that is a generic placeholder (also called a nonce term or a non-structural term having no specific structural meaning) for performing the claimed function;
(B)	the term “means” or “step” or the generic placeholder is modified by functional language, typically, but not always linked by the transition word “for” (e.g., “means for”) or another linking word or phrase, such as “configured to” or “so that”; and
(C)	the term “means” or “step” or the generic placeholder is not modified by sufficient structure, material, or acts for performing the claimed function.
Use of the word “means” (or “step”) in a claim with functional language creates a rebuttable presumption that the claim limitation is to be treated in accordance with 35 U.S.C. 112(f) or pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 112, sixth paragraph. The presumption that the claim limitation is interpreted under 35 U.S.C. 112(f) or pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 112, sixth paragraph, is rebutted when the claim limitation recites sufficient structure, material, or acts to entirely perform the recited function.
Absence of the word “means” (or “step”) in a claim creates a rebuttable presumption that the claim limitation is not to be treated in accordance with 35 U.S.C. 112(f) or pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 112, sixth paragraph. The presumption that the claim limitation is not interpreted under 35 U.S.C. 112(f) or pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 112, sixth paragraph, is rebutted when the claim limitation recites function without reciting sufficient structure, material or acts to entirely perform the recited function.
Claim limitations in this application that use the word “means” (or “step”) are being interpreted under 35 U.S.C. 112(f) or pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 112, sixth paragraph, except as otherwise indicated in an Office action. Conversely, claim limitations in this application that do not use the word “means” (or “step”) are not being interpreted under 35 U.S.C. 112(f) or pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 112, sixth paragraph, except as otherwise indicated in an Office action.
This application includes one or more claim limitations that do not use the word “means,” but are nonetheless being interpreted under 35 U.S.C. 112(f) or pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 112, sixth paragraph, because the claim limitation(s) uses a generic placeholder that is coupled with functional language without reciting sufficient structure to perform the recited function and the generic placeholder is not preceded by a structural modifier.  Such claim limitation(s) is/are: “rotatable flow control member” in claim(s) 1, 10, and those dependent therefrom.
Because this/these claim limitation(s) is/are being interpreted under 35 U.S.C. 112(f) or pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 112, sixth paragraph, it/they is/are being interpreted to cover the corresponding structure described in the specification as performing the claimed function, and equivalents thereof.
The Examiner’s interpretation(s) of the claim limitation(s) that invoke interpretation under § 112(f) are as follows:
Claim(s) 1, 10, and those dependent therefrom recite(s) the limitation “rotatable flow control member”, which recites a generic placeholder [“member”], modified by functional language [claimed functions of the rotatable flow control member], wherein in claim 1 and those dependent therefrom, the identified generic placeholder is further modified by sufficient structure, material, or acts for performing the claimed function, such that “rotatable flow control member” in claims 1 and those dependent therefrom does not invoke interpretation under § 112(f). However, in claim 10 and those dependent therefrom, the identified generic placeholder is NOT further modified by sufficient structure, material, or acts for performing the claimed function. As such, the Examiner has interpreted the claimed “rotatable flow control member” of claim 10 and those dependent therefrom as comprising an internal T- shaped fluid passage [Applicant’s Specification ¶0007], or an equivalent therefrom.

Claim 10 is considered to recite similar indefinite subject matter [line 7] that is rejected and interpreted mutatis mutandis to the rejection of claim 1 above.
Claims 2-4 each recite the limitation “wherein the micro-volume 3-way stopcock has a fluid volume dead space” [line 2 in each of claims 2-4], which is considered indefinite, as the microvolume 3-way stopcock is considered to comprise several elements, such that it is not clear whether the dead space is meant to be defined by the entirety of each fluid passage of the claimed microvolume 3-way stopcock or may be defined by any of the claimed fluid passages. As such, the Examiner has interpreted the indefinite limitation to refer to either interpretation in light of any prior art applied under § 102 or § 103.
Claims 6-8 each recite the limitation “cannula” [line 2 in each of claims 6-8], which is considered to lack antecedent basis, as there is no previously defined “cannula” in any of claims 1 or 5-8, respectively. The limitation of “cannula” is further considered to be indefinite, as it is not clear whether “cannula” is meant to define a cannula or refer to the previously defined “cannula insert” of claim 5 [line 2]. For examination purposes, the Examiner has interpreted the recited “cannula” of each of claims 6-8 to refer to the previously recited “cannula insert” of claim 5.
Claims 6-8 and 18-19 each recite the limitation “wherein microvolume 3-way stopcock and cannula have a combined dead space fluid volume” [lines 2-3 in each of claims 6-8; lines 1-2 in each of claims 18-19], which is considered indefinite, as the microvolume 3-way stopcock is considered to comprise several elements, such that it is not clear whether the dead space is meant to be defined by the entirety of each fluid passage of the claimed microvolume 3-way stopcock and the cannula or may be defined by any of the claimed fluid passages with the cannula. As such, the Examiner has interpreted the indefinite limitation to refer to either interpretation in light of any prior art applied under § 102 or § 103.
Claim 17 recites the limitation “wherein the steps of claim 16 are repeated to obtain a desired number of blood samples in additional blood sample syringes” [lines 1-3], which is considered indefinite, as claim 16 recites “removing the first blood sample syringe from the female luer connector of the blood sample fluid passage” [lines 6-7] and “connecting a self-venting second blood sample syringe to the female luer connector of the blood sample fluid passage” [lines 8-9], as well as additional steps with respect to the second blood sample syringe, such that claim 16 is written in such a way to define steps for only the first blood sample syringe and the second blood sample syringe. As such, it is not clear whether “repeating” the steps of claim 16 is meant to refer to repetitively performing steps using the same first blood sample syringe and the second blood sample syringe or if “repeating” the steps of claim 16 is meant to refer to “removing a current blood sample syringe from the female luer connector of the blood sample fluid passage; connecting a new self-venting blood sample syringe to the female luer connector of the blood sample fluid passage; and rotating the flow control member to permit fluid flow between the inlet fluid passage and the blood sample fluid passage and to allow a desired new blood sample into the new blood sample syringe”. For examination purposes, the Examiner has interpreted “repeating” the steps of claim 16 to refer to the latter interpretation regarding a current blood sample syringe and a new blood sample syringe.

It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to have modified the microvolume 3-way stopcock of Ellingson so as to incorporate wherein the waste fluid passage connector is a female luer connector, as Ellingson discloses male and female luer connectors known types of connectors for connecting a stopcock to other devices [Ellingson, Col 2, lines 30-35, 60-64], such that this modification would amount to mere simple substitution of one known element [waste fluid passage connector of Clarke] for another [female luer connector] to obtain predictable results [allow for connection of the stopcock to other devices] [MPEP § 2143(I)(B)].
However, Ellingson fails to explicitly disclose wherein the microvolume 3-way stopcock and cannula have a dead space volume in the range from 0.03 to 0.24 mL. Altman discloses fluid delivery devices [entire disclosure – see at least abstract], wherein Altman discloses that dead space is a known property of fluid passages [The dead space is the volume of the fluid pathway between the reservoir of therapeutic agent and the discharge point at the distal tip of the catheter (Altman Col 21, lines 5-7), wherein it is noted that while Altman is directed towards fluid delivery, the dead space as disclosed in Altman may still apply to the fluid collection of Clarke]. Altman further discloses that dead space in fluid passages may be considered result effective variable, as minimizing dead space results in less fluid being trapped within the dead space [The dead space in many catheters will be equivalent to the amount of drug in a proximally located reservoir that is not effectively delivered to the tissue. This dead volume will cause inaccuracy in dosing, which can be important when small volumes are delivered. It is desirable to have the dead space be small, and the preferred inner diameter of the drug delivery tubing is preferably less than 0.03 cm (0.012). (In a catheter of 100 cm length, this amounts to a dead space volume of 0.07 milliliters, and in a catheter of 135 cm length, it results in a dead space of about 0.09 milliliters. Preferably, the volume of the dead space is in the range of 0.05 ml and 0.25 ml.) (Altman Col 21, lines 8-21)].
It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to have modified the method of Ellingson so as to incorporate wherein the microvolume 3-way stopcock has a fluid volume dead space in the range of 0.03 to 0.24 mL, as a matter of routine optimization [see Altman Col 21, lines 8-21] since it has been held that “where the general conditions of a claim are disclosed in the prior art, it is not inventive to discover the optimum or workable ranges by routine experimentation. In re Aller, 220 F.2d 454, 456, 105 USPQ 233, 235 (CCPA 1955).
However, Ellingson fails to explicitly disclose connecting a waste blood collection device to the female luer connector of the waste fluid passage, such that rotating the flow control member to permit fluid flow between the inlet fluid passage and the waste fluid passage allows for waste blood into the waste syringe. Ellingson does disclose elsewhere the use of a self-venting syringe connected to the stopcock for blood collection [The exterior of the housing 35 further defines a threaded nut member 29 for threadingly engaging the Luer connector 42 of the self-sealing valve 44 (Ellingson Col 3, lines 3-5), which as seen in Figures 1-2 the male luer connector 42 is connected to syringe 61] that is not explicitly connected to the female luer of the waste fluid passage.
It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to have modified the method so as to incorporate wherein the bodily fluid collection device is a self-venting waste syringe, as Ellingson discloses the use of self-venting syringes to be connected to the stopcock [Ellingson Col 3, lines 3-5], as this modification would amount to mere simple substitution of one known element [undisclosed fluid sampling means for side port 69] for another [self-venting waste syringe] to obtain predictable results [allow for blood collection] [MPEP § 2143(I)(B)].
However, Ellingson fails to explicitly disclose returning the waste blood from the waste syringe to the patient. However, it also understood that syringes are operable to both withdraw a volume of fluid to be stored in the body of the syringe, as well as deliver a volume of fluid that is stored in the body of the syringe.
It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to have modified the method of Ellingson so as to incorporate wherein the waste blood from the waste syringe is returned to the patient, as this modification would amount to merely applying a known technique [fluid delivery via a syringe] to a known device (method, or product) [waste syringe] ready for improvement to yield predictable results [deliver fluid from the waste syringe] [MPEP § 2143(I)(D)].
