Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 112
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 112(b):
(b)  CONCLUSION.—The specification shall conclude with one or more claims particularly pointing out and distinctly claiming the subject matter which the inventor or a joint inventor regards as the invention.
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 112 (pre-AIA ), second paragraph:
The specification shall conclude with one or more claims particularly pointing out and distinctly claiming the subject matter which the applicant regards as his invention.
Claims 1-22 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 112(b) or 35 U.S.C. 112 (pre-AIA ), second paragraph, as being indefinite for failing to particularly point out and distinctly claim the subject matter which the inventor or a joint inventor (or for applications subject to pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 112, the applicant), regards as the invention.
In claim 1, the preamble is inconsistent with the claim body.  The preamble is for “features within images” (a plurality), but the claim body discusses at least one feature in one image (a single image).  It is suggested to use something similar to “for identifying at least one abnormal biomedical feature within at least one image of biomedical data” in the preamble.  In addition, the preamble states it is for an “abnormal” feature, but nothing in the claim body determines or references an “abnormal feature” and it is unclear if an “abnormal” feature must be found in the prior art or if the prior art must just highlight any feature.  In line 3, “an image of biomedical data” is vague as line 1 has “images of biomedical data” and it is unclear if the image in line 3 is referencing the images from line 1, or a different image.  It is suggested to use the above suggestion and in line 3 then to use “to capture the at least one image of biomedical data”.  In line 9, “a plurality of biomedical features” is vague as line 1 also used “biomedical features” and it is unclear if the features in line 9 are the same as the features in the preamble or different features.  In line 14, “at least a biomedical feature” is vague as line 9 and the preamble both reference biomedical features.  It is unclear if the feature of line 14 is the same as the feature in line 9, the feature of the preamble, or is a completely new separate feature.  If it is the same feature, then it is suggested to use something similar to “highlight at least one biomedical feature from the plurality of features…”.  If it is a different feature, then a modifier such as “highlight at least one additional biomedical feature” should be used.  In the last two paragraphs, “the at least a” is vague.  It is suggested to use “at least one” for the distance metric and the highlighted biomedical feature, then reference “the at least one” in the independent and dependent claims.
Similarly, claim 12 has these problems.

The following is a quotation of the appropriate paragraphs of 35 U.S.C. 102 that form the basis for the rejections under this section made in this Office action:
A person shall be entitled to a patent unless –
(a)(1) the claimed invention was patented, described in a printed publication, or in public use, on sale, or otherwise available to the public before the effective filing date of the claimed invention.
Claims 1-2, 5-7, 9-13, 16-18, and 20-22 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 102a1 as being anticipated by Grbie et al (20190021677).  Grbie discloses the claimed invention having an image capture device (e.g. CT, ultrasound, Xray, etc., para. 55 etc.) using a processor with memory to hold instructions for the processor to perform tasks (e.g. paras. 52-54, etc.) to display highlighted color heat map images of features/abnormal features (e.g. paras. 19, 63, 65, 116-118, figures 4, 14, etc.) where a plurality of the features are extracted (e.g. organs, bones, tissue, etc., paras. 84, 205, etc.) from time series images (e.g. paras. 94-96, 6, etc.) to compare to repository data of other patients that have trained the neural network (e.g. training the neural network with previous images to detect the injury/abnormality, paras. 87, 88, 93-99, 124, 201-203, 211, etc.) and then generates a distance metric between the trained data and the input captured image (e.g. similarity between the two, paras. 100, 124-126, 129 etc.) where the distance metric has been generated with a plurality of hypotheses/probabilities using a plurality of labels network (e.g. image compared to other patient images to determine probability of the abnormal feature, paras. 87, 88, 93-99, 124, 201-203, 211, etc.) to provide the displayed highlight feature in the image/in-silicon image (filtered, denoised, cleaned up image, paras. 212-213, etc.) and based on the user input to the user interface (e.g. paras. 65, 89, 230-239, etc.).
Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 103
In the event the determination of the status of the application as subject to AIA  35 U.S.C. 102 and 103 (or as subject to pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 102 and 103) is incorrect, any correction of the statutory basis (i.e., changing from AIA  to pre-AIA ) for the rejection will not be considered a new ground of rejection if the prior art relied upon, and the rationale supporting the rejection, would be the same under either status.
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 103 which forms the basis for all obviousness rejections set forth in this Office action:
A patent for a claimed invention may not be obtained, notwithstanding that the claimed invention is not identically disclosed as set forth in section 102, if the differences between the claimed invention and the prior art are such that the claimed invention as a whole would have been obvious before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which the claimed invention pertains. Patentability shall not be negated by the manner in which the invention was made.
This application currently names joint inventors. In considering patentability of the claims the examiner presumes that the subject matter of the various claims was commonly owned as of the effective filing date of the claimed invention(s) absent any evidence to the contrary.  Applicant is advised of the obligation under 37 CFR 1.56 to point out the inventor and effective filing dates of each claim that was not commonly owned as of the effective filing date of the later invention in order for the examiner to consider the applicability of 35 U.S.C. 102(b)(2)(C) for any potential 35 U.S.C. 102(a)(2) prior art against the later invention.
Claims 4, 8, 15, and 19 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Grbie et al.  Grbie discloses the claimed invention and that it works on multiple images and with ECG/heart data (e.g. para. 59, etc.) and training the system, but is not clear on the exact nature of using ECG heart image data to highlight ECG features and does not disclose validating the digitized signals by classifying the signals to a plurality of preliminary parameters, determining an accuracy status of the parameters by comparing the plurality of preliminary parameters to the plurality of biomedical parameters, and generating a quality diagnostic of the biomedical data based on the result of the validation.  It would have been obvious to one having ordinary skill in the art at the time the invention was made/before it was effectively filed to have modified the system and method as taught by Grbie, with using ECG heart image data to highlight ECG features, or validating the digitized signals by classifying the signals to a plurality of preliminary parameters, determining an accuracy status of the parameters by comparing the plurality of preliminary parameters to the plurality of biomedical parameters, and generating a quality diagnostic of the biomedical data based on the result of the validation, as is well known and common knowledge in the art (mpep 2144), since it would provide the predictable results of: providing another fast and accurate way to read ECG data images and determine if the patient is experiencing any heart troubles to provide immediate therapy to the patient; and allowing the system to be initially tested, determining the accuracy of the trained neural network to know if it is providing the correct/quality results, and then validating or retraining the network based on if there are correct/quality results or not.
