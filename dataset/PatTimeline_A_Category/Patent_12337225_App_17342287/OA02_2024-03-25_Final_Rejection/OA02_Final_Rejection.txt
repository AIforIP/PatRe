DETAILED ACTION
This Office Action is in response to applicant’s amendments and arguments filed 12/15/2023 for application 17/342,287. In applicant’s amendments filed 12/15/2023, claims 6 & 7 were cancelled, and claims 1, 4-5, and 9-18 were amended.
Claims 1-5 and 8-18 are acknowledged as pending in this application and are considered below.
Response to Amendment
The objections to the abstract of the disclosure withdrawn. The objections to the specifications have been obviated in view of applicant’s amendments and were withdrawn. Most of the claim objections have been withdrawn in view of the amendments, however, some remain or are new in light of the amendments filed 12/15/2023, and there is a note with regards to phrasing repetition and simplifying the claim language.
Some of the rejections under 35 U.S.C. 112(b) have been obviated and were withdrawn in view of applicant’s amendments and arguments, however some rejections remain, and some new rejections were introduced as a result of the amendments filed 12/15/2023. The claims are still rejected under 35 U.S.C. 101 despite applicant’s amendments and arguments. The rejections of claims 1, 2, and 18 under 35 U.S.C. 102(a)(1) have been withdrawn as applicant’s amendments introduced former claims 6 & 7 into claim 1, however, applicant’s amendments necessitated new grounds of rejection under 35 U.S.C. 103, see below.
Claim Objections
Claims 1, 4, 5, and 12 are objected to for the following informalities:
Claim 1, paragraph 4: Phrasing should be amended so that there is better flow from the method comprising to the action of what the step includes. Such as rephrasing the paragraph to read something akin to --a step of dynamically modifying, via the data processing unit, wherein if an instant of playing time falls within a set time interval provided by the configuration instructions, the control interface is dynamically modified so that it takes a respective operating configuration corresponding to a set time interval reached by the multimedia content,--. This would also cure the 112(b) issue below in regards to whether “a step of dynamically modifying” is the name of the step or the action.
Claim 4, lines 2-8: The suggestion from the previous 112(b) rejection should have substituted the lines with regards to “a step of automatically setting”. Adding the Office’s limitation suggestion to the end of the claim makes it redundant (the Office notes the non-final states to modify one language for the other, or something of the like that completes the phrase and action of what happened should be added to the end). Therefore, the claim should state --a step of automatically setting, at an end of a time interval corresponding to the operating configuration in which the user can select an initial workout difficulty level, via the data processing unit, wherein if the user has not made any selection, a first initial workout difficulty level will be set automatically.--
Claim 5, lines 1-8: Similar to the reasoning of the objection to claim 4, this claim should be restated to simply --the step of dynamically modifying comprises a step of removing, via the data processing unit, once the initial workout difficulty level has been set, wherein the temporary controls representative of the initial workout difficulty levels selectable by the user on the display module are removed.--
Claim 12, lines 5-6: “of at least one first operating parameter” should read --of the at least one first operating parameter-- or --of said at least one operating parameter-- as “at least one operating parameter” was previously introduced.
Although not a formal objection, the Office suggests amending the claims so that the phrasing is concise. For example, in limitations such as “a step of dynamically modifying, by the data processing unit of the exercise machine, based on the configuration instructions of the exercise machine”, the relationship of the data processing unit to the exercise machine is already known, as well as the configuration instructions are known to be related to the exercise machine. Further, it was already expressed that the configuration instructions are read by the data processing unit. Therefore, if applicant feels mentioning that the step of dynamically modifying is done by the data processing unit, that should be the only part that needs to be listed. For example, it would then be --a step of dynamically modifying, by the data processing unit,--. Other examples can be found in the non-final rejection mailed 07/20/2023 which drew attention (on at least page 7) to the redundancy and idiomatic errors in the previous set of claims.
Overall, it seems elements of independent claims 1 and 18 are able to be understood, but does not follow the current U.S. practice of phrasing the claim, and that some portions of the claim could be more succinct to make the claim set easier to understand and follow.

Step 2A, Prong 2
The above-identified abstract idea in each of independent Claims 1 and 18 (and their respective dependent Claims 2-5 and 8-17) is not integrated into a practical application under 2019 PEG because the additional elements (identified above in independent Claims 1 and 18), either alone or in combination, generally link the use of the above-identified abstract idea to a particular technological environment or field of use.  More specifically, each of Claims 1-5 and 8-17 recites no additional elements and Claim 18 recites the additional elements of: a data processing unit and a control interface that is operationally connected to the data processing unit and includes a display module. These additional elements are generically recited computer elements in independent Claim 18 which do not improve the functioning of a computer, or any other technology or technical field.  Nor do these above-identified additional elements serve to apply the above-identified abstract idea with, or by use of, a particular machine, effect a transformation or apply or use the above-identified abstract idea in some other meaningful way beyond generally linking the use thereof to a particular technological environment, such that the claim as a whole is more than a drafting effort designed to monopolize the exception.  Furthermore, the above-identified additional elements do not add a meaningful limitation to the abstract idea because they amount to simply implementing the abstract idea on a computer.  For at least these reasons, the abstract idea identified above in independent Claims 1 and 18 (and their respective dependent claims) is not integrated into a practical application under 2019 PEG.
Moreover, the above-identified abstract idea is not integrated into a practical application under 2019 PEG because the claimed method and system merely implements the above-identified abstract idea (e.g., mental process and certain method of organizing human activity) using rules (e.g., computer instructions) executed by a computer (e.g., data processing unit as claimed).  In other words, these claims are merely directed to an abstract idea with additional generic computer elements which do not add a meaningful limitation to the abstract idea because they amount to simply implementing the abstract idea on a computer.  Additionally, Applicant’s specification does not include any discussion of how the claimed invention provides a technical improvement realized by these claims over the prior art or any explanation of a technical problem having an unconventional technical solution that is expressed in these claims.  That is, like Affinity Labs of Tex. v. DirecTV, LLC, the specification fails to provide sufficient details regarding the manner in which the claimed invention accomplishes any technical improvement or solution.  Thus, for these additional reasons, the abstract idea identified above in independent Claims 1 and 18 (and their respective dependent claims) is not integrated into a practical application under the 2019 PEG.
Accordingly, independent Claims 1 and 18 (and their respective dependent claims) are each directed to an abstract idea under 2019 PEG.
Step 2B
None of Claims 1 and 18 include additional elements that are sufficient to amount to significantly more than the abstract idea for at least the following reasons.
Each of Claims 1-5 and 8-17 recites no additional elements and Claim 18 recites the additional elements of: a data processing unit and a control interface that is operationally connected to the data processing unit and includes a display module.
The above-identified additional elements are generically claimed computer components which enable the above-identified abstract idea(s) to be conducted by performing the basic functions of automating mental tasks.  The courts have recognized such computer functions as well understood, routine, and conventional functions when claimed in a merely generic manner (e.g., at a high level of generality) or as insignificant extra-solution activity. See, Versata Dev. Group, Inc. v. SAP Am., Inc. , 793 F.3d 1306, 1334, 115 USPQ2d 1681, 1701 (Fed. Cir. 2015); and OIP Techs., 788 F.3d at 1363, 115 USPQ2d at 1092-93.
Per Applicant’s specification, the claimed data processing unit 101 is, for example, a microprocessor or a microcontroller (Page 5, 5th paragraph) and operatively connected to a local memory unit 102 (Page 5, 6th paragraph). The claimed control interface is “of the touchscreen type” (Page 8, 1st paragraph). The control interface 103 further comprises a display module (display) 104 (Page 8, 2nd paragraph). In a preferred embodiment, in which the control interface 103 is of the touchscreen type, the display module 104 coincides with the control interface 103 (Page 8, 5th paragraph).
Accordingly, in light of Applicant’s specification, the claimed terms a data processing unit and a control interface that includes a display module is reasonably construed as a generic computing device.  Like SAP America vs Investpic, LLC (Federal Circuit 2018), it is clear, from the claims themselves and the specification, that these limitations require no improved computer resources, just already available computers, with their already available basic functions, to use as tools in executing the claimed process.
Furthermore, Applicant’s specification does not describe any special programming or algorithms required for the data processing unit, control interface and display module.  This lack of disclosure is acceptable under 35 U.S.C. §112(a) since this hardware performs non-specialized functions known by those of ordinary skill in the computer arts.  By omitting any specialized programming or algorithms, Applicant's specification essentially admits that this hardware is conventional and performs well understood, routine and conventional activities in the computer industry or arts.  In other words, Applicant’s specification demonstrates the well-understood, routine, conventional nature of the above-identified additional elements because it describes these additional elements in a manner that indicates that the additional elements are sufficiently well-known that the specification does not need to describe the particulars of such additional elements to satisfy 35 U.S.C. § 112(a) (see Berkheimer memo from April 19, 2018, (III)(A)(1) on page 3).  Adding hardware that performs “‘well understood, routine, conventional activit[ies]’ previously known to the industry” will not make claims patent-eligible (TLI Communications).
The recitation of the above-identified additional limitations in Claim 18 amounts to mere instructions to implement the abstract idea on a computer.  Simply using a computer or other machinery in its ordinary capacity for economic or other tasks (e.g., to receive, store, or transmit data) or simply adding a general purpose computer or computer components after the fact to an abstract idea (e.g., a fundamental economic practice or mathematical equation) does not provide significantly more. See Affinity Labs v. DirecTV, 838 F.3d 1253, 1262, 120 USPQ2d 1201, 1207 (Fed. Cir. 2016) (cellular telephone); and TLI Communications LLC v. AV Auto, LLC, 823 F.3d 607, 613, 118 USPQ2d 1744, 1748 (Fed. Cir. 2016) (computer server and telephone unit).  Moreover, implementing an abstract idea on a generic computer, does not add significantly more, similar to how the recitation of the computer in the claim in Alice amounted to mere instructions to apply the abstract idea of intermediated settlement on a generic computer.
A claim that purports to improve computer capabilities or to improve an existing technology may provide significantly more. McRO, Inc. v. Bandai Namco Games Am. Inc., 837 F.3d 1299, 1314-15, 120 USPQ2d 1091, 1101-02 (Fed. Cir. 2016); and Enfish, LLC v. Microsoft Corp., 822 F.3d 1327, 1335-36, 118 USPQ2d 1684, 1688-89 (Fed. Cir. 2016).  However, a technical explanation as to how to implement the invention should be present in the specification for any assertion that the invention improves upon conventional functioning of a computer, or upon conventional technology or technological processes.  That is, the disclosure must provide sufficient details such that one of ordinary skill in the art would recognize the claimed invention as providing an improvement.  Here, Applicant’s specification does not include any discussion of how the claimed invention provides a technical improvement realized by these claims over the prior art or any explanation of a technical problem having an unconventional technical solution that is expressed in these claims.  Instead, as in Affinity Labs of Tex. v. DirecTV, LLC 838 F.3d 1253, 1263-64, 120 USPQ2d 1201, 1207-08 (Fed. Cir. 2016), the specification fails to provide sufficient details regarding the manner in which the claimed invention accomplishes any technical improvement or solution.
For at least the above reasons, the methods of Claims 1-5 and 8-17 and the exercise machine of Claim 18 are directed to applying an abstract idea (e.g., mental process or certain method of organizing human activity) on a general purpose computer without (i) improving the performance of the computer itself (as in McRO, Bascom and Enfish), or (ii) providing a technical solution to a problem in a technical field (as in DDR).  In other words, none of Claims 1-5 and 8-18 provide meaningful limitations to transform the abstract idea into a patent eligible application of the abstract idea such that these claims amount to significantly more than the abstract idea itself.
Taking the additional elements individually and in combination, the additional elements do not provide significantly more.  Specifically, Claims 1-5 and 8-17 include no additional elements, and when viewed individually, the above-identified additional elements in independent Claim 18 does not add significantly more because they are simply an attempt to limit the abstract idea to a particular technological environment.  That is, neither the general computer elements nor any other additional element adds meaningful limitations to the abstract idea because these additional elements represent insignificant extra-solution activity.  When viewed as a combination, these above-identified additional elements simply instruct the practitioner to implement the claimed functions with well-understood, routine and conventional activity specified at a high level of generality in a particular technological environment.  As such, there is no inventive concept sufficient to transform the claimed subject matter into a patent-eligible application.  As such, the above-identified additional elements, when viewed as whole, do not provide meaningful limitations to transform the abstract idea into a patent eligible application of the abstract idea such that the claims amount to significantly more than the abstract idea itself.  Thus, Claims 1-5 and 8-18 merely apply an abstract idea to a computer and do not (i) improve the performance of the computer itself (as in Bascom and Enfish), or (ii) provide a technical solution to a problem in a technical field (as in DDR).
Therefore, none of the Claims 1 to 18 amounts to significantly more than the abstract idea itself.
Accordingly, Claims 1-5 and 8-18 are not patent eligible and rejected under 35 U.S.C. 101 as being directed to abstract ideas implemented on a generic computer in view of the Supreme Court Decision in Alice Corporation Pty. Ltd. v. CLS Bank International, et al. and 2019 PEG.

Response to Arguments
Applicant's arguments filed 12/15/2023 have been fully considered but they are not persuasive.
In regards to the arguments against the rejection of claims 1-5 and 8-18 under 35 U.S.C. 101, they are insufficient as elements cited by Applicant on page 14 of their remarks (12/15/2023) are either generic elements (an exercise machine, a control interface, a data processing unit, a display module) or a component (multimedia content) that would not be an advancement over the 101. As the few components are generic or components, it has not amounted to the significantly more. The amendments did not appear to include any additional structure, and thus the rejection remains.
In regards to the arguments of the prior art rejection, wherein the Office notes limitations of claims 6 and 7 have been introduced into both independent claims 1 and 18 (claims 6 and 7 having previously been rejection under 35 U.S.C. 103), they are not sufficient in overcoming the prior art of record. Specifically, Applicant states (pages 16-19 of remarks filed 12/15/2023) the objective of claim 1 is to provide a method for controlling a control interface of an exercise machine which allows said machine to be “intuitive, versatile, and simple as possible”. It is argued that a person of skill in the art wouldn’t come to the method of claim 1 based on Intonato and Evancha as the combination of references only provides the user with a single box with a value to be selected by the user, wherein Intonato showing “jog”, “run”, and “sprint” doesn’t include an indication of a corresponding value. The Office argues it is never defined the corresponding value is a numerical value, and Intonato describes various operating parameters the combination of references would be obvious to modify the window comprising the temporary controls of Intonato to include additional operating parameters as Intonato is capable of having more than one, so displaying them in one area would be beneficial.
Further, they argue that the references provide the personal trainer instruction in a different window rather than in the same window the selectable operation modes are in, thus creating difficulty for a user that has to shift their gaze. Applicant is arguing narrower than claimed. The Office notes that lines 43-47 state that it is indicated “on the display module which of the temporary controls … is suggested by the personal trainer”, and that the indication does not need to be provided on one of the temporary controls, just on the display module. Therefore, it is not required that instructor suggestions and the temporary controls be listed in one window.
In regards to the argument about the personal trainers’ suggestions to an executable control, the Office believes an indication such as “10 MIN EASY RUN” would indicate a user to select the “run” executable control, and that personal trainer suggestions can vary, and not every user engagement from the personal trainer must result in an executable action (as some can be for means of encouragement).
The Office also aims to further clarify that the operating parameter is the speed (first) and slope (second) of the deck (although Intonato states other parameters of the exercise machine that associated with incremental increase or decreases could be used), and that the temporary controls are the selectable values, with those values being “jog”, “sprint”, “run”, and with Evancha, the slope numerical values. As Intonato teaches that operating parameters are associated with incremental increases or decreases, it would be obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art that jog, sprint, run, are incremental stages, and that the incremental values could apply to other parameters.
Further, Intonato describes in paragraph 0069 that the speed associated with, for example, the ““jog” control may comprise a mean, median, or mode belt speed included in user data received”. So, although Applicant argues descriptions such as ‘jog’ don’t constitute a value, it is never claimed that is must be a numerical value, and if is intended to be a numerical value, Intonato states that the short name controls are related to a numerical speed value, and that “relatively vague, nebulous, or nondescript executable controls 418 described herein may be configured in a similar fashion”.
