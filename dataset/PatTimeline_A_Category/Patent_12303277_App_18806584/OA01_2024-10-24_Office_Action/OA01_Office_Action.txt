DETAILED ACTION
Notice of Pre-AIA  or AIA  Status
The present application is being examined under the pre-AIA  first to invent provisions. Claims 2-19 are currently pending.
Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 112
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 112(b):
(b)  CONCLUSION.—The specification shall conclude with one or more claims particularly pointing out and distinctly claiming the subject matter which the inventor or a joint inventor regards as the invention.
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 112 (pre-AIA ), second paragraph:
The specification shall conclude with one or more claims particularly pointing out and distinctly claiming the subject matter which the applicant regards as his invention.
Claims 10-19 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 112(b) or 35 U.S.C. 112 (pre-AIA ), second paragraph, as being indefinite for failing to particularly point out and distinctly claim the subject matter which the inventor or a joint inventor (or for applications subject to pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 112, the applicant), regards as the invention.
Claim 10 recites the limitation a first substrate” and “a second substrate” in lines 9 and 11. The substrates in line four reads the same as the ones listed in lines nine and eleven, at present they are being read as the same layer. There is insufficient antecedent basis for this limitation in the claim.
Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 103
The following is a quotation of pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 103(a) which forms the basis for all obviousness rejections set forth in this Office action:
(a) A patent may not be obtained though the invention is not identically disclosed or described as set forth in section 102, if the differences between the subject matter sought to be patented and the prior art are such that the subject matter as a whole would have been obvious at the time the invention was made to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which said subject matter pertains. Patentability shall not be negated by the manner in which the invention was made.
The factual inquiries for establishing a background for determining obviousness under pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 103(a) are summarized as follows:
1. Determining the scope and contents of the prior art.
2. Ascertaining the differences between the prior art and the claims at issue.
3. Resolving the level of ordinary skill in the pertinent art.
4. Considering objective evidence present in the application indicating obviousness or non-obviousness.
Claims 2-7 and 9 are rejected under pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 103(a) as being unpatentable over Nissila 2002/0082491 (hereinafter Nissila) in view of Lindberg et al. US Publication 2007/0285868 (hereinafter Lindberg).
Regarding claim 2, Nissila discloses an electronic device for long-term adhesion to a mammal (Figures 2-8), the device comprising: a housing comprising a physiologic data collection circuit (203 with circuit 202 and Figure 12 which shows the circuit configuration), the housing positioned over a flexible layer extending from beneath the housing (Figure 3 with housing 203 and flexible layer 201) and comprising an electrode positioned on an underside of the flexible layer distal from the housing (electrode 122 under 116 which is the top portion of 101, see Figure 4), wherein the flexible layer comprises: a substrate lower layer (101), and a lower adhesive layer positioned on the underside of the flexible layer (127) and extending from beneath the housing to the electrode (127), the lower adhesive layer configured to provide adhesion to the skin of the mammal ([0034]), but is silent on the additional layers.
Lindberg teaches a physiological monitoring patch that includes a flexible layer  (Figures 2a-b) comprising: a substrate upper layer (16) overlying an electrical connection (14), the electrical connection extending linearly from the data collection circuit to the electrode (15) when viewed from above the electronic device (Figure 2b where the housing sits above 18 at the center and electrically connects to the connection 14 through the holes 171), an upper adhesive layer positioned under the substrate upper layer ([0077] where an adhesive is mentioned as being a method to combine the layers together), the upper adhesive layer adhering the substrate upper layer to a substrate lower layer underlying the electrical connection (upper substrate 16, lower substrate 12 with connection element 14 between them, where an adhesive can be used to laminate the layers together as per [0077]). Therefore, it would have been obvious to the skilled artisan at the time of invention to utilize the layers and configuration as taught by Lindberg with the device of Nissila as predictable results would have ensued (affording a non-stretchable, breathable flexible layer for attachment to a user).
Regarding claim 3, Nissila discloses that the lower adhesive layer extends at least partially below the housing (Figure 4a and 7 with housing 203 and the adhesive 127 extending away from it).
Regarding claim 4, Nissila discloses a flap extending beneath the housing (125 as per Figure 4).
Regarding claim 5, Nissila discloses that the housing is rigid (housing 203 is not detailed as being specifically rigid however the Office is taking Official Notice that rigid housings were well known before the effective filing date of the invention and for the purpose of protecting the internal sensitive electrical components, further “rigid” is a relative term).
Regarding claim 6, Nissila discloses that the housing is configured to remain connected to the flexible layer when the housing is tilted at an angle relative the adhesive layer in response to movement of the user (Figures 3-4 and 6-8 where when the device is placed over the chest as shown in Figure 2 or in other areas such as over the manubrium, the curvature of the individual would inherently have the conformable flexible layer 101 as per [0033] be offset at a different angle than the housing during regular usage).
Regarding claim 7, Nissila discloses that a hinge portion adjacent the housing (the portion along 101 that connects to the housing at 203 is considered the hinge portion).
Regarding claim 9, Nissila discloses that the physiologic data collection circuit is configured to collect cardiac rhythm data from the user (abstract).
Regarding claim 10, Nissila discloses an electronic device for long-term adhesion to a mammal (as mentioned above), the device comprising: a housing comprising a physiologic data collection circuit (as mentioned above); and a lower adhesive layer positioned on a bottom surface (as mentioned above), the lower adhesive layer providing adhesion to the skin of the mammal (as mentioned above), an electrode positioned on the bottom surface of the electrode-supporting section (118, 122), the electrode electrically connected to the physiologic data collection circuit [0037]); but is silent on the configuration of layers.
Lindberg teaches an electrode-supporting section comprising a first substrate layer (16, can also be read as 17, see Figures 2a-b), a second substrate layer (12), and a first substrate layer positioned over the electrode and extending horizontally away from the housing beyond a boundary of the electrode (Figures 2a-b, element 16); and a second substrate layer positioned over the first substrate layer and extending horizontally away from the housing beyond the boundary of the first substrate layer (element 18 as per [0027] which details that the layers 10 and 18 extend around the other interior layers to encapsulate them). Therefore, it would have been obvious to the skilled artisan at the time of invention to utilize the layers and configuration as taught by Lindberg with the device of Nissila as predictable results would have ensued (affording a non-stretchable, breathable flexible layer for attachment to a user).
Regarding claim 11, Nissila is silent on the third layer. Lindberg discloses that the electrode supporting section further comprises a third substrate layer adhered to the first substrate layer (12 of Figures 2a-b). It would have been obvious to the skilled artisan before the effective filing date to utilize the additional layer as taught by Lindberg with the device of Nissila in order to properly insulate the conductive components.
Regarding claim 12, Nissila is silent on the additional adhesive. Lindberg teaches  an upper adhesive layer positioned on the underside of the first substrate layer ([0077] where an adhesive is mentioned as being a method to combine the layers together). It would have been obvious to the skilled artisan before the effective filing date to utilize the adhesive between the layers as taught by Lindberg with the device of Nissila in order to aid in keeping the layers together as is known in the art.
Regarding claim 13, Nissila discloses that the lower adhesive layer extends at least partially below the housing (127 as per Figures 4a and 7).
Regarding claim 14, Nissila discloses a flap extending beneath the housing (125 as per Figure 4).
Regarding claim 15, see contents of rejected claim 5 above.
Regarding claim 16, Nissila discloses that the housing is configured to remain connected to the flexible layer when the housing is tilted at an angle relative the adhesive layer in response to movement of the user (Figures 3-4 and 6-8 where when the device is placed over the chest as shown in Figure 2 or in other areas such as over the manubrium, the curvature of the individual would inherently have the conformable flexible layer 101 as per [0033] be offset at a different angle than the housing during regular usage). The electrode supporting section rendered obvious above via Lindberg and for the same reasons (see contents of rejected claim 10 above).
Regarding claim 17, Nissila discloses that a hinge portion adjacent the housing (the portion along 101 that connects to the housing at 203 is considered the hinge portion).
Regarding claim 19, Nissila discloses that the physiologic data collection circuit is configured to collect cardiac rhythm data from the user (abstract).
Claims 8 and 18 are rejected under pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 103(a) as being unpatentable over Nissila in view of Lindberg, and in further view of Cross et al. US Publication 2008/0288026 (hereinafter Cross).
Regarding claims 8 and 18, Nissila is silent on the adhesive being a hydrocolloid. Cross teaches an ECG monitoring patch that includes that the adhesive layer comprises a hydrocolloid adhesive ([0002][0048] and element 28). Therefore, it would have been obvious to the skilled artisan before the effective filing date to utilize the hydrocolloid adhesive as taught by Cross in lieu of the adhesive of Nissila in order to improve long term skin comfort.
