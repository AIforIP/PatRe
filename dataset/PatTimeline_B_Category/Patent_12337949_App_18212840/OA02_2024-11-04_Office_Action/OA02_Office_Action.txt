Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 102
The following is a quotation of the appropriate paragraphs of 35 U.S.C. 102 that form the basis for the rejections under this section made in this Office action:
A person shall be entitled to a patent unless –
(a)(2) the claimed invention was described in a patent issued under section 151, or in an application for patent published or deemed published under section 122(b), in which the patent or application, as the case may be, names another inventor and was effectively filed before the effective filing date of the claimed invention.
Claim(s) 1, 8, 11, and 16 is/are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 102(a)(2) as being anticipated by Firanski (US PgPub #2015/0008857).
For Claim 1, the figures of Firanski ‘857 disclose a system comprising: at least one microcontroller (804) in communication with at least one engine (800), wherein the at least one microcontroller is configured to: determine an angular position (706) of at least one inboard propeller attached to the at least one engine; send a signal (712) to halt rotation of the at least one inboard propeller such that the at least one inboard propeller is held in an attitude that provides for clearance of a propeller blade to the ground upon landing; and wherein the at least one inboard propeller would strike the ground during rotation of the at least one inboard propeller upon landing.
For Claim 8, the figures and paragraph [0030] of Firanski ‘857 disclose a position sensor (110 and 802) in communication with the microcontroller, wherein the position sensor detects a position of the at least one inboard propeller, and wherein the position is a Hall effect rotary position sensor.
For Claim 11, the figures of Firanski ‘857 disclose a method comprising: determining, by at least one microcontroller (804) in communication with at least one engine, an angular position (706) of at least one inboard propeller connected to the at least one engine; sending, by the at least one microcontroller, a signal to halt rotation of the at least one inboard propeller such that the at least one inboard propeller is held in an attitude that provides for clearance of a propeller blade to a ground upon landing; and wherein the at least one inboard propeller would strike the ground during rotation of the at least one inboard propeller upon landing.
For Claim 16, the figures of Firanski ‘857 disclose at least one microcontroller (804) in communication with at least one engine (800), wherein at least one inboard propeller is attached to the at least one engine, and wherein the at least one microcontroller is configured to determine an angular position of the at least one inboard propeller; and send a signal to the at least one inboard propeller such that they at least one inboard propeller is held in an attitude that provides for clearance of a propeller blade to a surface, wherein at least a portion of the at least one inboard propeller would contact the surface during rotation of the at least one inboard propeller.
Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 103
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 103 which forms the basis for all obviousness rejections set forth in this Office action:
A patent for a claimed invention may not be obtained, notwithstanding that the claimed invention is not identically disclosed as set forth in section 102, if the differences between the claimed invention and the prior art are such that the claimed invention as a whole would have been obvious before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which the claimed invention pertains. Patentability shall not be negated by the manner in which the invention was made.
Claim(s) 2-7, 10, 12-15, and 17 is/are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Firanski (US PgPub #2015/0008857) as applied to claims 1, 11, and 16 above, and further in view of Rawdon (US PgPub #2016/0144969).
For Claims 2-4 and 17, while Firanski ‘857 discloses a fuselage mounted propeller on the UAV and that the propeller system can be used on other flying aircrafts, it is silent about the UAV having inboard and outboard wing panels with propellers and engines attached to the panels.  However, figures 1A-1B of Rawdon ‘969 teach a UAV having an inner wing panel (12) having inboard propellers with engines (18) and outer wing panels (14 and 16) that disposed at an upward angle to a plane formed by the inner wing panel and having a tip propeller with engine disposed one the outer wing panel that are free to rotate without hitting the ground surface.  Therefore it would have been obvious to someone of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date to use the known propeller and engine system of Firanski ‘857 on a larger UAV as taught by Rawdon ‘969.  The motivation to do so would be to provide greater thrust with larger propeller blade diameters which allowing for the propellers to be controlled to eliminate ground contract and damage to the aircraft.
For Claim 5, while Firanski ‘857 teaches a propeller that would hit the ground and paragraph [0003] discloses that it is known to not have landing gear or having landing gear that is not sufficient to clear the propeller during landing, it is silent about having landing pods.  However, the figures of Rawdon ‘969 teach having landing pods (20).  Therefore it would have been obvious to someone of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date to modify Firanksi ‘857 with the landing pods of Rawdon ‘969.  The motivation to do so would be to use larger landing pods for greater stability.
For Claims 6-7, while Firanski ‘857 is silent about the UAV being a high altitude long endurance aircraft having solar arrays covering the wings, figures 1A-1B and the abstract of Rawdon ‘969 teaches a high endurance UAV that has solar panels covering the wings of the UAV so as to provide power to the UAV.  Therefore it would have been obvious to someone of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date to modify Firanski ‘857 with the long endurance solar powered UAV of Rawdon ‘969.  The motivation to do so would be to use clean energy for a more efficient aircraft.
For Claim 10, Firanski ‘857 discloses that the microcontroller sends a signal to start rotation of the propeller when the UAV is above the ground.
For Claims 12-15, while Firanski ‘857 discloses a fuselage mounted propeller on a UAV that is controlled by a microcontroller to halt rotation of the propeller or rotate the propeller based on the height or location of the UAV, the propeller system can be used on other flying aircrafts, it is silent about the UAV having inboard and outboard wing panels with propellers and engines attached to the panels.  However, figures 1A-1B of Rawdon ‘969 teach a UAV having an inner wing panel (12) having inboard propellers with engines (18) and outer wing panels (14 and 16)  that disposed at an upward angle to a plane formed by the inner wing panel and having an outboard propeller with engine disposed one the outer wing panel, the inboard and outboard propellers being identical.  Therefore it would have been obvious to someone of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date to use the known propeller and engine system of Firanski ‘857 on a larger UAV as taught by Rawdon ‘969.  The motivation to do so would be to provide greater thrust with larger propeller blade diameters which allowing for the propellers to be controlled to eliminate ground contract and damage to the aircraft.
Claim(s) 9 is/are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Firanski (US PgPub #2015/0008857) in view of Rawdon (US PgPub #2016/0144969) as applied to claims 2 and 17 above, and further in view of Rahrig (US Patent #10526069).
For Claims 9, 18, and 19, while the combination of Firanski ‘857 and Rawdon ‘969 teach controlling an inner propeller to halt to avoid contact of the ground, it is silent about adjusting other propellers when the inner propeller is halted.  However, figure 12 of Rahrig ‘069 teaches that it is known to halt rotation of the larger blade (200) that would hit the ground and to send a signal to rotate the smaller propeller (202) to provide thrust for takeoff and landing.  Therefore it would have been obvious to someone of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date to modify Firanski ‘857 and Rawdon ‘969 with the propeller control of Rahrig ‘069.  The motivation to do so would be to provide thrust for the aircraft during take off and landing while protecting the larger propeller that can be used once there is clearance to provide more efficient thrust.
For Claim 20, while Firanski ‘857 discloses a fuselage mounted propeller on the UAV and that the propeller system can be used on other flying aircrafts and teaches a propeller that would hit the ground and paragraph [0003] discloses that it is known to not have landing gear or having landing gear that is not sufficient to clear the propeller during landing, it is silent about the UAV having inboard and outboard wing panels with propellers and engines attached to the panels and having landing pods.  However, figures 1A-1B of Rawdon ‘969 teach a UAV having an inner wing panel (12) having inboard propellers with engines (18) and outer wing panels (14 and 16) that disposed at an upward angle to a plane formed by the inner wing panel and having a tip propeller with engine disposed one the outer wing panel that are free to rotate without hitting the ground surface and having landing pods (20).  Therefore it would have been obvious to someone of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date to use the known propeller and engine system and landing pods of Firanski ‘857 on a larger UAV as taught by Rawdon ‘969.  The motivation to do so would be to provide greater thrust with larger propeller blade diameters which allowing for the propellers to be controlled to eliminate ground contract and damage to the aircraft and to use larger landing pods for greater stability.
