Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 102
The following is a quotation of the appropriate paragraphs of 35 U.S.C. 102 that form the basis for the rejections under this section made in this Office action:
A person shall be entitled to a patent unless –
(a)(1) the claimed invention was patented, described in a printed publication, or in public use, on sale, or otherwise available to the public before the effective filing date of the claimed invention.
Claim(s) 1, and 7-11, are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 102a1 as being anticipated by Kelly et al. (US 20130018526 A1, published: 1/17/2013).
Claim 1:  Kelly teaches a computer-implemented method for detecting an obstacle on a route ahead of a first vehicle (ACATs use various types of sensors to detect obstacles in the path of the subject vehicle 650 [Kelly, 0034]), the method comprising the following steps:
recording information on a second vehicle driving ahead on a route in the first vehicle by at least one sensor of the first vehicle (while radar is a common sensor used in ACAT systems, several other sensors may be used in ACATs including lasers, sonar, and infra-red and visual image cameras [Kelly, 0002]);
detecting, by a computer in the first vehicle, depending on the recorded information (ACATs use various types of sensors [Kelly, 0034]), an avoidance maneuver of the second vehicle due to an obstacle or that the second vehicle has driven over the obstacle (ACATs use various types of sensors to detect obstacles in the path of the subject vehicle 650, and to alert the driver or take evasive action or some other action if the ACAT determines that the subject vehicle is likely to collide with such an obstacle [Kelly, 0034]),
detecting the obstacle on the route depending on the detected avoidance maneuver or the detection that the vehicle has driven over an obstacle (ACATs use various types of sensors to detect obstacles in the path of the subject vehicle 650, and to alert the driver or take evasive action or some other action if the ACAT determines that the subject vehicle is likely to collide with such an obstacle [Kelly, 0034]);
initiating a measure for protecting the first vehicle and/or the obstacle, depending on the detected obstacle (some ACAT systems may trigger an alarm or some other type of response if they detect something in the roadway as large as a DME 100 [Kelly, 0034]).
Claims 12 and 13, sharing similar deficiencies to claim 1, are likewise rejected.
Claim 7:  Kelly teaches the method as recited in claim 1.  Kelly further teaches wherein the second vehicle is a vehicle that is driving directly ahead of the first vehicle ([Kelly, FIG. 6C-6D]; Examiner's Note: as illustrated in the figures).
Claim 8:  Kelly teaches the method as recited in claim 1.  Kelly further teaches wherein information is recorded for a plurality of second vehicles, and the obstacle is detected on the route depending on the recorded information for the plurality of second vehicles (to achieve accurate results when testing ACATs against GSTs that simulate objects such as vehicles, pedestrians, or other objects, it is helpful to minimize the distortion of the sensor signature of the simulated vehicle, pedestrian, or other object that is caused by the presence of the DME 100 [Kelly, 0034]).
Claim 9:  Kelly teaches the method as recited in claim 1.  Kelly further teaches wherein the first vehicle monitors the second vehicle both for an avoidance maneuver due to the obstacle and to see if the second vehicle drives over the obstacle, and the obstacle being detected depending on the detected avoidance maneuver and the detection that the vehicle has driven over the obstacle (ACATs use various types of sensors to detect obstacles in the path of the subject vehicle 650, and to alert the driver or take evasive action or some other action if the ACAT determines that the subject vehicle is likely to collide with such an obstacle [Kelly, 0034]).
Claim 10:  Kelly teaches the method as recited in claim 1.  Kelly further teaches wherein the measure includes an automatic steering intervention, and/or an automatic braking intervention, and/or a warning signal to a driver of the first vehicle (the A-CMBS system was designed to alert the driver in the event of a likely collision and to mitigate the collision severity through automatic application of the brakes for imminent collisions [Kelly, 0058]).
Claim 11:  Kelly teaches the method as recited in claim 1.  Kelly further teaches wherein the at least one sensor includes an environment sensor, and the information on the second vehicle includes a camera image, and/or a LiDAR point cloud, and/or a radar signature, and/or audio information, and/or ultrasonic data (while radar is a common sensor used in ACAT systems, several other sensors may be used in ACATs including lasers, sonar, and infra-red and visual image cameras [Kelly, 0002]).
Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 103
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 103 which forms the basis for all obviousness rejections set forth in this Office action:
A patent for a claimed invention may not be obtained, notwithstanding that the claimed invention is not identically disclosed as set forth in section 102, if the differences between the claimed invention and the prior art are such that the claimed invention as a whole would have been obvious before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which the claimed invention pertains. Patentability shall not be negated by the manner in which the invention was made.
Claim(s) 2 and 4, are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Kelly et al. (US 20130018526 A1, published: 1/17/2013), in view of Lee et al. (US 20200255013 A1, published: 8/13/2020).
Claim 2:  Kelly teaches the method as recited in claim 1.  Kelly does not teach wherein the information on the second vehicle includes information on movements of the second vehicle.
However, Lee teaches wherein the information on the second vehicle includes information on movements of the second vehicle (the sensing module 200 may include a plurality of sensors to sense a vehicle around a host vehicle and may obtain information associated with a location of the vehicle around the host vehicle, a speed of the vehicle around the host vehicle, a movement direction of the vehicle around the host vehicle, and/or a type (e.g., a sedan, a compact car, a mid-size car, a full-size car, or the like) of the vehicle around the host vehicle [Lee, 0063]).
Therefore, it would have been obvious to a person of ordinary skill in the art, before the effective filing date of the claimed invention, to modify the obstacle detection and evasion invention of Kelly to include the information of the second evadable vehicle movement and direction features of Lee.
One would have been motivated to make this modification to use second vehicle movement and heading information to determine if that vehicle is an obstacle to a first vehicle.
Claim 4:  The combination of Kelly, and Lee, teaches the method as recited in claim 2.  Kelly further teaches further comprising: deducing a change to a relative position of the second vehicle in a lane or a yaw motion of the second vehicle from the movements, and the avoidance maneuver is detected depending on the deduced change or the deduced yaw rate (Example DME 100 subsystems may comprise the following components, among others, for instance: wireless LAN bridge; PC 104 computer; yaw rate sensor.  a DME computer such as a PC 104 computer that performs functions such as the following example functions: Guidance, Navigation and Control (GNC) computations; analog and digital data input and output; inputs, including: differential GPS information; electronic compass (heading angle); yaw rate [Kelly, 0045]).
Claim 3 is rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Kelly et al. (US 20130018526 A1, published: 1/17/2013) and Lee et al. (US 20200255013 A1, published: 8/13/2020), and in further view of Nakai et al. (US 20050169530 A1, published: 8/4/2005).
Claim 3:  The combination of Kelly, and Lee, teaches the method as recited in claim 2.  The combination of Kelly, and Lee, does not teach wherein the movements include at least one pitch motion, and the second vehicle driving over the obstacle is detected depending on the information on the pitch motion.
However, Nakai teaches wherein the movements include at least one pitch motion, and the second vehicle driving over the obstacle is detected depending on the information on the pitch motion (a vehicle in which at least two image pick-up devices each pick up an image of a preset common detection region, and which determines whether an obstacle is present or not in the detection region from the stereo images picked up using the image pick-up devices [Nakai, Claim 18].  The vehicle according to claim 18, wherein: the slope degree calculation means regards the slope angle as a pitch angle between the basic plane and the detection plane, and a parameter of an affine transformation matrix indicating a relationship between the corresponding points in the images or a unique value derived from the parameter as a slope degree [Nakai, Claim 19]).
Therefore, it would have been obvious to a person of ordinary skill in the art, before the effective filing date of the claimed invention, to modify the obstacle detection and evasion invention of the combination of Kelly, and Lee, to include the pitch information of the second evadable vehicle feature of Nakai.
One would have been motivated to make this modification to use second vehicle pitch movement to determine if that vehicle is an obstacle to a first vehicle.
Claim 5 is rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Kelly et al. (US 20130018526 A1, published: 1/17/2013), in view of Nugent et al. (US 20210129750 A1, published: 5/6/2021).
Claim 5:  Kelly teaches the method as recited in claim 1.  Kelly does not teach further comprising: determining, depending on the recorded information on the second vehicle, a location of the obstacle, the location including a distance between the first vehicle and the obstacle or a position of the obstacle in a lane, and the initiated measure depends on the determined location.
However, Nugent teaches further comprising: determining, depending on the recorded information on the second vehicle, a location of the obstacle, the location including a distance between the first vehicle and the obstacle or a position of the obstacle in a lane, and the initiated measure depends on the determined location (this method comprises mounting at least one sensor on a vehicle, wherein the vehicle includes a vehicle control system, and wherein the at least one sensor is in electrical communication with at least one processor located within the vehicle and powered by the vehicle, and wherein the at least one processor includes a global positioning system; determining a reference height, wherein the reference height is the height aboveground level at which the at least one sensor is mounted on the vehicle; inputting the reference height into the at least one processor; determining the height of the tallest portion of the vehicle above ground level; inputting the height of the tallest portion of the vehicle above ground level into the at least one processor; using the at least one sensor to measure the overhead distance between the lowest portion of an obstacle and the at least one sensor; and using the at least one processor to determine a measured height of the overhead obstacle, wherein the measured height of the overhead obstacle is the reference height added to the distance between the overhead obstacle and the at least one sensor. The method may further comprise using the global positioning system to mark the location and height of the overhead obstacle and then using a server in communication with the global positioning system to communicate the location and height of the overhead obstacle to other vehicles that are in communication with the server [Nugent, 0007]).
Therefore, it would have been obvious to a person of ordinary skill in the art, before the effective filing date of the claimed invention, to modify the obstacle detection and evasion invention of Kelly to include the location and distance information of the second evadable vehicle features of Nugent.
One would have been motivated to make this modification to use second vehicle location and distance information to determine if that vehicle is an obstacle to a first vehicle.
