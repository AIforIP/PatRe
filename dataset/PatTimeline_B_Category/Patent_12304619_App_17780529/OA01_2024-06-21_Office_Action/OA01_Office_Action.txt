Claim Rejections—35 USC §103
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 103 which forms the basis for all obviousness rejections set forth in this Office action:
A patent for a claimed invention may not be obtained, notwithstanding that the claimed invention is not identically disclosed as set forth in section 102, if the differences between the claimed invention and the prior art are such that the claimed invention as a whole would have been obvious before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which the claimed invention pertains. Patentability shall not be negated by the manner in which the invention was made.
Claim(s) 1 is/are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Leitl (AT 508 274) in view of Bunker (US 2017/0234134).
Regarding claim 1, Leitl teaches a method for applying an aerodynamically functional film 2 to a body 1 of complex structure which is moved in a fluid medium such as a gas (Abstract; Fig. 1-2; [0003-0006, 0011, 0013, 0015, 0050-0051, 0053]), the method comprising: determining flow conditions on a surface of the body 1 around which flow occurs for a predetermined surrounding flow state on a basis of a computer model of the body using a numerical flow simulation (Fig. 2; [0017, 0022, 0024, 0038-0039, 0058-0059]); developing at least one surface segment of the computer model to form a two-dimensional surface, i.e., modeling the variation of local flow conditions across the overall surface in two dimensions (Fig. 2; [0009, 0011, 0015, 0059]); locating at least one coherent field in the developed surface segment, i.e., modeling a continuous region thereof without internal gaps (Fig. 2; [0024, 0058-0059, 0066]); and applying the aerodynamically functional film 2 to the body 1 around which flow occurs within the field boundary of the at least one field located in the developed surface segment in such a way that the aerodynamically functional film 1 is aligned along a main flow direction (i.e., longitudinally) of the respective field [0006, 0009, 0011, 0013, 0015, 0033-0034, 0036-0037].
Note: In light of the instant specification (paragraphs [0020] and [0027]), the term “complex flow” is treated herein as referring to a flow profile along a surface section which cannot be reasonably approximated by a parallel flow profile, and the term “coherent field” is treated herein as referring to a continuous region without internal gaps.
Leitl teaches that the body 1 has a complex structure/geometry, such as that of an aircraft (Abstract; Fig. 1-2; [0003-0006, 0011, 0013, 0015, 0050-0051, 0053]).  While Leitl does not specifically describe the resulting flow around this complex structure as being a complex flow in the sense of having a non-parallel flow profile, it is well known in the art that the flow profiles that develop around such complex structures as aircraft and airfoils may become significantly non-parallel, as evidenced by Bunker (Abstract; Fig. 10; [0058-0059]).  Further, while Leitl likewise does not specifically teach that the computer model determines flow lines on a surface of the body 1 around which flow occurs, and thus that the at least one coherent field in the developed surface segment has local flow directions readable at the flow lines in a predetermined angle range around a main flow direction of the respective field, Bunker teaches that, by determining the variation of flow lines’ local directions (“flowpath surfaces”) upon an aerodynamic body and then designing an aerodynamically functional surface (riblets 128) to follow these local flow directions, greater reduction in drag can be achieved (Fig. 10; [0058-0061]).  Thus, it would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art to determine flow lines and the variation of flow directions thereof in the method of Leitl as claimed, in order to achieve the reduction in drag taught by Bunker.
Claim(s) 2-5 and 11-12 is/are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Leitl and Bunker as applied to claims 1 above, and further in view of either one of Berschback et al. (US 2017/0167510) or Rawlings et al. (US 2011/0073710).
Regarding claim 2, Leitl teaches that the alignment of the aerodynamically functional film 2 in the field takes place on the basis of the alignment of the main flow direction on the body 1, i.e., that the ribs/riblets of the aerodynamically functional film 2 are aligned longitudinally with the main flow direction [0006, 0009, 0011, 0013, 0015, 0033-0034, 0036-0037].  While Leitl does not specifically depict a plan view of the aerodynamically functional film 2 in place on the body 1, it is well known in the art to apply such films within a field boundary that is also selected in accordance with alignment lines identifiable on the body, i.e., such that side edges of the film are also aligned longitudinally with the main flow direction, as evidenced by Berschback et al. (Abstract; Fig. 7; [0039]) or Rawlings et al. (Abstract; Fig. 1; [0035]), such that it would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art to further align the aerodynamically functional film 2 of Leitl in this manner.
Regarding claim 3, it is further well known in the art to identify alignment lines on a basis of structural features of the surface of the body (which influence the flow around the body), such as the fore-aft axis of an aircraft fuselage or the root of an airfoil, and to orient applied aerodynamically functional films in accordance with these alignment lines, as evidenced by Berschback et al. (Abstract; Fig. 3, 7; [0031-0032, 0039]) or Rawlings et al. (Abstract; Fig. 1; [0035]).  Thus, it would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art to map these flow-influencing structural features in the computer model and to further align the aerodynamically functional film 2 of Leitl in this manner.
Regarding claim 4, Leitl teaches that the alignment of the aerodynamically functional film 2 in the field takes place on the basis of the alignment of the main flow direction on the body 1, i.e., that the ribs/riblets of the aerodynamically functional film 2 are aligned longitudinally with the main flow direction [0006, 0009, 0011, 0013, 0015, 0033-0034, 0036-0037].  While Leitl does not specifically depict a plan view of the aerodynamically functional film 2 in place on the body 1, it is well known in the art to apply such films within a field boundary that is also selected in accordance with an alignment line, i.e., such that side edges of the film are also aligned in parallel to the main flow direction, as evidenced by Berschback et al. (Abstract; Fig. 7; [0039]) or Rawlings et al. (Abstract; Fig. 1; [0035]), such that it would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art to further align the aerodynamically functional film 2 of Leitl in this manner.
Regarding claim 5, Leitl does not specifically depict a plan view of the aerodynamically functional film 2.  However, it is well known in the art to configure such aerodynamically functional films to have field boundaries that are straight lines, thereby enabling, for instance, consistent tessellation of the films, as evidenced by Berschback et al. (Abstract; Fig. 7; [0039]) or Rawlings et al. (Abstract; Fig. 1; [0035]).  Thus, it would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art to select field boundaries of the at least one field on the developed surface segment of Leitl to be straight lines, and to produce an aerodynamically functional film 2 of corresponding shape.
Regarding claim 11, Leitl teaches that the aerodynamically functional film 2 has a riblet structure [0006].
Regarding claim 12, it is well known in the art to configure aerodynamically functional films like that of Leitl to form a closed traverse having at most, for instance, 4 sides, thereby enabling, for instance, consistent tessellation of the films, as evidenced by Berschback et al. (Abstract; Fig. 7; [0039]) or Rawlings et al. (Abstract; Fig. 1; [0035]).  Thus, it would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art to select field boundaries of the at least one field on the developed surface segment of Leitl to have at most 4 sides, and to produce an aerodynamically functional film 2 of corresponding shape.
Claim(s) 6 is/are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Leitl, Bunker, and either one of Berschback et al. or Rawlings et al. as applied to claims 2-5 and 11-12 above, and further in view of the document “Processing Guidelines: ORAFOL® Films for Aircraft Applications.”
Regarding claim 6, Leitl does not specifically teach trimming the aerodynamically functional film 2.  However, it is well known in the art that functional films applied to aircraft like that taught by Leitl frequently require trimming around structural features such as windows, as evidenced by ORAFOL (p. 1, “Area of Use and Limitations”; p. 2, “Application – General Information”).  Furthermore, ORAFOL teaches that, by applying knifeless tapes around the boundaries of such features before applying the functional films, the films can be precisely trimmed to the necessary shape without the risk of cutting or scratching the aircraft skin with sharp tools (p. 2, “Required Tools and Materials”, “Application – General Information”; pp. 3-4, “Film Cutting on Aircraft Bodies”).  Thus, it would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art to apply one or more knifeless tapes along the alignment line of at least one field boundary of the field on the body 1 of Leitl in view of Bunker before application of the aerodynamically functional film 2, then to trim the aerodynamically functional film 2, in order to safely avoid covering significant structural features of the aircraft as taught by ORAFOL.

Regarding claim 7, Leitl teaches that even better results in drag reduction can be achieved by locally calculating the flow characteristics across smaller areas of the surface of the body 1 and applying a corresponding plurality of locally tailored aerodynamically functional films 2 [0040]. Thus, it would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art to apply the aerodynamically functional film 2 of Leitl in view of Bunker in at least one field in the form of film sections like as tiles, in order to achieve greater drag reduction using multiple films each optimized for local flow conditions.
