Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 103
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 103 which forms the basis for all obviousness rejections set forth in this Office action:
A patent for a claimed invention may not be obtained, notwithstanding that the claimed invention is not identically disclosed as set forth in section 102, if the differences between the claimed invention and the prior art are such that the claimed invention as a whole would have been obvious before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which the claimed invention pertains. Patentability shall not be negated by the manner in which the invention was made.
Claim(s) 1-9, 12, 13, and 19-21 is/are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Sato (US PgPub #2022/0274693) in view of Marshall (US PgPub #2023/0339601).
For Claims 1-2, figures 2 and 9-12 of Sato ‘693 disclose an aircraft, comprising: a plurality of vertical lift propulsors assemblies (2A), each of the plurality of vertical lift propulsor assemblies comprising: a propulsor (3A) coupled to the aircraft and driven by an electric motor (21A) including a rotor and a stator (22A), the propulsor including a hub and a plurality of blades each extending outward from the hub, the hub being coupled to a shaft that is driven by the rotor, the hub and blades being moved to as to be nit individually adjustable; and a cyclic control assembly comprising an actuator (56A); and a push rod (57A), wherein the push rod is coupled to the actuator and the propulsor; the push rod is configured to increase a blade angle of the propulsor in response to a displacement of the push rod in a first direction; and the push rod is configured to decrease the blade angle of the propulsor in response to a displacement of the push rod in a second direction.
While Sato ‘693 discloses the push rod external to a shaft coupling the rotor to the propulsor, the shaft mechanically coupling the rotor and the propulsor.  The shaft having a proximal end; and a distal end mechanically coupled to the propulsor.  Sato ‘693 is silent about the shaft being within the shaft and the hub and the blades being formed as a single element.  However, figures 8-13B of Marshal ‘601 teach a cyclic control assembly that has a push rod (1242) extending through a shaft (1272), wherein the shaft has a central lumen extending from a proximal end to a distal end, and wherein the push rod extends through the central lumen, the shaft being able to move the hub and blades that are a single element so as to not be individually adjustable.  Therefore it would have been obvious to someone of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date to modify Sato ‘693 with the internal push rod of Rock ‘777.  The motivation to do so would be to create and internal structure where the push rod is protected by the shaft and to have less parts.
For Claim 3, figures 2 and 9-12 of Sato ‘693 disclose that the push rod is oriented in parallel to a rotational axis of the rotor of the electric motor.
For Claim 4, figures 2 and 9-12 of Sato ‘693 disclose that the cyclic control assembly further comprises a swashplate rocker (571A), wherein the swashplate rocker mechanically connects the push rod and the actuator.
For Claim 5, figures 2 and 9-12 of Sato ‘693 disclose that the swashplate rocker is configured to translate movement by the actuator in a first actuation direction into movement of the push rod in the first direction and translate movement by the actuator in a second actuation direction into movement of the push rod in the second direction.
For Claim 6, figures 2 and 9-12 of Sato ‘693 disclose that the cyclic control assembly further comprises a propulsor rocker (544A), wherein the propulsor rocker is mechanically connected to the push rod and the propulsor.
For Claim 7, figures 2 and 9-12 of Sato ‘693 disclose that the propulsor rocker is configured to: translate movement by the push rod in the first direction to an increase in the blade angle of the propulsor; and translate movement by the push rod in the second direction to a decrease in the blade angle of the propulsor.
For Claim 8, figures 2 and 9-12 of Sato ‘693 disclose that the propulsor rocker comprises a first propulsor rocker end and a second propulsor rocker end; the second propulsor rocker end is attached to the push rod; and the first propulsor rocker end is attached to a propulsor link.
For Claim 9, figures 2 and 9-12 and paragraph [0043-0044] of Sato ‘693 disclose a flight controller, wherein the flight controller is communicatively connected to the actuator; and the flight controller is configured to: command the actuator to increase the and decrease the blade angle of the propulsor.
For Claim 12, figures 2 and 9-12 of Sato ‘693 disclose that the plurality of blades comprises two blades.
For Claim 13, While Sato ‘693 discloses an electric motor with a stator and a rotor, it is silent about having a second one.  It would have been obvious to one having ordinary skill in the art at the time the invention was made to have a secondary motor, since it has been held that mere duplication of the essential working parts of a device involves only routine skill in the art. St. Regis Paper Co. v. Bemis Co., 193 USPQ 8.
For Claim 19, while Sato ‘693 discloses a first actuator, it is silent about a second actuator  coupled to the push rod.  However, figure 11 of Rock ‘777 teaches a first and second actuator (272 and 282) coupled to the push rod and configured to rotate the push rod about a transverse axis.  Therefore it would have been obvious to someone of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date to modify Sato ‘693 with the second actuator of Rock ‘777.  The motivation to do so is to provide another input for greater control.
For Claim 20, paragraphs [0042-0044] of Sato ‘693 disclose a flight controller; and remote pilot controls, wherein the flight controller is in communication with the flight controller, such that the flight controller generates commands for actuation of the actuator in response to inputs received from the plurality of pilot controls.
For Claim 21, figures 2 and 9-12 of Sato ‘693 disclose that the first and second directions are substantially parallel to a rotational axis of the electric motor and the propulsor, and to an orientation of the push rod.
For Claim 22, figures 2 and 9-12 of Sato ‘693 disclose that a reference plane corresponding to a chord of the propulsor is substantially orthogonal to the rotational axis, and wherein the blade angle of the propulsor is changed relative to the reference plane of the propulsor in response to movement of the push rod in the first or second direction.
Claim(s) 14-18 is/are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Sato (US PgPub #2022/0274693) in view of Marshall (US PgPub #2023/0339601) as applied to claim 1 above, and further in view of Howes (US PgPub #2022/0153407).
For Claims 14-16, while Sato ‘693 is silent about a passive flap mechanism, the figures of Howes ‘407 teach a flap mechanism (3c) connected to the propulsor, wherein  the flap mechanism is configured to allow the propulsor to rotate about a pivot point of the flap mechanism, wherein the rotor is mechanically connected to the propulsor such that the rotor and the propulsor are configured to rotate about a rotational axis, and wherein the flap mechanism comprises: a base rotatably attached to the aircraft and configured to rotate about the rotational axis; and a hinge coupling the base and the hub of the propulsor wherein the hinge is configured to allow the propulsor to rotate about the pivot point of the flap mechanism; the flap mechanism is configured to passively control in-flight transients.  Therefore it would have been obvious to someone of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing to modify Sato ‘693 with the passive flap mechanism of Howes ‘407.  The motivation to so would be to provide passive flapping to increases stability of the rotor.
For Claims 17-18, while Howes ‘407 teaches elastomeric bearings (EB) that are used to lock or prevent movement of the flap mechanism at certain forces or positions it is silent about using springs.  However, the Examiner takes Official Notice that it is well known in the art to use a variety of elastic devices including bearings, springs, etc. to dampen and mechanically control the movement of the rotors.  Therefore it would have been obvious to someone of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date to modify Sato ‘693 in view of Howes ‘407 with a spring as a known dampening device and equivalent of an elastic bearing.
