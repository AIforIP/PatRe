Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 103
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 103 which forms the basis for all obviousness rejections set forth in this Office action:
A patent for a claimed invention may not be obtained, notwithstanding that the claimed invention is not identically disclosed as set forth in section 102, if the differences between the claimed invention and the prior art are such that the claimed invention as a whole would have been obvious before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which the claimed invention pertains. Patentability shall not be negated by the manner in which the invention was made.
Claims 1, 8, 15, and 19 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Park (US 2022/0332319 A1) in view of Shalev-Shwartz et al. (US 2021/0078570 A1), hereinafter referred to as Shalev.
Regarding claim 1:
Park discloses the following limitations:
“An apparatus for preventing car collision, the apparatus comprising: a communication unit configured to communicate with a preceding vehicle or a camera.” (Park ¶ 28 discloses “a vehicle including: a communicator configured to communicate with a plurality of other vehicle.” As shown in Park FIG. 7B, the plurality of vehicles can include a second vehicle 2 that travels in front of the ego vehicle 1. This at least teaches a communication unit configured to communicate with a preceding vehicle as claimed.)
“and a processor operatively connected to the communication unit.” (Park ¶¶ 150 and 219: “the cruise control device may correspond to a second controller, and may communicate with … a communicator 250,” where “The second controller 280 may be implemented as a single processor.”)
“wherein the processor is configured to: obtain information about a third vehicle in front of the preceding vehicle acquired by the preceding vehicle.” (Park FIG. 7B shows a third vehicle 3 in front of the second vehicle 2. Further, Park ¶ 274 discloses that “The vehicle may also determine whether the third vehicle is in a stationary state from the travel information and position information of the third vehicle received by the communicator 250.”)
“calculate a deceleration amount for preventing a collision with the third vehicle based on the information about the third vehicle.” (Park ¶ 275: “As shown in FIG. 7B, the vehicle may determine whether there is an obstacle that is stopped in the travelling path, and upon determining that there is an obstacle that is stopped in the path, acquire a target deceleration based on position information of the obstacle in the stationary state and the travelling velocity information of the first vehicle, and control deceleration and braking based on the acquired target deceleration.”)
“predict a preset event related to the preceding vehicle based on a change in a position of the preceding vehicle.” (Park ¶¶ 262-263: “as shown in FIG. 7A, the vehicle may, upon determining that the second vehicle 2 is not detected through the obstacle detector 220, determine that the path of the second vehicle 2 is suddenly changed (cut out). The sudden path change may include a state in which the distance to the obstacle acquired based on obstacle information detected by the obstacle detector changes to a degree greater than or equal to a reference distance within a predetermined time.”)
“and control an autonomous driving vehicle based on the deceleration amount according to a prediction result regarding the preset event.” (Park ¶¶ 267-268: “the vehicle decelerates and performs braking while maintaining the cruise control mode based on the travel information of the third vehicle in front of the vehicle during execution of the cruise control mode, so that the cruise control mode is prevented from being deactivated, thereby eliminating a hassle of the driver in resetting the cruise control mode. As described above, even when the first vehicle 1 controls acceleration because the second vehicle 2 travelling in front of the first vehicle departs from the front of the first vehicle 1, the first vehicle 1 obtains the amount of acceleration in consideration of the travel information of the third vehicle 3 travelling in front of the second vehicle 2, so that the first vehicle 1 is prevented from colliding with the third vehicle 3.”)
Note that under the broadest reasonable interpretation (BRI) of claim 1, consistent with the specification, the communication unit being “configured to communicate with a preceding vehicle or a camera” is being treated as an alternative limitation. Applicant has elected to use the word “or” in the claim language, and therefore, the BRI covers the scenario in which only one of the limitations applies. Accordingly, while only communicating with a preceding vehicle has been addressed here, the claim is still rejected in its entirety.
The following limitations are not specifically disclosed by Park, but are taught by Shalev:
“wherein the processor detects the change in the position of the preceding vehicle and predicts occurrence of the preset event using an event prediction model based on the detected change in the position of the preceding vehicle.” (Shalev ¶ 189: “If the distance between the leading vehicle's instantaneous position and the look-ahead point varies during the specific period of time, and the cumulative sum of variation exceeds a predetermined threshold (for example, 0.3 to 0.4 meters on a straight road, 0.7 to 0.8 meters on a moderately curvy road, and 1.3 to 1.7 meters on a road with sharp curves), processing unit 110 may determine that the leading vehicle is likely changing lanes.” The leading vehicle changing lanes reads on the “preset event” as claimed.)
“and wherein the event prediction model is trained based on information related to the detected change in the position of the preceding vehicle using a machine learning algorithm.” (Shalev ¶ 190: “At step 584, processing unit 110 may determine whether or not leading vehicle 200 is changing lanes based on the analysis performed at step 582. … the analysis may make use of trained system (e.g., a machine learning or deep learning system), which may, for example, estimate a future path ahead of a current location of a vehicle based on an image captured at the current location.”)
Before the effective filing date of the claimed invention, it would have been obvious to a person having ordinary skill in the art to modify the system of Park by using a trained machine learning model to predict a lane change of the preceding vehicle based on a detected change in its position as taught by Shalev with a reasonable expectation of success. A person having ordinary skill in the art could have been motivated to do this because Shalev ¶ 387 teaches that this modification “may help ensure that from a planning perspective there will be no accident of the AV's own responsibility.” A person having ordinary skill in the art would have recognized that using a machine learning model to predict a preceding vehicle’s lane change would help automate the process and allow the host vehicle to brake sooner and less sharply in response to the predicted lane change.

Claims 2-3, 6, 9-10, and 13 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Park in view of Shalev as applied to claims 1 and 8 above, and further in view of Grace et al. (US 2021/0304607 A1), hereinafter referred to as Grace.
Regarding claim 2:
The combination of Park and Shalev teaches “The apparatus of claim 1,” but does not specifically disclose “wherein, when it is not possible to obtain, from the preceding vehicle, the information about the third vehicle in front of the preceding vehicle, the processor obtains the information about the third vehicle in front of the preceding vehicle detected by one or more cameras installed on a road on which the autonomous driving vehicle is traveling.” However, Grace does teach this limitation. (Grace ¶ 69 discloses that data can be obtained about a static obstacle such as a bus 610. Further, Grace ¶ 86: “Turning back to the step 1006, upon negative determination in the step 1006 (e.g., when it is determined that there is no perception unit X in the vicinity of AV1 and/or when it is determined that there is no perception unit X that is capable of providing information indicative of the environment surrounding AV1), the method 1000 may proceed with a step 1016 that includes the vehicle management system 140 determining whether a further perception unit X capable of obtaining information indicative of the environment surrounding AV1 can be deployed.” Also, Grace ¶¶ 33 and 56-57: “the sensor suite 120 may include a camera 210,” and “The sensor suite 520 may be substantially similar to the sensor suite 120 of the AV 110… The perception analysis module [530] may be configured to process the perception data gathered by the sensor suite 520 of the infrastructure object 150.”)
Before the effective filing date of the claimed invention, it would have been obvious to a person having ordinary skill in the art to modify the system disclosed by the combination of Park and Shalev by using infrastructure image data when a vehicle perception unit is not capable of providing information to the autonomous vehicle as is taught by Grace with a reasonable expectation of success. A person having ordinary skill in the art could have been motivated to do this because Grace ¶¶ 84-85 teach that with this modification, the autonomous vehicle can adjust its driving strategy, resulting in safer behavior.
Regarding claim 3:
The combination of Park, Shalev, and Grace teaches “The apparatus of claim 2,” and Grace further teaches “wherein the information about the third vehicle in front of the preceding vehicle includes at least one piece of information among the group of information about a distance between the preceding vehicle and the third vehicle in front of the preceding vehicle and information about a lane in which the third vehicle in front of the preceding vehicle is located.” (Grace ¶ 69: “with reference to the setting shown in FIG. 6, the complexity score computed based on the sensor data of the AV 110b may be indicative of … the lane direction of the bus 610.” This at least teaches the information including “information about a lane in which the vehicle stopped in front of the preceding vehicle is located” as claimed.)
Note that under the BRI of claim 3, consistent with the specification, the stopped vehicle information including “at least one piece of information among the group of information about a distance between the preceding vehicle and the third vehicle in front of the preceding vehicle and information about a lane in which the third vehicle in front of the preceding vehicle is located” is treated as an alternative limitation. Applicant has elected to use the phrase “at least one” in the claim language, and therefore, the BRI covers the scenario in which only one of the limitations applies. Accordingly, while only the “information about a lane in which the third vehicle in front of the preceding vehicle is located” has been addressed here, the claim is still rejected in its entirety.
Before the effective filing date of the claimed invention, it would have been obvious to a person having ordinary skill in the art to modify the system disclosed by the combination of Park and Shalev by obtaining information about which lane the obstacle is located in as is taught by Grace, because modification amounts to a combination of prior art elements according to known methods to yield predictable results (see MPEP 2143(I)(A)). Obtaining data indicative of a lane in which an obstacle is located would have predictably functioned similarly whether done within the vehicle collaborative perception system of Grace or whether integrated into the advanced driver assistance system disclosed by the combination of Park and Shalev. A person having ordinary skill in the art would have recognized that identifying a lane of an obstacle would be beneficial since it would help the vehicle to avoid colliding with the obstacle.
Regarding claim 9:
Claim 9 is rejected with the same rationale applied to claim 2 above, mutatis mutandis.
Regarding claims 6, 10, and 13:
Claims 6, 10, and 13 are each rejected with the same rationale applied to claim 3 above, mutatis mutandis.
Claims 5 and 12 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Park in view of Shalev as applied to claims 1 and 8 above, and further in view of Kato et al. (US 2020/0247417 A1), hereinafter referred to as Kato.
Regarding claim 5:
The combination of Park and Shalev teaches “The apparatus of claim 1,” and Shalev further teaches “wherein the preset event includes an event where one side of the preceding vehicle is positioned at a point in an adjacent lane of either side of a lane in which the preceding vehicle is traveling.” (Shalev ¶ 190: “processing unit 110 may determine whether or not leading vehicle 200 is changing lanes.”)
Before the effective filing date of the claimed invention, it would have been obvious to a person having ordinary skill in the art to modify the system of Park by predicting that a leading vehicle will move into an adjacent lane as taught by Shalev, because this modification amounts to a combination of prior art elements according to known methods to yield predictable results (see MPEP 2143(I)(A)). Predicting whether the leading vehicle is changing lanes would have predictably functioned similarly whether done within the vehicle navigation system of Shalev or whether integrated into the advanced driver assistance system of Park. A person having ordinary skill in the art would have recognized the importance of detecting a lane change of the preceding vehicle in order to prevent collisions with the preceding vehicles or other obstacles in the road.
The combination of Park and Shalev does not specifically teach “the point being separated by a distance exceeding 1/3 of a total width of the preceding vehicle from any one lane marking of a corresponding side of the lane in which the preceding vehicle is traveling.” However, Kato does teach this limitation. (Kato ¶ 60: “the calculation unit 54 determines whether the lane change is completed. For example, in a case where a half or more of the host vehicle 10 in a vehicle width direction is positioned in the adjacent lane 78, the calculation unit 54 can determine that the lane change is completed.” This teaches the claim limitation since half the width of the vehicle is “a distance exceeding 1/3 of a total width of the preceding vehicle” as claimed.)
Before the effective filing date of the claimed invention, it would have been obvious to a person having ordinary skill in the art to modify the system disclosed by the combination of Park and Shalev by using a threshold vehicle width such as half the vehicle width when predicting a lane change occurrence as taught by Kato, because this amounts to a combination of prior art elements according to known methods to yield predictable results (see MPEP 2143(I)(A)). Using a threshold width when identifying a lane change occurrence would have predictably functioned similarly whether done within the vehicle control system of Kato or whether integrated into the advanced driver assistance system that is disclosed by the combination of Park and Shalev. A person having ordinary skill in the art would have recognized that a vehicle is unlikely to cross halfway into an adjacent lane unless it intends to change lanes, and that using a threshold vehicle width would provide an objective, quantifiable measure of lane changing that could easily be utilized by an autonomous vehicle system.
Regarding claim 12:
Claim 12 is rejected with the same rationale applied to claim 5 above, mutatis mutandis.
Claims 16 and 20 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Park in view of Shalev as applied to claims 1 and 8 above, and further in view of Schmudderich et al. (US 2013/0054106 A1), hereinafter referred to as Schmudderich.
Regarding claim 16:
The combination of Park and Shalev teaches “The apparatus of claim 1,” and Park further teaches “wherein the processor performs deceleration control of the autonomous driving vehicle based on the deceleration amount, at a point in time at which the processor determines that the preset event will occur based on the prediction result of the event prediction model.”
The combination of Park and Shalev does not specifically teach to perform deceleration control “before a point in time at which the preset event actually occurs.” However, this limitation is taught by Schmudderich. (Schmudderich ¶ 7: “the invention targets at predicting the target object's movement behavior before it actually performs a cutting-in or cutting-out maneuver. The prediction allows the host vehicle to either decelerate earlier and thus longer preventing under-runs of the safety distance or to accelerate earlier and thus allow for quicker overtaking process, or both.”)
Before the effective filing date of the claimed invention, it would have been obvious to a person having ordinary skill in the art to modify the system disclosed by the combination of Park and Shalev by allowing the host vehicle to decelerate before the preceding vehicle performs its lane change maneuver as taught by Schmudderich with a reasonable expectation of success. A person having ordinary skill in the art could have been motivated to do this because Schmudderich ¶ 7 teaches that this allows the host vehicle to decelerate earlier and longer to prevent coming within an unsafe distance of the preceding vehicle.
Regarding claim 20:
Claim 20 is rejected with the same rationale applied to claim 16 above, mutatis mutandis.
Claims 18 and 22 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Park in view of Shalev as applied to claims 1 and 20 above, and further in view of Xue et al. (US 2022/0375349 A1), hereinafter referred to as Xue.
Regarding claim 18:
The combination of Park and Shalev teaches “The apparatus of claim 1,” but does not specifically teach “wherein the processor predicts that the preset event will occur based on data regarding a probability of the occurrence of the preset event generated by the trained event prediction model.” However, Xue does teach this limitation. (Xue ¶ 5 discloses “receiving a velocity and a position of the target vehicle; respectively obtaining, based on the velocity and the position of the target vehicle, a first lane-changing probability and a second lane-changing probability of the target vehicle by using a first machine learning model and a second machine learning model; and determining a possibility of lane changing of the target vehicle based on the first lane-changing probability and the second lane-changing probability.”)
Before the effective filing date of the claimed invention, it would have been obvious to a person having ordinary skill in the art to modify the system disclosed by the combination of Park and Shalev by predicting a probability of a preceding vehicle changing lanes as taught by Xue with a reasonable expectation of success. A person having ordinary skill in the art could have been motivated to do this because Xue ¶ 3 teaches that a preceding vehicle changing lanes “poses a significant threat to road traffic safety. Therefore, the prediction of a lane-changing intention of vehicles on two sides of a present vehicle on a road can effectively prevent traffic incidents from happening and improve the driving safety.”
Regarding claim 22:
Claim 22 is rejected with the same rationale applied to claim 18 above, mutatis mutandis.
