Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 103
In the event the determination of the status of the application as subject to AIA  35 U.S.C. 102 and 103 (or as subject to pre-AIA  35 U.S.C. 102 and 103) is incorrect, any correction of the statutory basis for the rejection will not be considered a new ground of rejection if the prior art relied upon, and the rationale supporting the rejection, would be the same under either status.
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 103 which forms the basis for all obviousness rejections set forth in this Office action:
A patent for a claimed invention may not be obtained, notwithstanding that the claimed invention is not identically disclosed as set forth in section 102, if the differences between the claimed invention and the prior art are such that the claimed invention as a whole would have been obvious before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which the claimed invention pertains. Patentability shall not be negated by the manner in which the invention was made.
The factual inquiries for establishing a background for determining obviousness under 35 U.S.C. 103 are summarized as follows:
1. Determining the scope and contents of the prior art.
2. Ascertaining the differences between the prior art and the claims at issue.
3. Resolving the level of ordinary skill in the pertinent art.
4. Considering objective evidence present in the application indicating obviousness or nonobviousness.

It would be obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to modify Wyman to have a set of UV LEDs affixed to inner surface of the curved enclosure, as taught by Marshall, as an alternative means of placing the UV LEDs so that they are in a position to sterilize a handle.
Wyman modified by Marshall does not teach
at least one switch placed on at least one of the first surface and a second surface of the enclosed area, wherein each of the at least one switch is activated in a closed state of the enclosed area and each of the at least one switch is deactivated in an open state of the enclosed area,
wherein the controller is communicatively coupled to the at least one switch, and the at least one locking mechanism and wherein the controller is configured to activate the set of UV LEDs, when each of the at least one switch is activated and the locking mechanism engages the first surface in the closed state
Mackin teaches a UV sterilization system for a foldable tray table that only operates when the tray table is in a folded position (abstract: A system (100) for sanitizing a tray table (102) may include a sanitation assembly (108) operatively coupled to the tray table (102)… The sanitation control unit (114) operates the UV light source (112) to emit the UV light onto the tray table (102) when the tray table (102) is secured in an upright position, and prevents the UV light source (112) from emitting the UV light when the tray table (102) is not secured in the upright position). Mackin teaches a lock mechanism that is paired with a rotation switch so that unlocking the mechanism would deactivate the UV lights (par. 59: Notably, some tray tables may include a rotatable latch. In connection with such trays, the position of the latch may be sensed. Rotation of the latch out of a locked position may automatically deactivate the UV light source). Thus, Mackin implicitly teaches a switch located on the tray table (alternately interpreted as either the first or second surface) since the rotation of the latch is sensed. The state of the switch when detecting a locked position (a closed state of the tray table, since it is enclosing the space between it and the frame) is interpreted be activated, and the state of the switch when detecting an unlocked position (an open state of the tray table) is interpreted to be deactivated. Furthermore, Mackin teaches wherein a controller is configured to detect whether or not the tray table is in a locked state by being connected to one or more sensors, in order to activate the set of UV LEDs only when the tray table is in a locked state (par. 30: In response to the sanitation control unit 114 detecting that the tray table 102 is securely stowed in the upright position, such as through signals detected by the tray table position sensor 116, the sanitation control unit 114 may transition to a standby mode, in which a sanitation cycle may be activated). Under the broadest reasonable interpretation, the latch sensor and thus the lock mechanism would be connected to the controller in order its signal to be detected for determining whether or not to activate the UV LEDs.
Therefore, it would be obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to modify the first or second surface of Wyman modified by Marshall to have a switch, wherein each of the at least one switch is activated in a closed state of the enclosed area and each of the at least one switch is deactivated in an open state of the enclosed area, and wherein the controller is communicatively coupled to the at least one switch, and by extension, the at least one locking mechanism, and wherein the controller is configured to activate the set of UV LEDs, when each of the at least one switch is activated and the locking mechanism engages the first surface in the closed state, in order to automate UV sterilization to happen when the device is not in use.

Allowable Subject Matter
Claim 5 is objected to as being dependent upon a rejected base claim, but would be allowable if rewritten in independent form including all of the limitations of the base claim and any intervening claims.
Regarding claim 5, Wyman modified by Marshall and Mackin teaches the aircraft sanitization device of claim 4, as set forth above, and teaches wherein the controller is communicatively coupled to the rotating mechanism (par. 68: For example, when the user grasps door handle 706, the UV lights are turned off. After the user uses and subsequently releases door handle 706, doors 730 and 732 close; NOTE: since the controller controls the operations of the device, implicitly it must also control the door 732 in order for the closing of the door to be automated) but does not teach and is further configured to:
instruct the rotating mechanism to move the curved enclosure to the first position, when each of the at least one switch is activated and the locking mechanism engages the first surface in the closed state; and
instruct the rotating mechanism to move the curved enclosure to one of the second position and the at least one intermediate position, when at least one of the at least one switch is deactivated and the locking mechanism disengages the first surface.
This is because it would be counter-intuitive to close the curved enclosure right after the first surface is unlocked and opened, since the latter would require a hand to touch the handle, and the hand would be in the way of the curved enclosure at that moment.
The closest prior art is Pasch (US 20120176241 A1), which teaches a similar rotatable enclosure for sterilizing a door handle (Fig. 7a-7b) and teaches a method for sterilizing the door handle wherein the rotation of the enclosure is timed with the locking of the door (Fig. 3). However, Pasch teaches wherein the enclosure is closed when the door is closed, and wherein the enclosure opens after a sterilization cycle while the door is still closed.
