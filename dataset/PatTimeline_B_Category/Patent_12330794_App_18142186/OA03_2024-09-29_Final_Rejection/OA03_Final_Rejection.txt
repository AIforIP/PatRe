Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 103
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 103 which forms the basis for all obviousness rejections set forth in this Office action:
A patent for a claimed invention may not be obtained, notwithstanding that the claimed invention is not identically disclosed as set forth in section 102, if the differences between the claimed invention and the prior art are such that the claimed invention as a whole would have been obvious before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which the claimed invention pertains. Patentability shall not be negated by the manner in which the invention was made.
The factual inquiries for establishing a background for determining obviousness under 35 U.S.C. 103 are summarized as follows:
1. Determining the scope and contents of the prior art.
2. Ascertaining the differences between the prior art and the claims at issue.
3. Resolving the level of ordinary skill in the pertinent art.
4. Considering objective evidence present in the application indicating obviousness or nonobviousness.
This application currently names joint inventors. In considering patentability of the claims the examiner presumes that the subject matter of the various claims was commonly owned as of the effective filing date of the claimed invention(s) absent any evidence to the contrary.  Applicant is advised of the obligation under 37 CFR 1.56 to point out the inventor and effective filing dates of each claim that was not commonly owned as of the effective filing date of the later invention in order for the examiner to consider the applicability of 35 U.S.C. 102(b)(2)(C) for any potential 35 U.S.C. 102(a)(2) prior art against the later invention.
Claim 67 is rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Dowty.
Regarding claim 67, Dowty discloses an aircraft seat as claimed in claim 1, but does not appear to specifically disclose comprising a leg rest decoupling mechanism operable selectively to decouple the leg rest link from the leg rest.
However, it would have been obvious to one having ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to have made the leg rest link disclosed by Dowty operable to selectively decouple from the leg rest, with a reasonable expectation of success, since it has been held that constructing a formerly integral structure in various elements involves only routine skill in the art.  Nerwin v. Erlichman, 168 USPQ 177, 179. The benefit being selectively decoupling the leg rest for maintenance, inspection and general upkeep.
Claims 69-70 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Dowty as applied to claim 1, and further, in view of Higgins.
Regarding claim 69, Dowty discloses an aircraft seat as claimed in claim 68, but does not appear to specifically disclose wherein the leg rest comprises a cam track and a cam follower for controlling movement of the extending portion.
However, Dowty teaches a leg rest in a similar field of endeavor including a cam track (90, Fig. 10) and a cam follower (respective slider 88, Fig. 10) for controlling movement.
It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to have provided the leg rest disclosed by Dowty with the cam track and the cam follower for controlling movement, as taught by Higgins, with a reasonable expectation of success, so that the leg rest comprises a cam track and a cam follower for controlling movement of the extending portion.  The benefit being optional manual extension of the extending portion of the leg rest enhancing user experience.
Regarding claim 70, modified Dowty discloses an aircraft seat as claimed in claim 69, but does not appear to specifically disclose wherein the cam follower is disposed on a pivotally mounted arm.
However, it would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to have disposed the cam follower on the pivotally mounted arm (38, Fig. 1) disclosed by Dowty, with a reasonable expectation of success, so that the cam follower is disposed on the pivotally mounted arm, since it has been held that rearranging parts of an invention involves only routine skill in the art. In re Japikse, 86 USPQ 70. The benefit being an arrangement that allows the extending portion to function best for the seat in the desired aircraft.
Claims 71-72 and 74 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103 as being unpatentable over Dowty and Higgins as applied to claim 69, and Dowty as applied to claim 73; And further, in view of Ward (US 2018/0178915 A1).
Regarding claim 71, modified Dowty discloses an aircraft seat as claimed in claim 69, but does not appear to specifically disclose wherein the cam track comprises at least one recess for retaining the extending portion in an extended position, wherein each recess comprises an aperture for receiving the cam follower to retain the extending portion in the extended position.
However, Ward teaches a seat footrest in a similar field of endeavor including a cam track comprising at least one recess (arcuate member 132 with a first notch 151, Fig. 10) for retaining an extending portion in an extended position (§[0045]),
wherein each recess comprises an aperture for receiving a cam follower (lock pin 150, Fig. 10) to retain the extending portion in the extended position (Fig. 10); (Examiner notes shape of notch 151 discloses an aperture that is capable of receiving a cam follower to retain).
It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to have provided the aircraft seat with the cam track and cam follower disclosed by Dowty with the at least one recess for retaining an extending portion in an extended position, wherein each recess comprises the aperture for receiving a cam follower to retain an extending portion in the extended position, as taught by Ward, with a reasonable expectation of success, so that the cam track comprises the at least one recess for retaining the extending portion in an extended position, wherein each recess comprises the aperture for receiving the cam follower to retain the extending portion in the extended position. The benefit being the ability to lock the extending portion in an orientation.
Regarding claim 72, modified Dowty discloses an aircraft seat as claimed in claim 71, but does not appear to specifically disclose comprising cam follower biasing means for biasing the cam follower into the aperture.
However, Ward teaches a cam follower biasing means for biasing a cam follower into an aperture (spring biased  via gas spring assembly 148, Fig. 10).
It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to have provided the aircraft seat disclosed by modified Dowty with the cam follower biasing means for biasing a cam follower into an aperture as taught by Ward, with a reasonable expectation of success, so that the cam follower comprises biasing means for biasing the cam follower into the aperture.  The benefit being the ability for an occupant to select an orientation.
Regarding claim 74, Dowty discloses an aircraft seat as claimed in claim 73, but does not appear to specifically disclose wherein the extending portion biasing means comprises at least one tension gas spring.
However, Ward teaches a seat footrest in a similar field of endeavor wherein an extending portion biasing means comprises at least one tension gas spring (148, Fig. 10).
It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art before the effective filing date of the claimed invention to have provided the aircraft seat with an extending portion biasing means disclosed by Dowty with at least one tension gas spring, as taught by Ward, with a reasonable expectation of success, so that the extending portion biasing means comprises at least one tension gas spring. The benefit being at least smooth adjustability and compact design.
